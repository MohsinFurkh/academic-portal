“Does our new caching layer speed up the API?”

The team reports the following method, verbatim:

  • “We deployed the caching layer to production on 14 March.”
  • “We compared average response time in the week before against the week after.”
  • “Average response time fell from 340 ms to 250 ms.”
  • “We conclude the caching layer improves API performance by 26%.”

Prompts, if you get stuck:

  1. What else changed between those two weeks, other than the cache?
  2. Is average the right number for a response time?
  3. How would you know whether 340 → 250 is bigger than the ordinary week-to-week wobble?

There are at least six distinct problems. Groups usually find three unaided. Every one of them has a name, and by the end of the hour you will know all six.

Reveal · every flaw has a name

Six problems, and this hour is the repair manual

1 · No control

The two weeks differ in the cache and in traffic mix, deploys, term dates, weather, and whatever else happened in March. Any of them could produce 90 ms.

2 · Confounded by time

“Before vs after” is the weakest design there is. Mid-March may simply be quieter than early March.

3 · Wrong aggregate

The mean of a latency distribution is dominated by its tail and hides it. Users experience p95 and p99, not the average.

4 · No variance

One week vs one week is n = 1 vs n = 1. Nothing tells you whether 90 ms exceeds normal week-to-week noise.

5 · No baseline discipline

The “before” system was not tuned, warmed, or given the same care as the new one. The comparison measures effort, not architecture.

6 · Construct slip

The claim is “speeds up the API”; the measure is server-side response time, which excludes the network and the client — most of what a user feels.

Note what is not on the list: the code. The caching layer may well be excellent. A good system and a bad experiment produce exactly the same evidence as a bad system and a bad experiment — none.

Part A · experiment design

Every experiment has four kinds of variable. You control three.

Independent

What you deliberately change.

The treatment. Cache on / cache off. Scheduler A / B / C. Its possible values are called levels.

Rule: vary one at a time, or use a factorial design and say so. Changing two and reporting one is the classic student error.

Dependent

What you measure.

The outcome: p95 latency, top-1 accuracy, task completion time, defects per KLOC.

Rule: fix the metric and its aggregate before you collect. Choosing after you see the data is metric-shopping.

Controlled

What you hold fixed.

Hardware, OS, dataset, compiler flags, input order, time of day, the person running it.

Rule: list them explicitly in the paper. An unlisted control is an uncontrolled variable as far as a reader can tell.

Confounding

What varied without your permission.

Warm caches, thermal throttling, a noisy neighbour VM, a library upgrade, learners improving with practice.

Rule: you cannot list what you did not think of — so randomise and interleave, which defend against confounders you never named.

The definition worth memorising: a confounder is anything that varies together with your treatment and could also explain your result.

It is the exact mechanism of the internal validity threat from II.1 — and every experimental technique in this hour exists to eliminate confounders, one way or another.

Part A · the thing reviewers attack first

Your result is only as good as what you compared it to

Four ways a baseline is dishonest (usually by accident)

  • The straw man. Comparing against something nobody would use. A new sort beating bubble sort proves nothing.
  • The untuned rival. You spent six weeks on your method and an afternoon on theirs. The comparison measures your attention, not your idea.
  • The stale state of the art. The best published method is from 2024; you compared against 2019 because its code was easier to run.
  • The missing trivial baseline. Nobody checked whether “always predict the majority class”, “return the most recent item”, or “do nothing” already scores as well as you do.

The last one is the most humbling and the most frequently omitted. Run it first, before you build anything.

What an honest comparison requires

  • Use the authors' own released code where it exists. Re-implementing a rival is how you accidentally weaken it.
  • Equal tuning budget. State the hyperparameter search for both sides — same number of trials, same search space, same person.
  • Same everything else. Same data, same split, same hardware, same session, same metric, same aggregate.
  • Include the trivial baseline and report it even when it is embarrassing.
  • Report the strongest rival, not the most convenient one — and say why it is the strongest.

The test: would the author of the baseline method agree that you gave it a fair run?

Part A · the number you report

One run is an anecdote. Report a distribution.

The mechanics, and they are not optional

  • Repeat. 3–5 seeds minimum for anything stochastic; 10+ runs for timing. Report mean ± SD, never a bare number.
  • Discard warm-up. Say how many runs you dropped and why. JIT, caches and page tables all need warming.
  • Interleave, do not batch. Run A,B,A,B,A,B — not all of A then all of B. Batching confounds your treatment with drift, heat and time.
  • Randomise order of conditions and inputs.
  • Report the right aggregate. Latency lives in the tail: give p50, p95, p99. The mean of a heavy-tailed distribution is close to meaningless.
  • Effect size, not just significance. “p < 0.05” says the difference is probably real; it says nothing about whether it matters.

What a stranger needs to reproduce you

Write this list into your methodology section. Every item, every time.

Code at a specific commit hash · data with a version or retrieval date

Seeds · hyperparameters · the search that chose them

Hardware, OS, driver, library versions · compiler flags

Raw per-run results, not only the summary table

The reason for the last one: a distribution cannot be recovered from a mean. Throw away the raw runs and nobody — including you, in six months — can ever recheck the claim.

Reproducibility = same data, same code, same result. Replicability = new data or new implementation, same conclusion. The second is much harder and much more valuable; it is what II.5's replication studies are for.

Part C · designs used in computing research

Five designs, in decreasing order of control

Design You control Answers CS example Main weakness
Controlled experiment Assignment to conditions, and everything else Causal: does X cause Y? 120 students, assistant on/off, 3 counterbalanced debugging tasks Artificial setting; often small; hard to recruit
Quasi-experiment Conditions, but not who goes where Causal-ish, with named alternatives Teams that already adopted a tool vs teams that did not Selection bias — the groups differed beforehand
Case study Nothing; you observe in depth, in context How and why, in a real setting One company's migration to microservices over 18 months n = 1; generalises to theory, not to a population
Action research You intervene and study your own intervention Does this change help here, and what did we learn? Introducing code review in a team while studying the effect Researcher is not neutral — you want it to work
Replication Whatever the original study controlled Does the earlier finding hold up? Re-running a published SE experiment on a new population Undervalued; needs the original to be well reported

The rule that chooses for you: only random assignment buys you the word “causes”. Everything below the first row supports “is associated with”, and must say so.

The one to take seriously as a student: replication. The problem is already defined, the success criteria are published, and either outcome is a genuine result — provided you report honestly, including when it does reproduce.

Part C · user studies and surveys

People are instruments that change when you measure them

Design choices you must make and state

  • Between-subjects — each person sees one condition. Needs more people; no carry-over effects.
  • Within-subjects — each person sees all conditions. Far more statistical power from fewer people, but now you must counterbalance the order, because people learn and people tire.
  • Latin square when there are more than two conditions and every order cannot be covered.
  • Pilot with 3–5 people and time it. Every user study that ran long, confused participants or produced unusable data skipped this step.

Four effects that will contaminate your data

  • Hawthorne / observer effect — people behave differently because you are watching.
  • Demand characteristics — participants work out what you are hoping for and helpfully deliver it. Worst when you built the tool and you run the session.
  • Learning and fatigue — the second task is easier because it is second, and the fourth is worse because they are tired. Counterbalancing is the only defence.
  • Non-response bias — the people who did not reply to your survey differ systematically from those who did. Always report the response rate.

Ethics is not optional here: informed consent, the right to withdraw, anonymisation, and no identifier you do not need. Unit III makes this a formal requirement of your proposal.

Twenty CS students in a lab are not “users” — they are twenty CS students in a lab. Say so, and your study survives.

Design the experiment that earns your claim

Step 1 · Seven lines. No prose.

  1. Claim — the comparative sentence you want to earn.
  2. Independent variable and its levels.
  3. Dependent variable, with metric and aggregate (p95? mean? top-1?).
  4. Baseline — named, and why it is the strongest available, not the easiest.
  5. Controlled — at least five things you hold fixed.
  6. Repetition — how many runs/seeds/participants, and what you will report (mean ± SD).
  7. Failure threshold — the number that would make you say “my method did not work”. Write it now, before any data exists.

Line 7 is the one that matters. If you cannot state today what result would count as failure, you are not running an experiment — you are collecting decoration for a conclusion you already wrote.

Step 2 · You are a red team. Be unpleasant.

Your job is to find one confounder — something that varies with their treatment and could also explain their result. Use this checklist:

  • Different hardware, machine state or time between conditions?
  • Was the baseline tuned as hard as the method?
  • Order effects — did anything run or get seen first, always?
  • Does the metric actually capture the claim's noun?
  • Would the trivial baseline (do nothing / majority class) score the same?
  • Is n big enough for the difference they expect to find?

What will happen. The commonest confounder found in this room will be the untuned baseline — and the commonest missing line will be number 7.

Consolidation

Eight things that must survive this hour

  1. Four variables: independent · dependent · controlled · confounding. You choose the first three; the fourth chooses you.
  2. A confounder is anything varying with your treatment that could also explain the result.
  3. Randomise and interleave — they defend against confounders you never thought of.
  4. Your result is only as good as your baseline. Run the trivial one first.
  1. One run is an anecdote. Repeat, report mean ± SD, keep the raw data.
  2. Latency lives in the tail — p95 and p99, not the mean.
  3. Only random assignment licenses the word “causes.” Everything else is “associated with”.
  4. With people: counterbalance, pilot, report response rate, and never call 20 CS students “users”.

If you remember one sentence: write down the number that would count as failure before you collect any data — everything else in experiment design is a way of making sure that number means what you think it means.
