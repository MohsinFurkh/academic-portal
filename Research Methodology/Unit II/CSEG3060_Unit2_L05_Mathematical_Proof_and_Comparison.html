Reveal · read the quantifier

Look at the quantifier. It tells you the method.

# Quantifier Settled by Why
1 For all inputs Proof only Infinitely many inputs; testing samples finitely many, forever
2 On this workload Experiment A bounded empirical comparison — exactly II.4
3 Never (= for all executions) Proof or model checking Concurrency bugs hide in interleavings you will not happen to hit
4 Every possible file It is false Counting argument: no lossless scheme shrinks all inputs. One counter-example ends it
5 Users, on average Experiment A claim about people — no theorem available
6 There exists a graph One example Existence claim — construct the graph and you are done. This is the trap: it looks theoretical but needs no general proof

The rule that runs the whole hour: “for all” needs a proof · “there exists” needs one witness · “on average, here” needs an experiment.

Read the quantifier in your own claim before choosing a method. Most wasted student effort comes from running experiments at a “for all” claim, which can never succeed no matter how many runs you do.

Part A · the toolkit

Four techniques, four jobs

Proof by induction

For claims about all n, or all structures built in stages.

Base case: true for the smallest case. Inductive step: if true for k, then true for k+1. Conclude: true for all n.

CS use: correctness of recursive algorithms, properties of trees and lists, complexity recurrences, structural induction over grammars and ASTs.

Remember from II.2: this is deductive, despite the name.

Proof by contradiction

For impossibility, uniqueness and lower bounds.

Assume the opposite of what you want. Derive something absurd. Conclude the assumption was false.

CS use: the halting problem, the n log n sorting lower bound, “no lossless compressor shrinks every input”, the impossibility of asynchronous consensus with one crash fault.

This is the technique behind almost every impossibility result in computing — and impossibility results are how a field learns to stop wasting effort.

Proof by construction

For existence claims — “there is an X with property P”.

Build the X explicitly. Show it has P. Done.

CS use: reductions (construct an instance of problem B from one of A), showing an algorithm achieves a bound, exhibiting a schedule or a coding scheme.

Note the family resemblance to proof by demonstration in II.3 — both discharge an existence claim with one witness. The difference is that construction proves the witness has the property; a demo shows it running.

Proof by counter-example

For refuting a universal claim.

To destroy “all A are B”, produce one A that is not B. That is the entire proof.

CS use: showing a heuristic is not optimal, that an optimisation is unsound, that a claimed invariant fails on an input the authors did not consider.

The cheapest publishable result in computing — and a genuinely realistic student contribution.

Part B · proof as a research argument

The loop invariant: induction, applied to code

Three obligations, and they mirror induction exactly

  1. Initialisation. The invariant holds before the first iteration. = base case
  2. Maintenance. If it holds at the start of an iteration, it holds at the start of the next. = inductive step
  3. Termination. The loop ends, and when it does, the invariant plus the exit condition give you what you wanted to prove.

Worked, in one line. Insertion sort: “at the start of each iteration of the outer loop, the subarray A[1..i−1] contains the original elements of A[1..i−1] in sorted order.” Initialisation: i = 1, one element, trivially sorted. Maintenance: the inner loop shifts larger elements right and inserts A[i] in place. Termination: i = n+1, so A[1..n] is sorted — which is the postcondition.

Termination is the obligation students forget. An invariant that holds forever in a loop that never exits has proved nothing useful. You typically show a non-negative integer quantity that strictly decreases each iteration.

Why this counts as research evidence

In a paper, a correctness argument does something no experiment can: it covers the inputs nobody ran.

  • It licenses “for all inputs” in the abstract.
  • It makes the assumptions explicit — and reviewers attack assumptions, which is a much more productive argument than attacking numbers.
  • It does not age. Your 2026 benchmark is obsolete by 2028; your theorem is not.
  • It is cheap to check — a reviewer needs a pen, not your cluster.

The standard failure, worth stating plainly: you proved the algorithm correct and shipped an implementation. Integer overflow, floating-point rounding, a memory model, a compiler optimisation and a hardware erratum all sit between the two. A proof about a model that your system does not satisfy is valid and useless — the Unit I failure, in its most expensive form.

Part B · algorithmic approaches · complexity as evidence

A complexity bound is a theorem, not a measurement

You met this analysis in Design and Analysis of Algorithms as a technique. Here it is a form of research evidence — and the four kinds below are four different claims, with four different quantifiers.

Worst case

“For every input of size n, cost ≤ f(n).”

Universally quantified, so nothing can violate it. The only bound you may use for a guarantee — real-time deadlines, or any adversarial setting.

Proved by: induction, or a direct argument over the algorithm's structure.

Average case

“Over a stated input distribution, expected cost is f(n).”

Weaker, and it carries a hidden model — the distribution is an assumption, exactly the kind II.6 will teach you to audit. Quicksort is O(n log n) average and O(n²) worst.

Breaks when: inputs are chosen by someone who read your paper.

Amortised

“Over any sequence of m operations, total cost ≤ m·f(n).”

Not probabilistic — this is a worst-case guarantee about sequences, which is why it survives an adversary. Dynamic-array append is O(1) amortised; union-find with path compression is O(m·α(n)).

The one students omit, and the one that explains most real data structures.

Lower bound

“No algorithm can do better than Ω(g(n)).”

Quantifies over every algorithm that could ever be written — the strongest claim in this deck, and the one that tells you when to stop optimising.

Proved by: contradiction — the decision-tree argument for the Ω(n log n) comparison-sort bound.

Every technique from slide 4 reappears here: recurrences are solved by induction · lower bounds are proved by contradiction · reductions are proof by construction · and “this heuristic is not optimal” is a counter-example.

A reduction is worth naming separately: “if you could solve B efficiently, you could solve A efficiently” constructs a solver for A out of a solver for B. That is how NP-completeness is established, and it is a construction proof about algorithms rather than about objects.

Part B · the balance sheet for algorithmic evidence

Asymptotic analysis and measurement answer different questions

What a bound gives you

  • All inputs, including ones nobody will run. No benchmark can do this.
  • Machine independence — still true on hardware not yet built.
  • It does not age. Your 2026 benchmark is stale by 2028; the bound is not.
  • Cheap to check — a reviewer needs a pen, not your cluster.
  • Lower bounds tell you when to stop. No other kind of evidence does.

What it hides

  • Constant factors — which decide everything at the n you actually have.
  • Cache behaviour, memory hierarchy, branch prediction — invisible to the model.
  • The value of n in your application. O(n log n) mergesort loses to O(n²) insertion sort for small n, which is why every production sort switches below a threshold. The asymptotics were never wrong — they answered a question about large n.

Where the average-case assumption bites. Hash-table lookup is O(1) on average — until an attacker sends keys that all collide, and your service degrades to O(n) per lookup. Algorithmic-complexity denial of service, including regex catastrophic backtracking, is precisely an adversary refusing your distribution assumption.

The move that uses both, and it is a genuinely strong research pattern: derive the bound, then measure across a range of n and check the curve has the shape the theory predicts.

If theory says O(n log n) and your measurements bend quadratically, you have found a bug, an unmet assumption, or a hidden cost — and every one of those is a finding worth reporting. II.7 supplies the measurement half; this hour supplies the proof half.

Part C · the comparison · the core of Unit II

Demonstration · Empirical · Mathematical

Demonstration Empirical Mathematical
Answers Can it be done? Is it better, here? Must it always hold?
Evidence A working artefact Measurements over repeated trials A derivation from stated assumptions
Quantifier it settles There exists On average, over this population For all
Reasoning (II.2) Existence — one witness Inductive Deductive
Certainty Certain, but only about possibility Probabilistic; always revisable Certain, given the assumptions
Advantages Settles feasibility outright; surfaces unknown unknowns; credible to practitioners; leaves a reusable artefact Applies to messy real systems and to people; measures what actually happens; the only option when no theory exists Universal reach; does not age; cheap to check; covers inputs nobody ran
Limitations No comparative force; n = 1; confounded by your engineering effort Never universal; costly; confounders; results age as hardware and data change Only as good as its assumptions; the model is not the system; unavailable for most human and messy questions
Fails when You say “better” having compared with nothing Weak baseline, one seed, over-general conclusion The assumptions do not describe any real system

They are not ranked. Each is the correct and complete method for its own kind of question — and the wrong one for the other two.

Part C · how real papers do it

The strongest papers stack the methods

Prove the bound

“Our scheduling algorithm is within a factor 2 of optimal, for all instances.”

Mathematical. Covers every input — including the adversarial ones a benchmark would never contain.

Build the thing

“We implement it in 2,400 lines against the Kubernetes scheduler interface.”

Demonstration. Shows the bound is not merely theoretical — the algorithm can actually be realised in a real system.

Measure it

“On 12 production traces, 5 seeds, it cuts p95 wait time 18–24% versus the default.”

Empirical. Shows the worst-case bound also behaves well on the cases people actually have.

Each method covers the others' blind spot. The proof covers inputs the benchmark lacks; the benchmark covers the realism the proof assumed away; the implementation proves neither was fantasy.

What this means for your project. You are not required to do all three — a one-semester project rarely can. You are required to say which one you did, which you did not, and what therefore remains unestablished. A report that names its own gap is stronger than one that pretends there is none.

Which method does your claim actually need?

Step 1 · Five lines

  1. Write your claim, exactly as it currently stands.
  2. Circle the quantifier. “all” · “never” · “there exists” · “on average” · none stated.
    If none is stated, that is itself the finding — an unquantified claim is why reviewers and authors argue past each other.
  3. From the quantifier, name the method the claim requires.
  4. Name the method you are actually able to supply this semester.
  5. If lines 3 and 4 differ — and for most of you they will — choose one:

    Narrow the claim to match the method you have. Cheap, honest, almost always correct.

    Supply the method the claim needs. Sometimes a short proof is easier than you fear.

    Keep both and hope. This is what happens by default, and it is what the examiner finds.

Step 2 · The checker's three questions

  1. “Read me your quantifier.” If they cannot find one, make them add one.
  2. “Does your method settle that quantifier?” Experiments never settle “all”. Demos never settle “better”.
  3. “If you cannot supply the method — what does your claim shrink to?” Make them say the smaller sentence out loud.

What will happen. A good number of you will find you have written a “for all” claim — “our method is robust”, “the system always recovers”, “this approach ensures security” — with only empirical or demonstration evidence behind it. Those three verbs each promise a proof.

The good news: narrowing costs one sentence and makes the claim true.

Consolidation

Ten things that must survive this hour

  1. Read the quantifier. “For all” → proof. “There exists” → one witness. “On average, here” → experiment.
  2. Induction for all-n claims · contradiction for impossibility and lower bounds · construction for existence · counter-example to refute.
  3. A loop invariant is induction applied to code: initialisation · maintenance · termination.
  4. Termination is the obligation students forget.
  5. A complexity bound is a theorem, not a measurement — it is deductive evidence.
  1. Worst-case · average · amortised · lower bound are four different claims. Worst case for guarantees; amortised is about sequences; average case hides a distribution assumption an adversary can refuse.
  2. Proof buys universal reach and does not age — and costs you the truth of its assumptions.
  3. The model is not the system. A valid proof about an abstraction nothing satisfies is worthless.
  4. The three methods are not ranked — each is correct for its own question and wrong for the others.
  5. A counter-example is the cheapest publishable result in computing.

If you remember one sentence: the words “always”, “never”, “ensures” and “guarantees” are promises of a proof — if you have not written one, change the word.
