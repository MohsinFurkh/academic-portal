Reveal · the thing that made your ranking hard

You were not ranking difficulty. You were ranking reach per unit of evidence.

A · a report, not a claim

“We built X and it works.”

Nothing is asserted about the world — only about one artefact, on one class, once. Compared with what? Would 94% beat marking a register by hand? The sentence cannot be false, because it does not claim anything beyond itself. This is the shape of most final-year project reports.

B · a bounded empirical claim

Named comparison, named workload, named metric, a number.

It can be false: run TPC-C again and get 2%. It reaches exactly as far as TPC-C and no further — and it says so. Modest, checkable, and by far the most common shape of a real CS paper.

C · a universal claim

Quantifies over every algorithm and every program.

Enormous reach — and no experiment could ever establish it, because no experiment ranges over all programs. Only a proof does. Its weak point is not data: it is the definitions it starts from.

Three claims, three completely different kinds of evidence: an artefact, a measurement, a proof. Unit II is the study of which kind of evidence earns which kind of claim.

A is repaired not by better engineering but by adding a comparison and a population — which is exactly the Unit I move: baseline, dataset, metric, constraint.

Part A · the dialectic

Knowledge in computing is what survives disagreement

Dialectic — the old word, the exact meaning

A dialectic is the advance of knowledge through claim and counter-claim. Someone asserts; someone else objects; the claim is narrowed, repaired or abandoned; what remains is stronger than what was first said.

Computing runs this process formally. Peer review is institutionalised objection. A rebuttal is literally a stage of the process at most CS conferences. A Threats to Validity section is you objecting to yourself, in writing, before anyone else gets to.

A paper is not a report of work done. It is a move in an argument — and it is addressed to someone who intends to disagree.

The habit this course has to break

You have five semesters of training in doing: build it, make it run, demo it, submit it. The reviewer's question is not “did you do it?”

Doing — “I trained a model and got 94%.”
A fact about your afternoon.

Claiming — “Fine-tuning on code-mixed data recovers 6 points of the accuracy lost when an English-trained classifier is applied to Hinglish.”
A statement about the world, which could be wrong.

The claim is the sentence a reviewer can disagree with. If no such sentence exists in your report, you have not made a contribution — you have described an afternoon.

Part A · the questions computing science asks

Five kinds of research question in computing

Mary Shaw's survey of what actually gets published in software engineering and systems. Your own project is one of these — and the kind you pick decides what evidence you owe.

Kind of question Phrased as Worked CS example What you then owe the reviewer
Method or means of development How can we build X? How do we automate Y? A technique for auto-repairing failing unit tests A working artefact and evidence it beats doing it the old way
Method for analysis How do I evaluate the quality of X? How do I detect Y? A metric for measuring API-documentation completeness Evidence the measure agrees with reality — validity, not just numbers
Design or evaluation Is design P better than design Q, and under what conditions? Two cache-eviction policies on the same workload A fair comparison: same input, honest baseline, reported variance
Generalisation or characterisation What, exactly, do we mean by X? What are the varieties of Y? A taxonomy of the ways ML pipelines leak test data Coverage and distinctness — does the taxonomy fit cases it has not seen?
Feasibility or existence Is X possible at all? Does something with property P exist? Can a transformer run inference on a $5 microcontroller? One convincing instance — existence needs a single example, not a study

Notice the last row. A feasibility claim is discharged by one working system; a comparison claim never is. Students routinely build the first and write up the second.

Part A · what you can contribute

Six things a computing paper can contribute

1 · Procedure or technique

A new way to do something: an algorithm, a method, a pipeline, a heuristic.

Ex: a scheduling heuristic for GPU sharing. Owes: a comparison against the obvious alternative.

2 · Qualitative model

A taxonomy, framework or structure that organises a messy domain so others can navigate it.

Ex: a taxonomy of adversarial attacks. Owes: coverage, and cases it correctly classifies.

3 · Empirical / predictive model

A model fitted to observed data that predicts something not yet observed.

Ex: a defect-prediction model from commit history. Owes: held-out evaluation, and a baseline.

4 · Analytic model

A formal model from which results can be derived: bounds, theorems, impossibility results.

Ex: a queueing model of a request pipeline. Owes: stated assumptions, and a valid derivation.

5 · Notation or tool

A language, format or artefact that lets other people do work they could not do before.

Ex: a fuzzing harness, a benchmark suite. Owes: evidence somebody else can use it.

6 · Empirical evidence / report

A measurement of what is the case — a study, a replication, a negative result, an experience report.

Ex: how often CI is left red across 50k repos. Owes: sampling, and honesty about what it cannot explain.

Adapted from M. Shaw, “What makes good research in software engineering?” (2002) and “Writing good software engineering research papers” (ICSE 2003). Most strong papers make one of these contributions and are honest that the rest are out of scope. A paper that claims four is usually making none.

Part A · the validation ladder

How you back it up — from strongest to worthless

Analysis — a formal derivation or proof. Strongest available, and only available for some claims.

Controlled experiment — you varied one thing, held the rest fixed, and repeated it.

Evaluation on real data — benchmarks, corpora, deployments you did not construct to flatter yourself.

Experience — “we used it for two years in production and here is what happened.” Weaker, but honest and often valuable.

Example — “here is one case where it works.” Sufficient only for a feasibility claim.

Persuasion — “it is clearly better because it is more modern / uses deep learning.” Not evidence.

Blatant assertion — no evidence offered at all. Shaw's own term, and it appears more often in student reports than anywhere else.

The rule that generates the rest of Unit II

The strength of your evidence must match the reach of your claim.

Not stronger — that is wasted effort. Not weaker — that is the single most common reason a paper is rejected and a viva goes badly.

“X is possible” one example is enough
“X beats Y on workload W” needs an experiment on W
“X beats Y generally” needs many workloads — and is usually overclaiming
“X is always true” needs a proof; no amount of data will do

Lectures II.3, II.4 and II.5 take the middle three rows one at a time: demonstration, empirical, mathematical.

Part B · the Toulmin model

Every argument has three parts. You will have written two.

Stephen Toulmin, The Uses of Argument (1958). The most useful thing in this course for reading a paper critically — and for surviving your own review panel.

Grounds (data)

“Our policy showed 18% lower p95 latency than LRU on TPC-C, over 5 runs.”

The evidence you actually collected. Students are good at this.

→

Claim

“Our eviction policy is better than LRU for OLTP workloads.”

The sentence you want the reader to accept. Students are good at this too.

Warrant — the step underneath

“TPC-C p95 latency is a valid proxy for real OLTP performance, and a 5-run difference of this size is not noise.”

The principle that licenses the arrow. It is almost never written down — and it is where every serious objection lands. A reviewer who says “TPC-C is not representative of modern workloads” has not disputed your data or your claim. They have refused your warrant, and the whole argument falls.

Grounds and claim are yours. The warrant is borrowed from your field — and fields change their minds.

Part B · the other three

The three parts that make a claim defensible

Backing

Why should we accept your warrant?

Citations, standards, prior validation. “TPC-C is the standard OLTP benchmark; the TPC council specifies it and 200+ papers use it.” Backing is where your literature review earns its keep — it is not decoration, it is load-bearing.

Qualifier

How strongly are you claiming it?

“For OLTP workloads with working sets under 8 GB, our policy is better.” The qualifier is the honest shrinking of a claim to the size of the evidence. Students think it weakens the paper; reviewers read it as competence.

Rebuttal

When does your claim not hold?

“Unless the workload is scan-heavy, where LRU's sequential behaviour wins.” Naming your own rebuttal costs you a paragraph. Letting the examiner find it costs you the viva. This is what a Threats to Validity section is.

Read as one sentence:

Since our policy showed 18% lower p95 latency than LRU on TPC-C over 5 runs, and because TPC-C p95 is an accepted proxy for OLTP performance (backed by the TPC specification and prior work), for working sets under 8 GB our policy outperforms LRU — unless the workload is scan-heavy.

That is a complete argument. It is also, almost word for word, the abstract of a publishable systems paper.

Part B · the diagnosis

Four unstated warrants you have already relied on

Your grounds Your claim The warrant you never wrote down How a reviewer refuses it
“99.2% accuracy on the test split” “The model works” The test split resembles deployment “Your split is random, but your users are a different hospital.”
“Users rated it 4.6 / 5” “The interface is usable” Satisfaction ratings measure usability “They rated it kindly because you were in the room and you built it.”
“Runs in 40 ms on my laptop” “It is fast enough for production” Your laptop predicts the target device, warm “Cold start, contended CPU, and a phone from 2019?”
“Beats the baseline by 3 points” “Our method is better” 3 points exceeds run-to-run noise “One seed. What is the standard deviation?”

The diagnostic question, and it works on any paper: what would have to be true for this evidence to support this claim?

Whatever you just said is the warrant. Now ask whether the authors gave you any reason to believe it.

Why this matters for you specifically. In every one of the four rows the work was fine. The measurement was real, the code ran, the numbers are honest. The argument still fails — because the step from evidence to claim was never defended.

This is why “but it works, sir” is not an answer at a review panel.

Part C · threats to validity

Four standard ways an argument fails

Construct

“Did you measure the thing you named?”

The gap between the concept and the number that stands in for it.

Ex: measuring “code quality” by lines of code; measuring “usability” by how much users say they liked it.

Internal

“Was it your change that caused the difference?”

Some other thing varied at the same time and explains the result.

Ex: the new version was benchmarked on a warm cache; the two groups were not equivalent to begin with.

External

“Does it hold anywhere else?”

The claim reaches further than the setting it was established in.

Ex: one dataset, one language, one company, 20 CS students standing in for “users”.

Conclusion

“Do the numbers support what you say they do?”

The statistical step from data to statement is unsound.

Ex: one seed and no variance; declaring a 0.3-pt difference a win; testing twenty things and reporting the one that worked.

Cook & Campbell's classification, adapted for computing in Wohlin et al., Experimentation in Software Engineering. Every serious CS venue expects a section naming these; writing one is not an admission of weakness, it is the price of being taken seriously.

Part C · the synthesis · the point of the whole hour

A threat is not vague criticism. It attacks one box.

Threat Attacks The objection, in the reviewer's own words Your repair
Construct the grounds “Your number does not measure the thing your claim is about.” Change the measure, or narrow the claim to what the measure covers
Internal the warrant “Something else could have produced that difference.” Control it, randomise it, or name it as a limitation
External the qualifier “This holds in your setting. You claimed it everywhere.” Add the qualifier — this is free, and almost always the right move
Conclusion the grounds' reliability “Run it again and I do not believe you get the same number.” More seeds, report variance, state an effect size

This is why the Toulmin model is worth an hour: it turns “your paper is not convincing” into a specific box you can go and fix.

And note which repair is cheapest. Three of the four cost you experiments. External validity is repaired by writing one clause. Almost every over-claiming student project could be rescued by a qualifier its author was too proud to add.

Claim

One sentence. Must contain a comparison or a magnitude, or it is not a claim.

Grounds

What you will measure, on what, how many times.

Warrant  ← the one that matters

“These grounds support this claim because…” Finish the sentence. If you cannot, you have found your problem.

Backing

Which paper, benchmark or standard says your warrant is acceptable? Name one.

Qualifier

“For…”, “under…”, “in the case of…”

Rebuttal

“Unless…” — one condition under which you expect to be wrong.

Rules for the attacker (step 2)

  • You may not attack the claim for being uninteresting. That was Unit I's job and it is settled.
  • You may not attack the grounds for being small. Everyone's grounds are small.
  • You attack one thing only: finish the sentence “these grounds would only support that claim if ______, and I do not believe ______.”
  • Then name which of the four threats you just used.

What will happen. Most of you will be unable to write the warrant in step 1 — not because you are unprepared, but because you have never been asked to. That blank box is the single most valuable thing you will take out of this hour.

Consolidation

Eight things that must survive this hour

  1. Research is a dialectic: a paper is a move in an argument addressed to someone who intends to disagree.
  2. A claim is the sentence a reviewer can disagree with. “I built it and it works” is not one.
  3. Computing asks five kinds of question, and feasibility needs one example while comparison never does.
  4. The strength of your evidence must match the reach of your claim — not exceed it, and never fall short of it.
  1. Toulmin: claim · grounds · warrant · backing · qualifier · rebuttal.
  2. You will have written the grounds and the claim. The warrant is where the argument breaks, and it is the one you left implicit.
  3. The four threats each attack a named box: construct → grounds · internal → warrant · external → qualifier · conclusion → reliability.
  4. Three of those repairs cost experiments. External validity costs one clause — add it.

If you remember one sentence: state the warrant you are relying on, and qualify the claim down to the size of your evidence — everything else in Unit II is a method for making the grounds stronger.
