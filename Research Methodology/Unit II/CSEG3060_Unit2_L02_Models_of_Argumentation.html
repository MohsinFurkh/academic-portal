Models of Argumentation
Deduction · Induction · Abduction

Last hour you drew an arrow from grounds to claim. That arrow is one of exactly three kinds — and each one entitles you to a different strength of conclusion.

Deduction

General → specific. If the premises hold, the conclusion must hold. Certainty, but nothing new about the world.

Induction

Specific → general. Evidence supports but never guarantees. New knowledge, permanently provisional.

Abduction

Observation → best explanation. The weakest warrant, and the only one that generates a hypothesis worth testing.

You leave with

Your own arrow classified, the claim strength it permits, and the specific fallacy your project is most exposed to.

A  Premise: Every comparison-based sort needs at least n log n comparisons in the worst case. Premise: Mergesort is comparison-based.  →  Conclusion: Mergesort needs at least n log n comparisons in the worst case.

B  Premise: We ran our parser on 10,000 real-world files and it never crashed.  →  Conclusion: Our parser does not crash on real-world files.

C  Premise: The service returns stale data, but only for users in Europe, and only after 6 p.m. IST.  →  Conclusion: The cache key is missing the locale field.

Three questions, for each of A, B and C:

  1. If the premises are all true, could the conclusion still be false?
  2. Does the conclusion tell you something the premises did not already contain?
  3. What single new observation would destroy it?

You already reason in all three ways every day. The point of the hour is to notice which one you are doing, because each carries a different bill.

Reveal · the three arrows

Same shape on the page. Completely different guarantees.

A · Deduction

Could the conclusion be false with true premises? No. Never.

Anything new? No — the conclusion was already inside the premises. Deduction rearranges what you had; it does not add.

What kills it? Nothing you could observe. Only a false premise or an invalid step.

B · Induction

Could the conclusion be false with true premises? Yes.

Anything new? Yes — it reaches from 10,000 files to files in general, which is far more than you looked at.

What kills it? File number 10,001. One counter-example, and the general claim is gone.

C · Abduction

Could the conclusion be false with true premises? Easily.

Anything new? Yes — a whole cause that appears nowhere in the evidence. You invented it.

What kills it? A better explanation. Perhaps a replica in Frankfurt is lagging, and the cache is fine.

The trade you cannot escape: certainty and informativeness move in opposite directions.

Deduction is certain and tells you nothing new. Abduction is highly informative and could easily be wrong. Induction sits in between — which is why nearly all of experimental computer science lives there.

Model 1 · deduction

Deduction: from the general rule to the specific case

The form

All A are B  ·  x is A   ∴  x is B

Truth-preserving. If the premises are true and the form is valid, the conclusion cannot be false. That is the whole point, and no other form of reasoning offers it.

Non-ampliative. Nothing is added. The conclusion was already contained in the premises; deduction only makes it visible. This is why a proof can feel simultaneously airtight and unsurprising.

Validity vs soundness — a distinction you will be asked for. An argument is valid if the form guarantees the conclusion; it is sound if it is valid and the premises are actually true. “All birds can fly; a penguin is a bird; so a penguin can fly” is perfectly valid and completely unsound.

Where deduction actually lives in computing

  • Algorithm correctness — loop invariants, pre/post-conditions, termination arguments.
  • Complexity results — worst-case bounds, lower bounds, reductions, NP-completeness.
  • Type systems — if it type-checks, an entire class of error cannot occur. A deductive guarantee, purchased at compile time.
  • Formal verification and model checking — the property holds on all reachable states, not on the ones you happened to test.
  • Cryptographic reduction — “if you can break this scheme, you can factor large integers.”
  • Impossibility results — no protocol, ever, can do this.

Every item on this list is a claim no experiment could establish, because each quantifies over infinitely many cases.

Model 1 · the bill

Certainty is bought with assumptions — and assumptions are where it breaks

Advantages

  • Conclusive. No sample size argument, no seeds, no p-values, no “more experiments needed”.
  • Universal reach. Covers inputs nobody has run and machines nobody has built.
  • Permanent. A theorem from 1936 is as true today as when it was written. Benchmarks from 2019 are already stale.
  • Cheap to check. A reviewer needs a pen, not a cluster.

Limitations

  • Tells you nothing new about the world. It cannot discover that users abandon your app after 3 seconds.
  • Only as good as its premises. A flawless proof about an assumption no real system satisfies is worth nothing — the failure named in Unit I.
  • The model is not the system. You proved the protocol correct; you deployed an implementation. Heartbleed sat in a protocol with proofs.
  • Not always available. Most interesting questions in computing are not provable at all — “is this interface usable?” has no theorem.

The trap with your name on it: mathematical induction is deductive.

Despite the name, proof by induction is a deductive technique — it establishes its conclusion with certainty for all n, via the axiom of induction. It has nothing to do with inductive reasoning, which is uncertain and generalises from samples. Confusing the two is a guaranteed mark lost in Class Test 1, and we return to the proof technique itself in II.5.

Model 2 · induction

Induction: from the observed cases to the general rule

The form

a₁, a₂, … an are all A, and all were observed to be B   ∴  A's in general are B (or: the next A will be B)

Ampliative. The conclusion goes beyond the evidence — that is precisely what makes it useful and what makes it risky. You looked at 10,000 files and made a claim about all of them.

Never certain, only stronger or weaker. Inductive arguments are not valid or invalid; they are strong or weak, and strength comes from sample size, sample diversity, and the absence of a mechanism that would bias what you saw.

The problem of induction (Hume): no finite number of observations logically guarantees the next one. There is no fix. Science proceeds anyway, because a well-designed inductive argument is the best evidence available for claims about the world.

Where induction lives in computing — almost everywhere

  • Benchmarking — “faster on these 12 workloads, therefore faster”.
  • Testing — passing tests supports, never proves, correctness. Testing shows the presence of bugs, not their absence.
  • Every empirical evaluation — held-out test sets, A/B tests, user studies.
  • Machine learning itself — a model generalising from training data to unseen data is induction, mechanised. This is why distribution shift breaks models: the inductive step was never guaranteed.
  • Repository mining — “in 50,000 projects, therefore in projects”.

Recognise the pattern: every one of your empirical claims is an inductive argument, and every threat to external validity is an attack on the inductive step.

Model 2 · the operational part

You cannot make induction certain. You can make it hard to attack.

What actually strengthens the inference

Lever Weak version Strong version
Sample size 1 benchmark, 1 seed 12 workloads, 5 seeds, variance reported
Diversity 12 workloads, all web servers web, batch, ML, embedded — chosen to disagree
Representativeness the repos that were easy to clone a stated sampling frame and a random draw
Adversarial selection cases you expected to win cases you expected to lose, reported anyway
Scope of the claim “our method is faster” “faster on read-heavy OLTP under 8 GB”

The move that costs nothing

The last row again. You cannot buy certainty, but you can shrink the claim until your evidence covers it.

This is the qualifier from II.1, and it is the single most effective repair in empirical computing research. It converts an argument a reviewer can destroy into one they must accept.

Survivorship bias — the one that catches students. You evaluate your crash-recovery tool on bug reports from the issue tracker. But bugs severe enough to be filed are not the population of bugs; they are the population of bugs someone noticed and cared about. Your induction generalises to the wrong set, and no increase in sample size will fix it.

Model 3 · abduction

Abduction: from a surprising observation to the cause that would explain it

The form (C. S. Peirce)

The surprising fact C is observed.  ·  If A were true, C would be a matter of course.   ∴  there is reason to suspect A is true.

Note how weak that conclusion is: reason to suspect. Abduction never concludes that A is true. It nominates A as worth investigating.

It runs backwards. Deduction and induction move from evidence forward to a conclusion. Abduction reasons from an effect back to a cause — which is why it can never be certain: many causes produce the same effect.

Also called inference to the best explanation. The load is carried entirely by the word best — best among which explanations? Only the ones you thought of.

You have been doing this since first year

Debugging is abduction. The test fails (surprising). If the cache key omitted the locale, that failure would be expected. So you suspect the cache key — and then you go and check, which is the deductive-then-inductive part.

  • Fault localisation and root-cause analysis — the entire discipline.
  • Incident post-mortems — reconstructing a cause from logs after the fact.
  • Performance diagnosis — “p99 spiked at 3 a.m.; what would produce exactly that shape?”
  • Reading an anomaly in your own results — why did accuracy drop when we added data?
  • Hypothesis generation — where your research question came from in the first place.

Abduction is the only one of the three that invents anything. Deduction rearranges; induction extends; abduction proposes.

Model 3 · the bill

Five tests for “best”, and the trap underneath all of them

Explanatory scope

Does it account for all the observations, or only the convenient ones? A cause that explains the Europe-only part but not the after-6-p.m. part is incomplete.

Simplicity

Fewer independent assumptions wins. Two unrelated bugs coinciding is possible; one bug is likelier.

Plausibility

Consistent with what is already known about the system. A cosmic-ray bit-flip explains it, and should not be your first guess.

Testability

Can you check it? An explanation with no distinguishing prediction is a story, not a hypothesis.

Rivals eliminated

Have you ruled the alternatives out, or merely not thought of them? This is the one that gets skipped.

The trap

“Best explanation” silently means best among the explanations I happened to generate.

If your hypothesis space is impoverished, abduction confidently hands you the least-bad member of a bad set — and it feels exactly like insight. This is the mechanism behind confirmation bias, and behind every debugging session that spent six hours on the wrong subsystem.

The discipline that fixes it

Write down three explanations before testing any of them. Not one. Three.

Then ask, for each pair: what observation would distinguish these two? That question converts abduction into an experiment — and an experiment is something you can actually report.

This is also the honest answer to “how did you come up with your research question?” — you abduced it, and then you were obliged to test it.

Synthesis · how the three fit together

A research project uses all three, in a fixed order

1 · Abduction — generate

“Field accuracy is far below the published figure. If the training images were all studio-lit and field images are not, that gap would be expected.”

Produces a hypothesis. Proves nothing.

→

2 · Deduction — derive

“If that is the cause, then accuracy must degrade with measured illumination variance, and must recover under controlled lighting.”

Turns the hypothesis into a prediction that could fail.

→

3 · Induction — test

“Across 900 field images at 5 illumination levels, 3 seeds: accuracy falls 21 points and recovers 16 under controlled lighting.”

Supports the hypothesis. Still does not prove it.

Abduction gives you something worth testing. Deduction tells you what would count as a test. Induction runs it.

Peirce's own account of scientific method, and it maps exactly onto a final-year project. Your proposal's motivation is abductive, its hypotheses are deductive consequences, and its evaluation is inductive.

Running it backwards is misconduct

Get a result first, then invent the hypothesis it supports, then present that hypothesis as though you had it all along. This is HARKing — Hypothesising After the Results are Known.

It is abduction disguised as induction, and it is undetectable in a write-up unless you declare it. Pre-committing to your hypothesis before you look — the discipline from Lecture I.6 — is what stops it. We return to this in Unit III as a research-integrity issue.

Failure modes · one per model

Each form of reasoning has a signature way of going wrong

Deduction → affirming the consequent

Valid: If P then Q. P. ∴ Q.
Invalid: If P then Q. Q. ∴ P.

In your report: “If our method is better, accuracy goes up. Accuracy went up. Therefore our method is better.” Accuracy also goes up with a bigger model, more epochs, a luckier seed, or a leakier split.

Also: denying the antecedent — “no significant difference, therefore no effect.” Absence of evidence, with n = 12, is not evidence of absence.

Induction → hasty generalisation

Too few cases, or the wrong cases, carrying a claim far larger than they can hold.

In your report: “Our approach is faster” after one benchmark on one machine with one seed. Or the biased-sample version: 20 CS students standing in for “users”.

Also: survivorship bias (you only measured what was still there to measure) and base-rate neglect — a 99%-accurate detector on a 0.1% attack rate flags far more false alarms than attacks.

Abduction → the only explanation I thought of

Treating the first plausible cause as established because no rival was ever generated.

In your report: “Accuracy improved because our attention module captures long-range dependencies.” Did you test that, or did it merely sound right? An ablation is the difference.

Also: post hoc ergo propter hoc — B followed A, therefore A caused B. This is the engine of every correlation-read-as-causation error you will meet.

Notice all three appear in results and discussion sections — not in the experiments. The work was fine; the reasoning about it was not.

Work through it in this order

  1. Write your claim and your grounds from last hour's Toulmin boxes.
  2. Ask the diagnostic: could the claim be false even if every ground is true? No → deduction. Yes, and I am generalising → induction. Yes, and I am naming a cause → abduction.
  3. Look up the claim strength your arrow permits:

    Deductive → you may write “is”, “always”, “cannot”.

    Inductive → you may write “on X, Y outperforms Z by n%” — and you must name X.

    Abductive → you may write “suggests”, “is consistent with”, “a plausible explanation is”. Not “because”.

  4. Rewrite your claim so its verb matches its arrow.

What almost everyone will find

Your arrow is inductive, and your claim is written in deductive language.

You measured something on one dataset and wrote “our method is better”. That single verb is the overclaim, and it is what a reviewer attacks first — because it is the easiest thing in the paper to prove wrong.

The second-commonest finding: a results section that is inductive and a discussion section that quietly turns abductive — “our method is better because it captures X”. The “because” was never measured. Either test it with an ablation, or downgrade the verb to “suggests”.

Both repairs cost you one word. Both save you the question you cannot answer in the viva.

Consolidation

Eight things that must survive this hour

  1. Deduction — general to specific, truth-preserving, adds nothing new.
  2. Induction — specific to general, ampliative, never certain.
  3. Abduction — effect to cause, weakest warrant, the only one that invents a hypothesis.
  4. Certainty and informativeness move in opposite directions. You cannot have both.
  1. Mathematical induction is deductive. The name is a historical accident.
  2. Testing, benchmarking and ML generalisation are all inductive — which is exactly why distribution shift and weak baselines hurt.
  3. Debugging is abduction, and so is your research question. Write three explanations, not one.
  4. The cycle: abduce a hypothesis → deduce a prediction → induce a test. Running it backwards is HARKing.

If you remember one sentence: match the verb in your claim to the arrow in your argument — “is” needs a proof, “outperforms on X” needs an experiment, and “because” needs an ablation you have not run yet.
