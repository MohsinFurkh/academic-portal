Reveal · four different questions were hiding in one word

“Faster” was never one question

What the table actually says

  • A has a better mean and a catastrophic tail. Its p99 is 47× its mean — something pathological happens to 1 in 100 requests. Users experience the tail, not the mean.
  • B is slower on average and dramatically more predictable. For an interactive service, B is the better system despite losing the headline row.
  • A uses 8× less memory — which may decide it on constrained hardware, or when you are paying per GB.
  • A barely scales: 4,000 → 12,000 on 8 cores is 3×. B goes 2,900 → 31,000, about 10.7×. B has far better scalability even though it is worse on one core.

Four independent parameters were in play

Time efficiency — and it split into latency (rows 1–3) and throughput (rows 5–6), which trade off against each other.

Space efficiency — row 4, and it can dominate everything else.

Scalability — visible only from rows 5 and 6 together. A single configuration can never show it.

Reliability — not measured at all here, and nobody noticed. What happens when a node dies?

A performance claim with one number is a performance claim that chose which question to answer without telling you.

Part A · parameters 1 and 2

Time and space — and the sub-questions inside each

Time efficiency

Three different things, routinely confused:

  • Latency — time for one operation. Report p50, p95, p99, never the mean alone. Users feel the tail.
  • Throughput — operations per unit time under load. Measured at saturation, with the offered load stated.
  • Total runtime — wall-clock for a whole job. The right measure for batch work, the wrong one for services.

They trade off. Batching raises throughput and worsens latency. A system tuned for one will look bad on the other, so state which you optimised.

Measure honestly: monotonic clock, warm-up discarded and counted, CPU time vs wall time distinguished, probe overhead measured, and the machine otherwise idle.

Space efficiency

Also three things:

  • Peak memory — what decides whether it runs at all on the target device. Usually the number that matters.
  • Working set / steady-state — what decides cache behaviour and, indirectly, most of your time performance.
  • Storage and bandwidth — on-disk size, model size, bytes on the wire.

The trade-off you will meet constantly: time–space. Caching, memoisation, indexes and precomputation all buy time with space. Neither number is meaningful without the other, so report both or your claim is incomplete.

Measure honestly: RSS vs virtual vs heap — say which. Include the runtime, the model weights and the buffers, not just your allocations.

Before measuring anything, answer: which of these six numbers would change someone's decision? Measure those.

Part A · parameter 3 · the one students get wrong

Scalability is a shape, not a number

The definition, and why one measurement cannot give it

Scalability is how a metric changes as a resource or a load changes. It is a curve. A single configuration produces a point, and a point has no shape.

Two directions, and they are different claims:

  • Scale-up (vertical) — more cores, more RAM on one machine.
  • Scale-out (horizontal) — more machines. Brings coordination cost.
  • Load scaling — same resources, more data or more users. Often the one that matters most and is measured least.

Report: speedup S(n) = T(1)/T(n) and efficiency E(n) = S(n)/n, plotted over at least four settings — 1, 2, 4, 8. Three points minimum, four preferred; two points is a line through anything.

What limits it — and the law you must be able to state

Amdahl's Law. If a fraction s of the work is inherently serial, maximum speedup is 1/s, no matter how many processors you add.

The number that ends arguments: if just 5% of your program is serial, you can never exceed 20× speedup — with a thousand cores or a million. This is why “we will just add machines” is not a performance plan.

Gustafson's counterpoint: in practice bigger machines get given bigger problems, and the serial fraction often shrinks as the problem grows — so scaled speedup can look much better. Both laws are right about different questions; say which one you are asking.

Other limits: coordination and synchronisation overhead, contention on a shared resource, network bandwidth, and load imbalance — the slowest worker sets the pace.

Part A · parameter 4 · the one that never gets measured

Reliability: performance when things go wrong

The vocabulary, kept apart

  • Reliability — probability of correct operation over a period. Often summarised as MTBF (mean time between failures).
  • Availability — the fraction of time the system is usable: MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Note that cutting recovery time (MTTR) improves availability just as much as preventing failures — and is usually far cheaper.
  • Fault tolerance — continuing correctly despite component failure.
  • Robustness — behaving sensibly on malformed, adversarial or out-of-distribution input.
  • Correctness under load — does it still give right answers at 95% utilisation, or does it start dropping and corrupting?

“Five nines” = 99.999% availability ≈ 5 minutes of downtime per year. Quote such figures only with the measurement window attached.

How to actually evaluate it

  • Fault injection. Kill a node, drop packets, fill a disk, add latency — and measure what happens. This is the core technique.
  • Stress and soak testing. Run past capacity, and run for a long time. Memory leaks and resource exhaustion are invisible in a 60-second benchmark.
  • Chaos engineering. Fault injection in production, deliberately.
  • Adversarial / edge-case input for robustness claims.
  • Graceful degradation: does it get slower, or does it fall over? Measure the difference — a system that degrades is a different product from one that collapses.

The examiner's question: you measured your system when everything worked. What did you measure when it did not?

For ML projects this parameter maps onto distribution shift, adversarial robustness and calibration — the same idea in different clothing.

Part B · carrying II.5 forward

You proved the bound in II.5. Now you have to measure it.

Recall from II.5 (the four claims)

A complexity bound is a theorem, not a measurement, and there were four different kinds:

Worst case (every input) · average case (a distribution you assumed) · amortised (any sequence of operations) · lower bound (every possible algorithm).

Carry the label into this hour: which of those four your evaluation is testing changes what workload you must run. Testing an average-case claim on adversarial input, or a worst-case claim on random input, measures the wrong thing.

Why measurement is still required

  • The constants, which decide everything at realistic sizes.
  • Real hardware effects — caches, NUMA, GC pauses, thermal throttling — all invisible to the model.
  • The inputs you actually have, rather than worst cases that never occur.

The move that uses both: derive the complexity, then measure across a range of n and check the curve has the shape the theory predicts. Theory says O(n log n) and the measurements bend quadratically? You have found a bug, an unmet assumption or a hidden cost — and each is a genuine finding.

II.5 gave you the proof half of algorithmic evidence. This hour is the measurement half — and neither is sufficient alone.

Part C · the process, in order

Seven steps, and the order is not negotiable

  1. State the question and the decision. Who will do what differently depending on the answer? If nobody, do not run it.
  2. Choose the parameters — which of the four, and why those.
  3. Choose metrics and aggregates — p95, not "time"; peak RSS, not "memory". Fix these before collecting.
  4. Choose the workload. Representative of the real use, diverse enough to disagree with itself, and including the trivial and adversarial cases.
  5. Choose baselines — the strongest available, tuned equally (II.4).
  6. Control the environment — same machine, idle, fixed frequency, interleaved randomised runs, warm-up discarded.
  7. Run, repeat, report — multiple runs, mean ± SD or full distribution, raw data kept, versions and seeds recorded.

Steps 3 and 4 done after seeing results is metric-shopping and workload-shopping — an integrity issue, not a technique issue. Unit III returns to it.

Choosing a workload — four options, ranked

  1. Real production traces. Best evidence; hardest to obtain. Ask early.
  2. Standard benchmark suites — SPEC, TPC, YCSB, MLPerf, GLUE, SPECjbb. Comparable across papers, which is their whole value; may not match your use.
  3. Synthetic workloads with stated parameters. Controllable and reproducible; you must justify that the parameters are realistic.
  4. Microbenchmarks. Isolate one operation. Useful for explaining why; dangerously misleading as a headline result, because they omit exactly the interactions that dominate real systems.

A benchmark is a model of a workload — so everything from II.6 applies. What did it delete, and does the deletion matter?

Part C · the pitfalls

Eight benchmarking crimes, all of them accidental

1 · Measuring the wrong thing

Timing the whole program when only 4% of it is your contribution. Amdahl applies to your evaluation too.

2 · Cold vs warm confusion

First run pays JIT, page faults and cold caches. Discard warm-up — and say how many runs you discarded.

3 · The dead-code trap

The compiler noticed you never use the result and deleted your benchmark. Consume the output, or check the assembly.

4 · Unstable environment

Thermal throttling, turbo boost, other processes, a noisy cloud neighbour. Pin frequency; use an idle machine.

5 · Mean of a heavy tail

The mean of latencies hides the tail users feel. Percentiles, always. Never average percentiles across machines.

6 · Cherry-picked workload

Reporting the 3 benchmarks you win, omitting the 9 you lose. Report all, and discuss the losses.

7 · Unfair baseline

Untuned, unoptimised, or an old version. The II.4 lesson, and the commonest of all.

8 · Ignoring variance

One run per configuration, differences reported to three decimal places. Precision is not accuracy.

Notice that not one of these requires dishonesty. Every one is what happens when a benchmark is run without a plan — which is why the seven steps come before the code, not after.

Consolidation · end of Unit II

Ten things that must survive this hour

  1. Four parameters: time · space · scalability · reliability. “Faster” names none of them.
  2. Time splits into latency, throughput and total runtime, and they trade off.
  3. Report percentiles, not means, for anything a user waits on.
  4. Time and space are a trade-off — report both or the claim is incomplete.
  5. Scalability is a curve, so it needs three or four configurations, never one.
  1. Amdahl: 5% serial caps you at 20× speedup, forever.
  2. Reliability is what you measured when things broke — and almost nobody measures it.
  3. Complexity and measurement answer different questions: derive the curve, then check its shape.
  4. Fix metric, aggregate and workload before collecting. Afterwards is metric-shopping.
  5. A benchmark is a model of a workload — ask what it deleted.

If you remember one sentence from all of Unit II: decide what would count as failure before you measure — the claim, the method, the model and the benchmark are all just machinery for making that decision honest.
