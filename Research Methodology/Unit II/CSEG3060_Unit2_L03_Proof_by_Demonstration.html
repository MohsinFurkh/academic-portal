Do first · 5 minutes · no theory yet

Here is a final-year project. What has it proved?

1 · Alone, 2 min — list every claim this project is entitled to 2 · Pairs, 2 min — cross out the ones it is not 3 · Class, 1 min 5:00

“SmartAttend — an automated attendance system using face recognition.”

The team built a working Android app plus a Flask backend. It runs. On a live demo in the lab it recognised 28 of 30 students in 40 seconds. The report concludes:

  • “The system is accurate, achieving 93.3% recognition.”
  • “The system is faster than manual roll-call.”
  • “The system is suitable for deployment across the university.”
  • “Face recognition is an effective solution for attendance management.”

For each of the four conclusions, answer:

  1. What would you have to compare against for this sentence to mean anything?
  2. Which of these could the demo possibly establish, and which could it never?
  3. Is there any claim here that the working system genuinely settles?

Be honest — most of you have written a report shaped exactly like this one, and it probably scored well.

Reveal · the autopsy

Three of those are not claims the demo can support. One is.

“Accurate — 93.3%”  Compared with what? Measured on whom? Thirty students, once, in lab lighting, with no strangers present and no attempt at impersonation. 93.3% of one session is not an accuracy; it is an anecdote.

“Faster than manual roll-call”  Manual roll-call was never timed. There is no baseline in the study at all. And 40 seconds for 30 students is roughly what calling names takes.

“Suitable for deployment university-wide”  Untested at scale, in corridors, in poor light, with 8,000 identities, with twins, with masks, and with no consideration of consent or data protection. This is a generalisation from n = 1 setting.

“Face recognition is an effective solution”  A claim about a whole technology, from one prototype. The most confident sentence in the report and the least supported.

What it genuinely proved: that a face-recognition attendance pipeline can be built and run end-to-end on commodity Android hardware by a student team in one semester, at roughly this latency, in these conditions.  That is a feasibility claim — and it is real, defensible, and exactly what a demonstration is for.

The system was fine. The claims were three sizes too big for the evidence.

Part A · the method

When building is the argument

The form of the argument

Claim: X is possible / X can be achieved by means M.
Grounds: Here is a working X, built by means M.
Warrant: A functioning instance establishes possibility.

That warrant is unusually solid — it is the one place in computing where a single example is logically sufficient, because existence claims are discharged by one witness. You do not need a sample to prove something can exist; you need the thing.

Which is why the method is legitimate. Proof by demonstration is not second-class evidence — it is the correct and complete method for a specific kind of question, and computing has more of those questions than most fields.

Recall II.1: the fifth kind of research question was feasibility or existence. This is its proof method.

Famous demonstrations that settled real questions

  • The first compiler (Hopper, A-0, 1952) — the question was whether a machine could translate symbolic notation into machine code at all. One working compiler settled it.
  • The Mother of All Demos (Engelbart, 1968) — mouse, hypertext, windows, video conferencing. No experiment; the demo was the contribution.
  • The first web server and browser (Berners-Lee, 1990) — feasibility of a global hypertext system.
  • MapReduce (2004) — that a simple functional abstraction could express most of a company's large-scale data processing, running on cheap unreliable machines.
  • AlexNet as an artefact (2012) — that GPUs made deep CNNs trainable at ImageNet scale. Note it also carried an empirical claim, via a benchmark. Most strong work does both.

Not one of these ran a controlled experiment. Every one changed the field.

Part A · the research method behind building

Design science: the discipline that makes a build into research

The two activities, and the cycle between them

Build

Construct an artefact that did not exist: a construct, a model, a method, or an instantiation.

⇄

Evaluate

Assess it against a stated need, by a stated criterion, and feed what you learn back into the next build.

The distinction that matters to you: development stops at build. Design-science research is the loop — and what makes it research is that the artefact is constructed to answer a question, and that what you learned is stated in a form someone else can use without your code.

Hevner, March, Park & Ram, “Design Science in Information Systems Research”, MIS Quarterly 28(1), 2004 — the standard reference, and the one to cite in your proposal if your project is a build.

Hevner's guidelines, as a checklist for your project

  1. Design as an artefact — produce a viable construct, model, method or instantiation.
  2. Problem relevance — it addresses an important, real problem. Unit I did this.
  3. Design evaluation — utility, quality and efficacy must be rigorously demonstrated. This is the one students skip.
  4. Research contributions — clear contributions in the artefact, the foundations or the methodology.
  5. Research rigour — rigorous methods in both construction and evaluation.
  6. Design as a search process — you explored alternatives; you did not build the first idea. Say what you rejected and why.
  7. Communication — presentable to both technical and managerial audiences.

Guideline 3 is where student projects die: the artefact exists and was never evaluated against anything.

Part A · the demonstration ladder

Not all demonstrations carry the same weight

Level What it is Claim it supports What it still cannot say
1 · Proof of concept A toy implementation on a chosen example. Happy path only. “This approach is not impossible.” Anything about performance, robustness, or other inputs
2 · Prototype End-to-end, handles realistic inputs, someone other than the author can run it. “This can be built, at roughly this cost and this latency.” That it is better than the alternative
3 · Pilot deployment Used by real users, on real data, for a real period, in the real setting. “It survives contact with reality; here is what broke.” That the effects generalise beyond that site
4 · Deployed system + study Instrumented in production, with baseline and measurement. Genuine empirical claims — but this is no longer demonstration alone Universal claims; those need a proof

Where most final-year projects actually sit: level 1, described as though it were level 4. The honest move is to name your level, claim exactly what that level supports, and put the rest in Future Work.

The examiner's question that separates them: “Has anyone other than you ever run this, on input you did not choose?” If the answer is no, you are at level 1 or 2, whatever the report says.

Part B · the distinction the whole hour turns on

“It works” and “it is better” are different claims with different bills

Existence / feasibility claim

“X is possible.”  “X can be done under constraint C.”

Evidence required: one working instance. That is all. One.

Why one is enough: the claim is existentially quantified — there exists an X. A single witness discharges it completely, and a thousand more add nothing.

Ex: “A transformer can run inference within 256 KB of RAM on a Cortex-M4.” Build one; done. The claim is now permanent.

Comparative / evaluation claim

“X is better than Y.”  “X improves Z.”

Evidence required: a fair comparison — same inputs, honest baseline, repeated runs, reported variance. An experiment, which is II.4.

Why one is never enough: the claim is universally quantified over a population of cases. One case is a sample of size one, and no amount of polish on that one case turns it into evidence about the rest.

Ex: “Our compression beats gzip.” On what corpus? Against which settings? At what CPU cost? Building it settles none of this.

The rule to write on the inside of your skull: a demonstration proves possibility, never superiority.

Every time you catch the word “better”, “improved”, “efficient”, “accurate” or “optimal” in a sentence whose only evidence is your own working system, you have crossed from a claim you proved to a claim you asserted. The fix is not more code.

Part C · the balance sheet

What demonstration buys, and what it cannot buy at any price

Advantages

  • Settles feasibility outright. No sampling argument, no statistics, no replication needed — the artefact is the proof.
  • Surfaces the unknown unknowns. Building forces you to confront every problem the design glossed over. You cannot hand-wave a compiler into existence.
  • Produces a reusable asset. The artefact outlives the paper; other people build on it.
  • Highly credible to practitioners. A running system persuades an industry audience that a table of numbers will not.
  • The only method available when the question is about something that does not yet exist — you cannot experiment on, or prove things about, a system nobody has built.

Limitations

  • No comparative force. It cannot establish “better”, “faster” or “more accurate” against anything.
  • n = 1. One artefact, usually one setting, usually run by its own author, on inputs its author chose.
  • Confounded by implementation skill. Did the approach win, or did you simply engineer your version harder than the baseline? This is the deepest problem in the method.
  • Not replicable in the usual sense. A second team rebuilding it makes different choices and gets different results.
  • Existence is often the easy part. That something is possible is frequently not in doubt; the interesting question was whether it is worth doing.
  • Survivorship in the write-up. You report the version that worked, not the six architectures you abandoned — so the reader cannot judge the search.

This is why II.4 and II.5 exist: demonstration answers “can it be done?”, experiment answers “is it better?”, proof answers “must it always hold?”

Part C · the operational part

Seven things that turn a demo into evidence

  1. State the claim as an existence claim. “We show that X is achievable under constraints C” — and stop there.
  2. Name the constraints C explicitly. Hardware, data, scale, time, assumptions. The claim is only as good as the box you draw around it.
  3. Report what it does not handle. Unsupported inputs, failure modes, the cases you excluded. This is your rebuttal from II.1.
  4. Let someone else run it. A second person, on an input you did not pick, is the cheapest credibility you will ever buy.
  5. Report the search, not just the result. What alternatives did you try and reject? Hevner's guideline 6.
  6. Release the artefact. Code, data, configuration, seeds, versions. An unreleased artefact is an assertion about an artefact.
  7. Separate the sections. What was built · what was measured · what is claimed. Most student reports fuse all three into one paragraph, which is how the overclaim hides.

The two sentences that fix most build projects

Before: “Our system is accurate and efficient, demonstrating that deep learning is an effective solution for crop-disease diagnosis.”

After: “We demonstrate that a MobileNetV3-based diagnosis pipeline can run end-to-end on a sub-$80 Android handset at 1.4 s per image and under 180 MB of RAM, for three crops and eight disease classes, on field-captured images. We do not claim improved accuracy over existing methods; that comparison is deferred to a controlled evaluation.”

The second version is shorter on ambition and infinitely stronger on evidence — and it is the one a reviewer cannot touch.

It also does something students find surprising: by naming what it does not claim, it pre-empts the objection instead of inviting it.

Step 1 · Write these four lines

Level

1 proof-of-concept · 2 prototype · 3 pilot · 4 deployed+study. Be honest; nobody is marking this.

Existence claim

“We demonstrate that ___ is achievable under ___.” Both blanks compulsory.

Constraints C

Hardware, data, scale, time, assumptions. At least four.

Explicitly not claimed

“We do not claim ___.” Write two.

If your project is not a build, do this for the artefact you will still produce — a dataset, a harness, a tool. Almost every project has one.

Step 2 · Rules for the cross-examiner

You get exactly four questions, in this order. No others.

  1. “Has anyone other than you run it, on an input you did not choose?”
  2. “Name the alternative you rejected, and why.”
  3. “Point at the word in your claim that means better — and tell me what it is better than.”
  4. “What breaks it? Give me one input class it cannot handle.”

What will happen. Question 3 will find a comparative word in most people's claims — efficient, accurate, robust, optimised, improved — with no comparison anywhere in the project. That word is the whole lesson.

These are the four questions your review panel will ask in October. Hearing them today is free.

Consolidation

Eight things that must survive this hour

  1. Proof by demonstration argues: here is a working instance, therefore it is possible.
  2. It is a legitimate and complete method — for existence and feasibility questions only.
  3. Existence claims need exactly one witness. Comparative claims are never settled by one.
  4. A demonstration proves possibility, never superiority. This is the sentence of the hour.
  1. Design science = build and evaluate, in a loop. Development stops at build; research does not.
  2. Hevner's guideline 3 — rigorous evaluation — is the one student projects omit.
  3. Four levels: proof-of-concept · prototype · pilot · deployed with study. Name yours honestly.
  4. The deepest limitation: you may have engineered your version harder than the baseline.

If you remember one sentence: say “we demonstrate that X is achievable under constraints C, and we do not claim Y” — and every objection to your build disappears, because you made it first.
