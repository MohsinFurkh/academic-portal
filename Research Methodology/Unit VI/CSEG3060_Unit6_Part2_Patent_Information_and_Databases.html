Why search patents at all

The largest technical literature nobody in your lab reads

To check novelty

Before filing — and before spending a year on something already claimed. This is the Unit V Part 2 use.

To avoid infringing

A freedom-to-operate question: can we sell this product in this country without a licence? Different search, different standard.

To read the state of the art

Much engineering detail appears only in patents — never in a paper. Applications publish at 18 months, often ahead of the literature.

To see the landscape

Who files in your field, in which countries, with what trend. Useful for a proposal's motivation and for choosing a licensee.

A granted patent whose term has expired, or which was never filed in India, is free to use here — and its specification is a complete, enabling technical disclosure. Lapsed patents are an underused engineering resource.

The key skill

Classification beats keywords, because patents avoid your words

An IPC code names a technical area, assigned by examiners. CPC is a finer-grained scheme used by the EPO and USPTO. Both are hierarchical.

How to use it in practice

  1. Find one clearly relevant patent by keyword.
  2. Open it and read its classification codes.
  3. Search by that code, with a couple of keywords to narrow.
  4. Repeat with any new codes you meet.

This is citation chaining from Unit III, performed on a taxonomy instead of a reference list.

Why keywords alone fail

Patent attorneys write to be broad, not to be found. Your “MRI scan” is their “image data set”; your “neural network” is their “trained computational model”; your “missing sequence” is their “incomplete input modality”.

Build a synonym list per feature before you search — and include the generic, abstracted phrasing a drafter would choose.

Two concepts that prevent expensive mistakes

Patent families and legal status

Family

All the applications and patents worldwide that share a priority — the Indian filing, the PCT application, the European, US and Japanese members.

Why it matters: one invention can appear as eight documents. Counting them as eight pieces of prior art is a classic student error — and the family view tells you instantly which markets the applicant chose to pay for.

Legal status

A document's status changes over time: pending, granted, refused, withdrawn, opposed, revoked, lapsed for unpaid renewals, or expired at the end of its term.

Why it matters: for novelty, status is irrelevant — a published application is prior art even if it was later refused. For freedom to operate, status is everything — only a patent in force in that country can stop you.

Keep the two questions apart. “Can I patent this?” is answered by everything ever published, anywhere. “Can I sell this in India?” is answered by patents currently in force in India, read claim by claim. Students routinely run one search and answer both questions with it.

Worked example

Searching the running example, step by step

1 · Features: volumetric segmentation · missing input modality · trained model selection · memory budget

Start from the claim you drafted, one feature per line.

2 · Synonyms: "image data set" · "modality" · "incomplete input" · "sub-model" · "resource constraint"

Write the drafter's vocabulary, not yours.

3 · Google Patents: segmentation AND ("missing modality" OR "incomplete modalities") AND MRI

A fast first pass, to find one relevant document.

4 · Open the best hit → note its CPC codes → search Espacenet by code + "modality"

This is the step that finds what keywords missed.

5 · InPASS: same concepts, restricted to India → check what is actually in force here

Novelty is worldwide; freedom to operate is national.

6 · Record each query: database · date · query string · hits · relevant

The search log from Unit III, unchanged.

Stop at saturation — when new queries keep returning documents you already have. Then build the feature × document matrix from Unit V Part 2 and decide.

Beyond novelty

Patent landscaping: reading a field from its filings

Who

Top applicants in your classification code. Imaging vendors? Start-ups? Universities? This tells you who a licensee might be — and who might sue you.

Where

Which countries the families cover. A field where nobody files in India is a field where you may be free to operate here.

When

Filings per year in the code. Rising sharply means a crowded, fast-moving area; flat and old means either mature or abandoned.

Three cautions before you put a chart in a proposal

1 · An 18-month blind spot. Applications publish at 18 months, so the last year and a half of any curve is incomplete and will rise later. 2 · Counting families, not documents. Otherwise a single invention inflates the count. 3 · Filing is not innovating. A rising count may reflect a company's filing policy, not the field's progress — the same construct-validity question you met in Unit II.

Lab · 30 minutes · laptops open

Prior-art search on your own project idea

Do this (20 min)

  1. Write your invention in one sentence; list four technical features.
  2. Write three synonyms for each feature, in drafters' language.
  3. Search Google Patents; find one relevant document; record its classification codes.
  4. Search Espacenet by classification; find two more.
  5. Search InPASS for the Indian position; note what is in force.
  6. For your best hit: record kind code, claim 1's features, family size and legal status.
  7. Fill the feature × document matrix.

Then answer, in writing (10 min)

  • Does any single document contain all four of your features?
  • Do any two together — and would a skilled person have combined them?
  • Which of your features is not shown anywhere? That is your candidate contribution.
  • Is the closest document in force in India?
  • On this evidence: file, narrow, or publish instead?

Submit the search log and the matrix. This is the assessed artefact for the prior-art lab, and it is also exactly what an IP cell asks for at an invention disclosure.

Key takeaways

Five habits of a competent patent searcher

1

Search at least two databases — one Indian, one international — and know each one's blind spot.

2

Classification beats keywords. Find one document, take its codes, search by code.

3

Read the claims and the kind code, never the title. A published application is not a granted patent.

4

Novelty is worldwide and status-blind; freedom to operate is national and status-dependent. Two questions, two searches.

5

Keep a search log and a feature matrix. A negative result is diligence, never proof.
