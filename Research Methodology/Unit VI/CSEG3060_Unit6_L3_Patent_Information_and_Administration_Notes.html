Course outcome addressed — CO4

Locate, interpret and analyse patent information using classification systems and databases, and describe the institutional machinery that administers the patent system.

Lectures 1 and 2 covered what a patent is and how it is transacted. This lecture covers where the information lives, and who runs the system.

How to use these notes

Sections 2—8 are patent information — literature, classification, databases and search. Sections 9—12 are administration — institutions, prosecution, post-grant procedure and reform.

One figure frames the whole lecture: roughly 70 percent of the world’s technical information is disclosed only in patent documents and never published elsewhere. Most of what you need is not in IEEE Xplore or the ACM Digital Library.

  1. Identify the components of a patent document and read a front page using INID codes.
  2. Explain the IPC hierarchy and decode a classification such as G06F 21/62.
  3. Distinguish CPC from IPC and name the CPC codes covering your own research area.
  4. Navigate PATENTSCOPE, USPTO, Espacenet and IPAIRS, and choose the right one for a given task.
  5. Formulate a search combining classification codes, keywords and Boolean syntax.
  6. Describe the three tiers of patent administration, the prosecution workflow, and recent reforms.

2. Patent Literature

2.1 Definition and Scope

Patent literature is the entire corpus of published patent documents and related materials generated by national and regional patent offices. According to WIPO it is the world’s most comprehensive source of technical information, with approximately 70 percent of all technical information disclosed only in patent documents and never published elsewhere.

ComponentPurpose
Bibliographic dataIdentification — number, date, country, applicant, inventor, classification codes
AbstractConcise technical summary of the invention
Description (specification)Detailed technical disclosure enabling a person skilled in the art to reproduce the invention
ClaimsLegally enforceable statements defining the scope of protection
DrawingsDiagrams, flowcharts or schematics supporting the disclosure

2.2 Types and Kind Codes

Primary literature consists of patent applications and granted patents published by national and regional offices. Secondary literature comprises search reports, examination opinions, patent gazettes, classifications, abstracting services and analytical reviews.

  • A1 — patent applications published with a search report.
  • A2 — patent applications published without a prior search report.
  • B1 — granted patents, with no prior application publication.
  • B2 — granted patents published after the application was already published.
  • Utility models — shorter-term protection for incremental innovations, common in Germany and China. They are prior art too.

2.3 Why It Matters in Computer Science

  • Prior-art search — determining whether an algorithm, architecture or system has already been patented before filing.
  • Freedom-to-operate analysis — identifying patents that a planned commercial product might infringe.
  • Technology landscape studies — mapping innovation trends in machine learning, blockchain, quantum computing and cybersecurity.
  • Citation analysis — tracking how patented technologies build on earlier scientific work.
  • Licensing opportunities — identifying patent holders open to licensing arrangements.

2.4 The Front Page and INID Codes

A typical front page, standardised under WIPO standard ST.36, contains numbered bibliographic fields:

FieldContent
(10)Document identification — patent number, kind code
(20)Application data — application number, filing date
(30)Priority data — earlier applications under the Paris Convention
(40)Publication date
(50)Technical information — IPC/CPC classification, title, abstract
(60)References — related applications
(70)Identification of parties — applicant, inventor, assignee, agent
INID codes

Internationally agreed Numbers for the Identification of bibliographic Data. The field numbers are constant across offices and languages — which is what makes a Japanese patent readable without Japanese. Within field 70, (71) is the applicant, (72) the inventor; (54) is the title. These are frequently asked.

3. The International Patent Classification

The IPC, established under the Strasbourg Agreement (1971) and administered by WIPO, is the international standard for classifying patents and utility models. It is revised annually to accommodate emerging technologies.

3.1 Five-Level Hierarchy

Section → Class → Subclass → Group → Subgroup(1)
Worked example — G06F 21/62

G — Section: Physics
G06 — Class: Computing; calculating; counting
G06F — Subclass: Electric digital data processing
G06F 21 — Group: Security arrangements for protecting computers, components, programs or data against unauthorised activity
G06F 21/62 — Subgroup: Protecting access to data via a platform, e.g. using keys or access control rules

The IPC version is appended to indicate the edition used — for example IPC 2025.01.

3.2 The Eight Sections

SectionTitleDomain
AHuman necessitiesAgriculture, food, health, personal items
BPerforming operations; transportingIndustrial processes, transport
CChemistry; metallurgyChemical engineering
DTextiles; paperTextile and paper industries
EFixed constructionsBuildings, mining
FMechanical engineeringEngines, pumps, lighting, heating
GPhysicsInstruments, computing, optics, nuclear engineering
HElectricityElectric power, electronics, communication

Most computer science patents fall within Section G and Section H — in practice, within G06 (computing) or H04 (electric communication technique). Learning those two branches is worth more than memorising all eight sections.

4. The Cooperative Patent Classification

The CPC was jointly developed by the EPO and the USPTO and entered into force on 1 January 2013. It is built on the IPC backbone but extends it with finer subdivisions.

4.1 Four Differences from the IPC

  • Depth of classification. CPC adds approximately 200,000 additional codes beyond the IPC’s roughly 70,000.
  • Tagging. CPC uses tagging schemes for cross-cutting technologies.
  • Update cadence. CPC is revised every three months; the IPC is updated annually.
  • Adoption. Used by both the EPO and the USPTO, harmonising the two largest patent-granting authorities.

4.2 CPC Codes for Computer Science

CPC codeSubject matter
G06N 3/04Neural network architectures
G06N 3/08Learning methods for neural networks
H04L 9/00Cryptographic mechanisms
H04L 9/06Block cipher systems
G06F 16/00Information retrieval; database structures
G06T 7/00Image analysis
G10L 15/22Speech recognition procedures
G06Q 50/18Legal services — relevant to IPR itself

4.3 Why Both Systems Matter, and What Else Exists

WIPO and many national offices — including IP India — still index primarily by IPC. The EPO and USPTO use CPC extensively, and the additional granularity permits far more precise searches in cutting-edge technologies, which is exactly where a CS researcher works.

Other systems: USPC, used by the USPTO until 2015 when CPC fully replaced it — you will still meet it on older documents; FI and F-term, Japanese systems offering very fine subdivisions; and DE, the German national scheme used by the DPMA.

Cross-reference, always

For comprehensive searches, researchers cross-reference multiple systems. A single-classification search is the equivalent of searching one database and declaring the field empty.

5. Online Patent Databases

5.1 WIPO PATENTSCOPE

Covers PCT international applications published 18 months after the priority date; national and regional collections from over 100 offices; and non-patent literature cited in search reports.

Key features: cross-lingual information retrieval (CLIR), searching several languages simultaneously through semantic translation; Boolean, phrase and proximity syntax; citation extraction and chemical structure search; machine translation of full text via the TAPTA system; and RSS feeds and alerts for newly published applications.

Strengths: unmatched coverage of PCT applications and developing-country filings; powerful translation. Limitations: variable OCR quality on older documents; some national collections lag in publication.

5.2 USPTO

ToolPurpose
PatFTFull-text search of US granted patents from 1976 onward
AppFTPublished US patent applications
Patent CenterThe unified portal replacing legacy PAIR and EFOAS systems

Search modes: quick two-field query; advanced Boolean search across title, abstract, claims, inventor, assignee and classification; and classification browse or query by CPC code.

Strengths: authoritative for US filings; rich bibliographic and legal-status data; Patent Center provides full prosecution histories — office actions and responses. Limitations: US jurisdiction only; pre-1976 patents are image-only.

The underused resource

The prosecution history in Patent Center shows exactly which arguments persuaded an examiner to allow a claim in your technical field. For anyone drafting their first application, it is worth more than any textbook.

5.3 EPO Espacenet

Free access to over 140 million patent documents from more than 100 countries.

  • Smart search — natural language queries.
  • Advanced search — supports IPC, CPC, applicant, inventor, date range and priority number.
  • CPC and IPC browser — interactive tree navigation.
  • Citation graph and INPADOC legal events — visualising family relationships and prosecution history across jurisdictions.
  • Patent Translate — machine translation supporting 32+ languages.
  • Open Patent Services (OPS) — a REST API for bulk data retrieval, used heavily in patent analytics.

Strengths: best-in-class coverage of European and global filings; rich family and legal-event data; excellent search interface. Limitations: some advanced bulk-data features require a free EPO account.

5.4 IP India — IPAIRS

The CGPDTM provides the Indian Patent Information Retrieval System and a public-facing search portal. Search by application number, patent number, title, abstract, claims, full text, inventor, applicant and CPC code.

It covers applications filed in India under the Patents Act, 1970, including international applications entering the Indian national phase via PCT. Status tracking spans Filed, Published, Under Examination, Granted, Withdrawn, Abandoned and Revoked.

Strengths: authoritative source for Indian filings, with reliable and current legal status. Limitations: the interface is less polished than WIPO or Espacenet; OCR quality on older scans is variable; English-only, despite India accepting applications in Hindi and other scheduled languages.

6. Practical Search Strategy

6.1 The Anatomy of a Search

  1. Topic identification — define the technical subject with precision.
  2. Query formulation — combine keywords, classification codes and applicant filters.
  3. Result refinement — apply date ranges, jurisdictions and citation analysis.

6.2 Keyword versus Classification Searching

StrategyStrengthsWeaknesses
Keyword searchDiscovers synonyms and emerging terminology; intuitiveMisses translations and unusual phrasings; subject to noise
Classification searchReliable across languages and synonyms; preciseRequires familiarity with IPC/CPC; may miss innovative cross-domain applications

The most effective searches combine both — classification codes to narrow the field, keywords to refine within it. Keyword-only searching is why students conclude an idea is novel when it is not; classification-only searching is why they miss the cross-domain application that anticipates them.

6.3 Boolean Operators and Syntax

Patent databases support extended Boolean logic: AND, OR, NOT; quotation marks for exact phrases; parentheses for nested grouping; and wildcards, typically * for zero or more characters and ? for a single character.

Espacenet field syntax
(cla = ("machine learning" OR "neural network")) AND ap = ("Google")
Searches claims mentioning machine learning or neural networks, in patents assigned to Google.

6.4 Worked Example — AI-Based Intrusion Detection

  1. Identify relevant CPC codes: G06N 3/04 and G06N 3/08 (neural networks); H04L 63/00 and H04L 63/14 (network security); G06F 21/55 (malware detection).
  2. Construct the query on Espacenet:
    CPC = (G06N3/04 OR G06N3/08) AND (H04L63/00 OR G06F21/55)
    Date range: last five years  ·  Jurisdiction: US, EP, WO
  3. Refine with keywords such as “intrusion detection” or “anomaly detection” in title and abstract.
  4. Analyse results — export bibliographic data, generate citation graphs, identify top assignees (Cisco, IBM, Palo Alto Networks), and observe temporal trends.

6.5 Citation and Family Analysis

  • Backward citations — references cited by the patent. Useful for finding earlier art; the examiner and applicant have already done part of your search.
  • Forward citations — patents that later cite this one. Useful for assessing influence and mapping downstream innovation; a heavily forward-cited patent is a foundational one.
  • Patent families — identical inventions filed in multiple jurisdictions. Useful for understanding global protection strategies and identifying all relevant legal events.
Landscape example — transformer architectures in NLP

Since Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need (2017), transformers have reshaped natural language processing. A landscape study reveals the commercial dimension of that academic breakthrough.

Databases: Espacenet and PATENTSCOPE.
Query: CPC = (G06N3/045 OR G06N3/0464 OR G06N3/0455) AND title/abstract = ("attention mechanism" OR "transformer" OR "self-attention")

What family analysis tells you

It answers a question no single patent can: where does this competitor think the money is? A family filed in the US, EP, CN, JP and KR signals a global product. One filed only in India signals something else entirely.

7. The Institutional Architecture

Modern patent administration operates at three interconnected tiers.

7.1 Tier 1 — National Patent Offices

Each sovereign state maintains a national or regional office examining applications under domestic law:

  • USPTO — the largest national office by volume.
  • EPO — a supranational office examining under the European Patent Convention for contracting states.
  • JPO — Japan Patent Office.
  • CNIPA — China National Intellectual Property Administration, the fastest-growing filer globally.
  • IPO India — operating through four regional offices at Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata under the Patents Act, 1970.
  • KIPO — Korean Intellectual Property Office.

7.2 Tier 2 — Regional Patent Systems

Regional offices consolidate filing procedures for groups of countries: the Eurasian Patent Organization (EAPO), the African Regional Intellectual Property Organization (ARIPO), the African Intellectual Property Organization (OAPI), and the EPO acting as a regional grant body.

7.3 Tier 3 — International Coordination

WIPO, headquartered in Geneva, administers the PCT, the Madrid System for marks and the Hague System for designs. WIPO functions as a treaty secretariat and normative standard-setter rather than a granting authority.

The distinction that matters

Tiers 1 and 2 grant patents. Tier 3 does not. WIPO administers treaties and sets standards but has never granted a patent — the same point made about the PCT in Unit V. Note also that the EPO appears in two tiers: it examines supranationally under the EPC and acts as a regional grant body, which is why a European patent is still validated nationally.

8. The Patent Prosecution Process

Prosecution follows a standardised workflow, though details vary across jurisdictions:

  1. Filing — submission of the application (specification, claims, abstract, drawings) to the office of first filing.
  2. Formalities examination — verification of administrative requirements.
  3. Search — identification of prior art, producing a search report.
  4. Substantive examination — assessment against the patentability criteria.
  5. Office actions and responses — exchanges between examiner and applicant, often involving claim amendments.
  6. Grant or refusal — the culminating decision; refused applications may be appealed.
Patentability as a logical conjunction
Patentability = N ∧ IS ∧ IA ∧ SD(2)

where N is novelty, IS inventive step (or non-obviousness), IA industrial applicability (or utility), and SD sufficient disclosure. Each condition must be independently satisfied — which is what a conjunction means, and why an examiner attacks whichever element is weakest.

8.1 Post-Grant Administration

  • Renewal and annuity payments — periodic fees maintaining the patent in force, typically escalating over the 20-year term.
  • Opposition procedures — third parties may challenge validity, under Article 99 EPC or Section 25 of the Indian Patents Act.
  • Invalidation and revocation — judicial or administrative processes for cancelling patents.
  • Licensing registers — public records of contractual patent exploitation.
  • Patent information and databases — the systematised repositories of Sections 2 to 6.

9. Recent Reforms in Patent Administration

Patent administration has undergone continuous reform since the early 2000s, on three themes.

9.1 Acceleration and Quality

  • USPTO Track One — prioritised examination introduced under the Leahy-Smith America Invents Act, 2011, providing expedited examination for an additional fee with a final disposition target of 12 months.
  • Patent Prosecution Highway networks — bilateral agreements allowing participating offices to fast-track applications relying on a partner office’s work product, reducing duplication and search costs.
  • EPO “Raising the Bar” (post-2010) — tightened examination standards, expanded supplementary European search reports, and increased reliance on examiner-added prior art.

9.2 Software-Related Subject Matter

Software remains the most contested category. Computer programs as such were historically excluded in India (Section 3(k)) and Europe (Article 52 EPC) — but technical applications of software are routinely patentable.

  • USPTO Revised Patent Subject Matter Eligibility Guidance (2019, with updates through 2024) — clarifies the two-step Alice/Mayo framework with specific worked examples in artificial intelligence and computer-implemented inventions.
  • EPO Compendium of Case Law updates — continuous refinement of the “technical character” requirement.
  • Indian Patent Office CRI Guidelines (2018, 2024 drafts) — computer-related inventions may be patentable where they demonstrate a technical effect or technical advancement.

9.3 Emerging Technologies

  • AI-assisted prior-art search — major offices including CNIPA and JPO have deployed machine-learning tools to assist examiners in source-document identification.
  • Deposit of biological materials — governed by the Budapest Treaty (1977, as amended).
  • SEPs and FRAND licensing — India’s DPIIT issued a draft Standard Essential Patents Policy in 2021; the European Commission has advanced FRAND licensing frameworks.
  • AI as inventor — the DABUS cases (Thaler v. Comptroller, EPO Boards of Appeal 2021–22; Thaler v. Vidal, US Federal Circuit 2022) rejected artificial intelligence systems as inventors, prompting continuing academic and policy debate.

9.4 Digital Transformation

The COVID-19 crisis accelerated modernisation: WIPO expanded ePCT filing and electronic priority document exchange through the Digital Access Service; the USPTO implemented fully electronic prosecution and launched Patent Center, replacing the legacy PAIR system; the EPO finalised its framework for opposition oral proceedings by videoconference (2021); and the Indian Patent Office extended timelines and accepted electronic filing under the Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2020, continuing through the 2024 Rules.

The line to watch

Every major jurisdiction has now held that an inventor must be a natural person. That holding is under active academic and policy pressure as AI systems contribute an increasing share of the inventive step.

10. Summary

  • 70 percent of technical information appears only in patent documents.
  • INID codes make any patent front page readable in any language.
  • The IPC is five levels deep; G06 and H04 cover most computer science work.
  • CPC adds roughly 200,000 codes, updates quarterly, and is used by both the EPO and the USPTO.
  • Search by classification and keyword together — neither alone is defensible.
  • Administration runs in three tiers; only tiers 1 and 2 grant patents.
  • Patentability = N ∧ IS ∧ IA ∧ SD — a conjunction, so every element must hold.
  • Reform has run on three themes: acceleration, software subject matter, and emerging technology.

11. Exam Preparation

Preparation points
  1. Memorise the IPC hierarchy — Section, Class, Subclass, Group, Subgroup — and be able to construct a code and identify its components.
  2. Distinguish IPC from CPC clearly: IPC is administered by WIPO under the Strasbourg Agreement; CPC is a joint EPO–USPTO system with greater granularity and more frequent updates.
  3. Know the major databases cold — compare PATENTSCOPE, USPTO, Espacenet and IP India on geographic scope, classification system and distinctive features.
  4. Practise constructing search queries combining CPC codes and keywords for given scenarios — blockchain consensus, IoT security, federated learning.
  5. Understand INID codes: (71) applicant, (72) inventor, (54) title are frequently asked.
  6. Connect to software IP — how these databases apply to software patents, including the Indian exclusion under Section 3(k).
  7. Distinguish the tiers: national offices, regional bodies, and international organisations. Examiners reward precise institutional attribution.
  8. Cite Articles, Sections and dates — TRIPS Article 27, PCT Article 8, Section 3(k), the AIA 2011.

Structured landscape question
