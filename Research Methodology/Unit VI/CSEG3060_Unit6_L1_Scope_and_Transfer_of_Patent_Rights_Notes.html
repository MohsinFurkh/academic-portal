Scope and Transfer of Patent Rights
Extent of protection, rights conferred and limitations — then assignment, succession and formalities · 60 minutes · B.Tech. (Computer Science & Engineering) · Dr. Mohsin Furkh Dar
CO3 Claims define scope Section 48 Six limitations Assignment Succession Stanford v. Roche
Course outcome addressed — CO3

Evaluate the scope, rights and limitations of patents, and the mechanisms by which patent rights are transferred, in computing research and innovation contexts.

Unit V established what a patent is and how it is obtained. Unit VI begins with what it actually covers, and how it moves from one owner to another.

How to use these notes

This lecture merges two syllabus topics — the scope of patent rights and their transfer by assignment and succession — because transfer is meaningless until you know what is being transferred. Sections 3—7 are scope; Sections 8—13 are transfer.

One sentence governs the first half: the claims define the monopoly. One governs the second: writing, stamping and recordal are what make a transfer good against the world.

Section 14.2 lists the statutory sections you should be able to cite from memory. In this unit they are the most reliable marks available.

1. Learning Outcomes

  1. Explain how the claims determine the extent of protection, and identify the four claim forms used in computing.
  2. State the rights conferred under Section 48 and identify infringing acts in a software context.
  3. Apply the six statutory limitations and the three judicially evolved doctrines that cut into the monopoly.
  4. Distinguish assignment from succession, and both from licensing.
  5. List the formalities that make a transfer enforceable, and the consequences of omitting them.
  6. Evaluate employee-invention and university–industry transfers using decided cases.

2. Key Concepts and Terminology

The two most useful to a developer

Exhaustion and prior user rights are the limitations most likely to protect someone actually shipping software — and the two students most often omit from an answer.

3. Extent of Protection — the Role of Claims

The extent of protection is determined principally by the claims. This is universally accepted: Section 10(5) of the Indian Patents Act, 1970, Article 69 of the European Patent Convention, and 35 U.S.C. §112 all converge on the principle. Everything outside the claims — abstract, description, drawings — is context for interpretation but does not, by itself, confer protection.

3.1 Four Claim Forms in Computing

  • Apparatus / system claims — directed to a hardware configuration, e.g. a multiprocessor architecture with a particular cache-coherence protocol.
  • Method / process claims — directed to a sequence of steps, e.g. a method of compressing video using a specific predictive algorithm.
  • Computer-readable medium (CRM) claims — directed to a non-transitory storage medium carrying instructions to perform the method; historically important in the United States.
  • Computer-implemented invention (CII) claims — a hybrid form often drafted for the European Patent Office to establish technical character.
The drafting trade-off

The broader the claim, the wider the scope of protection — but also the greater the risk of rejection over prior art, or of later invalidation for lack of novelty or inventive step. Claim scope is a negotiation with the examiner, not an aspiration.

4. Subject Matter of Protection

Patent protection extends only to patentable subject matter. In computer science this question has generated decades of litigation, and the three major systems answer it differently.

4.1 India — Section 3

Expressly excluded from patentability:

  • Computer programs per se, mathematical or business methods, and algorithms — Section 3(k).
  • Topographies of integrated circuits — Section 3(l).
  • Traditional knowledge — Section 3(p).

The Indian Patent Office Manual nevertheless recognises that a computer program combined with novel hardware or a technical effect is not “per se”.

4.2 United States — 35 U.S.C. §101

Subject matter is determined by the two-step Alice/Mayo framework. An abstract idea implemented on a generic computer is not patent-eligible. This is the holding of Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International (2014), which invalidated software-related business-method patents claiming no inventive concept beyond the abstract idea.

4.3 Europe — EPC Article 52

Programs for computers and mathematical methods are excluded “as such”, but technical character can restore patentability — see T 258/03 (auction method) and the G1/19 line on computer-implemented simulations.

The practical takeaway, identical in all three

A pure algorithm, formula or business method cannot be patented. An algorithmic invention that produces a technical effect beyond the ordinary interaction between software and hardware may be.

5. Territorial and Temporal Extent

5.1 Territorial Extent

Patents are strictly territorial. A US patent has no legal force in India, and an Indian patent is unenforceable in Germany. To obtain multi-jurisdictional protection the inventor must file in each country of interest, ordinarily within twelve months of the priority date under the Paris Convention.

This is fundamental to freedom-to-operate analysis: a developer shipping software worldwide must examine the patent register in every relevant jurisdiction.

5.2 Temporal Extent

The standard term is twenty years from the date of filing, subject to renewal and annuity fees. India follows this under Section 53. On expiry the subject matter enters the public domain, free for all to use. There is no concept of an “evergreen” patent beyond the term, although supplementary protection certificates exist for pharmaceuticals in Europe.

Scope has three axes

Protection is bounded simultaneously by what the claims cover, where the grant runs, and how long it lasts. A student who answers only the first has answered a third of the question.

6. Rights Conferred

6.1 The Negative Nature of the Right

The distinction that governs everything

A patent does not grant the patentee an affirmative right to make, use or sell the invention. It grants the right to prevent others from doing so.

In computing this is stark: a patent on a novel data-compression algorithm does not license the inventor to encode video; it empowers the inventor to stop third parties from encoding video using that algorithm without consent.

6.2 The Statutory Bundle — Section 48

  1. Where the invention is a product, to prevent third parties from making, using, offering for sale, selling or importing that product for purposes of commercial exploitation.
  2. Where the invention is a process, to prevent third parties from using that process, and from using, offering for sale, selling or importing the product obtained directly by that process.

This language is mirrored in Article 28 of the TRIPS Agreement, 1994, and is the universal standard.

The process limb matters commercially in computing: a method claim reaches not only the running of the process but the product obtained directly by it — which for a patented encoding method can extend to the encoded file itself.

6.3 Infringing Acts in Computing

ActIllustration in computer science
MakingManufacturing a chip embodying a patented architecture
UsingRunning a patented machine-learning inference method on a server
Offering for saleAdvertising a SaaS product that implements a patented process
SellingDistributing copies of software that include a patented data structure
ImportingBringing into the territory hardware manufactured abroad that infringes an apparatus claim

Indirect infringement under Section 114A and analogous provisions addresses contributory liability — supplying a means for working the invention when the supplier knows the means are intended for infringing use.

6.4 Enforcement

  • Civil action — injunction, damages or account of profits, delivery-up of infringing goods.
  • Administrative action — seizure of infringing goods at customs under the Customs Act, 1962.
  • Criminal action — generally unavailable: in most jurisdictions patent infringement is a civil wrong, not a crime. Criminal sanctions for software piracy arise under copyright, not patent.

7. Limitations on Patent Rights

Limitations balance the patentee’s exclusive right against the public interest, enabling follow-on innovation and preventing abuse. They fall into statutory exceptions and judicially evolved doctrines.

7.1 Six Statutory Exceptions

  1. Exhaustion of rights (first sale). Once a patented product is lawfully sold by or with the patentee’s consent, rights in that specific article are exhausted. The buyer may use, resell or repair it freely. A customer who lawfully purchases a patented microprocessor may install it in any device without further licence.
  2. Research and experimental use — Section 47(3). Acts done for experimental purposes relating to the subject matter do not infringe. The scope is narrower in India than in Europe. In the United States, Madey v. Duke University (2002) held that even non-profit university research with commercial implications falls outside the exception.
  3. Bolar exception — Section 47(1)(d). Acts reasonably related to obtaining regulatory approval before patent expiry are exempt, preventing de facto term extension through regulatory lag. Derived from Roche Products v. Bolar Pharmaceutical (1984); formulated for drugs, but the Indian statutory language is not so limited.
  4. Prior user rights — Section 47(2). Protects a person in bona fide use of the invention in India before the priority date, who continues to use it. Vital in software, where trade-secret developers frequently claim prior private practice of the algorithm.
  5. Government use — Section 47(1)(a)–(c) and Chapter XVI. Permits the Central Government to use a patented invention for its own purposes on specified grounds — a form of compulsory licence.
  6. Compulsory licensing — Section 84. Any interested party may apply three years after the patent’s seal date, on grounds including non-working, unreasonable refusal to licence, or public health necessity. India’s first was granted in 2012 (Natco v. Bayer, Nexavar). Doctrinally relevant to standard-essential patents in ICT.

7.2 Three Judicially Evolved Doctrines

  • The doctrine of equivalents. Prevents an infringer escaping liability by making insubstantial modifications. Developed in the US and applied cautiously in India; substantially narrowed by Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo (2002), which imposed prosecution-history estoppel. In computing this arises when a competitor writes the algorithm in a different language or runs it on a different platform.
  • Inequitable conduct and patent misuse. A patentee who withholds material prior art from the examiner, or extends the patent beyond its legitimate boundaries — for instance by tying sales of patented software to other products — may be barred from enforcement.
  • Abuse of dominance. Under Article 102 TFEU and Section 4 of the Indian Competition Act, 2002, a dominant holder’s refusal to license standard-essential patents may constitute abuse. Huawei v. ZTE (2015) and Microsoft v. Motorola (2013) mark the intersection of FRAND commitments with patent scope.
The organising distinction

Statutory limitations protect specific activities. Judicial doctrines police the patentee’s own conduct. A patent can be perfectly valid and still unenforceable.

8. Case Law on Scope

8.1 Diamond v. Diehr, 450 U.S. 175 (1981)

Facts: a process for curing rubber that used a computer to calculate cure times from the Arrhenius equation. Held: a claim reciting a mathematical formula is not thereby unpatentable, so long as the formula is applied in a non-abstract, practical manner producing a transformation. Significance: established that software-implemented processes with a tangible technical effect are patent-eligible; still a foundational citation worldwide.

8.2 State Street Bank v. Signature Financial Group (1998, CAFC)

Held: business methods implemented on a computer are patentable provided they produce a “useful, concrete and tangible result”. Significance: opened the floodgates to US business-method patents in the late 1990s; subsequently limited by Bilski (2010) and Alice (2014).

8.3 Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International, 573 U.S. 208 (2014)

Facts: a computer-implemented method for mitigating settlement risk using a third-party intermediary. Held: invalid under the two-step test — directed to an abstract idea (intermediated settlement) with no inventive concept. Significance: narrowed software patent eligibility sharply, particularly for business methods, and has been used to invalidate hundreds of subsequent claims.

8.4 Novartis AG v. Union of India (2013)

Primarily a pharmaceutical case, but instructive: Section 3(d) denied a patent on the basis of evergreening — minor variations of a known substance not showing enhanced efficacy. Commentators apply the reasoning by analogy to argue that incremental software updates contributing no technical effect should likewise fail the inventive-step test.

8.5 Koninklijke Philips v. Princo (EPO, 2009)

A leading European authority on exhaustion applied to CD-R patents. It demonstrates that even where one patent has been exhausted, related patents may remain enforceable where the exhaustion argument fails for a different claim category — relevant to layered software–hardware portfolios.

9. Why Patents Are Transferred

The economic rationale is that an inventor’s commercial capacity may differ from their inventive capacity. A university laboratory may lack the manufacturing, marketing or distribution infrastructure to commercialise a patented algorithm; a multinational may lack the research base to invent it but possess the capacity to scale and monetise it.

Transfer therefore bridges invention and commercialisation. In computer science this matters acutely because software patents often originate in academic research, open-source ecosystems coexist with proprietary patent strategies, and collaborative university–industry R&D requires structured transfer mechanisms.

Three mechanisms exist: assignment (voluntary transfer of ownership), succession (transfer by operation of law), and licensing (grant of permission with ownership retained — the subject of Lecture 2).

10. Assignment of Patent Rights

10.1 Definition and Nature

Definition — Assignment

An assignment is the transfer of the entire ownership of a patent, or an undivided share in it, from the assignor to the assignee. It is, in essence, a transfer of title. Governed by Section 68 of the Indian Patents Act, 1970.

Assignment may occur before grant (assignment of the right to obtain a patent) or after grant; for the entire term or a portion of it; and of the entire patent or an undivided share in it.

10.2 Seven Conditions for a Valid Assignment

ConditionRequirement
Written instrumentMust be in writing. Oral or implied assignments are not recognised
Identification of partiesAssignor and assignee, with full legal names and addresses
Subject-matter specificityThe patent or application clearly identified by number, title or filing date
ConsiderationMonetary or otherwise, specified unless the assignment is by way of gift
Territorial scopeWhether the assignment covers India alone or multiple jurisdictions
Effective dateThe date from which the assignment takes effect
SignaturesDuly signed by the assignor and accepted by the assignee

10.3 Partial versus Complete Assignment

  • Complete assignment — the entire right, title and interest passes; the assignor retains no residual rights.
  • Partial assignment — only an undivided share (say 50 percent), or rights limited to a specific territory, field of use or period.

Partial assignments are common in computer science, where joint inventions leave multiple inventors holding undivided shares.

10.4 Assigning the Right to Obtain a Patent

Inventors frequently assign rights before grant. This is common in employment agreements (inventions vest in the employer), sponsored research agreements, and pre-grant assignment to a startup founded by the inventor.

Such assignments are conditional: they become absolute only upon grant. Until then the assignee holds a contingent right.

11. Succession of Patent Rights

Succession is the transmission of patent rights by operation of law, without any voluntary act between parties. It arises on death of the patentee, insolvency or bankruptcy (rights vest in the official assignee or trustee), merger or amalgamation of a corporate patentee, and compulsory acquisition by the State.

11.1 Succession by Inheritance

On death, patent rights devolve upon the legal representatives in accordance with the applicable personal law — the Hindu Succession Act, the Indian Succession Act, Muslim Personal Law.

  • The heirs require no fresh patent application; the patent number and term remain unchanged.
  • The succession must be recorded with the patent office to confer enforceable rights.
  • Until recorded, the heirs cannot institute proceedings for infringement.

11.2 Succession by Corporate Restructuring

In technology companies, patents transfer automatically on mergers, acquisitions, demergers or spin-offs, and transfers of undertakings. Such transfers do not require a separate deed of assignment — but must still be notified to the patent office for recordal in the Register of Patents.

12. Legal Formalities for Transfer

All transfers, whether by assignment or succession, must comply with specific formalities. Failure renders the transfer ineffective against third parties, though it may still bind the parties inter se.

12.1 Five Formalities

  1. Written and duly executed instrument. Section 68 requires every instrument dealing with the property in a patent to be in writing and signed by the parties.
  2. Stamp duty. Assignments are treated as conveyances under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 or the relevant state legislation. An inadequately stamped instrument may be inadmissible in evidence.
  3. Registration with the patent office. Lodged with the CGPDTM, typically within six months of execution, extendable by a further period not exceeding six months.
  4. Entry in the Register of Patents. A public document, serving as prima facie evidence of the particulars entered.
  5. Notification in the Official Journal. Providing constructive notice to the world.

Documents typically required: the original assignment deed or a certified copy; the form for recording the assignment (Form 16 in India — application for direction for substitution of the applicant or patentee); a power of attorney if filed by an agent; proof of payment of fees; and an affidavit verifying the facts in cases of succession.

12.2 Consequences of Non-Compliance

Four consequences
  • The assignment is void against third parties — bona fide purchasers for value without notice.
  • The assignee cannot institute or defend infringement suits until recordal.
  • The assignee will not receive renewal notices or other official communications.
  • In priority disputes, an unrecorded assignment will not be recognised.

However, as between the parties to the assignment, the contract remains valid as a private obligation.

The examinable line

It runs down the Ownership row. Assignment and succession are transfers of ownership; licensing is a grant of rights with ownership retained. Everything else in the table follows from that distinction, which is the subject of Lecture 2.

13.1 Software-Specific Complications

  • Modular nature. A software patent may cover multiple components; an assignment may be of a specific module only, which makes subject-matter specificity harder to draft.
  • Open-source conflict. Assigning patents covering open-source code may conflict with the licence — for example the GPL’s patent retaliation clauses.
  • Inventorship disputes. Collaborative coding environments generate frequent disputes about who the true inventor is, complicating every downstream assignment.
  • Standard-essential patents. In 5G, Wi-Fi and video codecs, transfer must respect FRAND commitments — they travel with the patent.

13.2 Employee Inventions

Under Section 6 and the surrounding jurisprudence: an invention made in the course of employment generally vests in the employer; an invention outside the scope of employment but using employer resources leaves rights with the employee, who may owe compensation. Software engineers routinely sign proprietary information and inventions assignment agreements (PIIAAs) on joining, prospectively assigning future inventions.

13.3 University–Industry Transfers

Three routes: exclusive licensing; assignment of applications in exchange for equity or milestone payments; and spin-off formation with rights assigned to the new company. Shaped in India by CSIR, IIT and related policies; globally by the Bayh-Dole Act (1980), which allowed US universities to retain title to federally funded inventions and catalysed the technology transfer office model.

13.4 Case Law on Transfer

Smith International v. Hughes Tool (Fed. Cir. 1983). Issue: validity of an assignment executed by an employee shortly after the application was filed. Held: upheld — a valid assignment may be made even before the application is filed, provided it clearly identifies the subject matter and the intent to assign future rights. Relevance: confirms the enforceability of pre-grant assignments in university–industry collaboration.

Stanford v. Roche, 563 U.S. 776 (2011). Issue: whether an automatic assignment clause in a university research agreement takes precedence over a later assignment signed by the researcher with a third party. Held: an automatic present-assignment clause does not automatically vest title in the university; the researcher must execute a subsequent assignment, and until then holds legal title. Relevance: reshaped university technology transfer practice worldwide.

Bishwanath Prasad Radhey Shyam v. Hindustan Metals, AIR 1979 SC 1519. Held: an assignment must be construed strictly according to the language used; rights not expressly assigned remain with the assignor. Relevance: the foundational Indian authority on interpreting assignment deeds.

The drafting lesson from Stanford v. Roche

“Agrees to assign” and “hereby assigns” are not equivalent. Stanford used the first and lost the patent to a company that used the second. Draft in the present tense.

14. Practical Implications and Summary

14.1 For the Computer Science Researcher

  1. Pre-filing disclosures. Avoid premature disclosure that may defeat patentability.
  2. Inventorship declarations. Ensure correct identification of inventors; misidentification can invalidate the patent and complicate transfers.
  3. Employment agreements. Review existing agreements for pre-existing assignment clauses.
  4. Collaborative research agreements. Define ownership and transfer terms upfront to avoid post-facto disputes.
  5. Documentation. Maintain meticulous records of contributions to support inventorship and assignment claims.
  6. Valuation. Engage qualified valuers when transferring patents, particularly in startup equity contexts.

14.3 Summary

  • The claims define the monopoly — s.10(5), Art. 69 EPC and §112 all agree.
  • Scope is bounded on three axes: claims, territory and term.
  • A patent is a negative right: it excludes, it does not authorise.
  • Section 48 confers product and process limbs; TRIPS Article 28 mirrors it.
  • Six statutory limitations and three judicial doctrines cut into the monopoly.
  • Assignment transfers title; succession transfers it by law; licensing does not transfer it at all.
  • Writing, stamping and recordal are what make a transfer good against the world.

Preparation points

15.3 Applied Questions

Scenario 1

Dr. A, a professor at UPES, files a patent application for a novel machine-learning algorithm jointly with her PhD scholar B. The research was funded by a corporate sponsor C. The application is filed before grant. Subsequently B leaves the university and joins a startup D.

Task: draft the essential clauses of an assignment deed transferring B’s share in the patent application to D, and identify the legal formalities required.

Scenario 2

A patent granted to software company X is transferred to company Y by assignment. The assignment deed is not registered with the patent office. Company Y now sues an infringer Z.

Task: discuss whether Y’s suit is maintainable, and what Y must do to cure the defect.
