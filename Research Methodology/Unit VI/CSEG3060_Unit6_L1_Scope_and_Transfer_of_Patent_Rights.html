Extent of protection · 1 of 3

The claims, and nothing but the claims

Three systems converge on one principle: Section 10(5) of the Indian Patents Act, Article 69 of the European Patent Convention and 35 U.S.C. §112 all provide that the claims define the scope. The abstract, description and drawings are context for interpretation — they confer no protection by themselves.

Apparatus / system claims

Directed to a hardware configuration — e.g. a multiprocessor architecture with a particular cache-coherence protocol.

Method / process claims

Directed to a sequence of steps — e.g. a method of compressing video using a specific predictive algorithm.

Computer-readable medium

A non-transitory storage medium carrying instructions to perform the method. Historically important in the United States.

Computer-implemented invention

A hybrid form drafted for the European Patent Office, framed to establish technical character.

The drafting trade-off

The broader the claim, the wider the scope of protection — and the greater the risk of rejection over prior art, or later invalidation for lack of novelty or inventive step. Claim scope is a negotiation with the examiner, not a wish.

Extent of protection · 2 of 3

Three jurisdictions, three tests, one underlying question

India — Section 3

Expressly excluded from patentability:

  • Computer programs per se, mathematical and business methods, and algorithms — Section 3(k)
  • Topographies of integrated circuits — s.3(l)
  • Traditional knowledge — s.3(p)

The Patent Office Manual recognises that a program combined with novel hardware or a technical effect is not “per se”.

United States — 35 U.S.C. §101

Determined by the two-step Alice/Mayo framework. An abstract idea implemented on a generic computer is not patent-eligible.

This is the holding of Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International (2014), which invalidated business-method patents claiming no inventive concept beyond the abstract idea itself.

Europe — EPC Article 52

Programs for computers and mathematical methods are excluded “as such” — but technical character restores patentability.

See T 258/03 (auction method) and the G1/19 line on computer-implemented simulations.

The practical takeaway is identical in all three: a pure algorithm, formula or business method cannot be patented; an algorithmic invention producing a technical effect beyond the ordinary interaction between software and hardware may be.

Extent of protection · 3 of 3

Where it applies, and for how long

Territorial extent

Patents are strictly territorial. A US patent has no legal force in India; an Indian patent is unenforceable in Germany.

Multi-jurisdictional protection requires filing in each country of interest, ordinarily within twelve months of the priority date under the Paris Convention.

This is why freedom-to-operate analysis is jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction: a developer shipping software worldwide must examine the patent register in every relevant territory.

Temporal extent

Twenty years from the date of filing in most jurisdictions, subject to renewal and annuity fees. India follows this under Section 53.

On expiry the subject matter enters the public domain, free for all to use.

There is no concept of an evergreen patent beyond the term — although supplementary protection certificates exist for pharmaceuticals in Europe.

Scope is therefore bounded on three axes at once: what the claims cover, where the grant runs, and how long it lasts. A student who answers only the first has answered a third of the question.

Rights · 1 of 2

A negative right — the distinction that governs everything

What a patent does not give you

A patent does not grant the affirmative right to make, use or sell the invention. It grants the right to prevent others from doing so.

Illustrated starkly in computing: a patent on a novel data-compression algorithm does not license the inventor to encode video. It empowers the inventor to stop third parties from encoding video using that algorithm without consent.

The statutory bundle — Section 48

Where the invention is a product: to prevent third parties from making, using, offering for sale, selling or importing that product for purposes of commercial exploitation.

Where the invention is a process: to prevent third parties from using that process, and from using, offering for sale, selling or importing the product obtained directly by that process.

This language is mirrored in Article 28 of the TRIPS Agreement, 1994, and is the universal standard.

Why the process limb matters in computing

A method claim reaches not only the running of the process but the product obtained directly by it. For a patented encoding method, that can extend to the encoded file itself — which is how a method claim acquires commercial teeth against downstream distributors.

Rights · 2 of 2

Five infringing acts, illustrated in computing

ActIllustration in computer science
MakingManufacturing a chip embodying a patented architecture
UsingRunning a patented machine-learning inference method on a server
Offering for saleAdvertising a SaaS product that implements a patented process
SellingDistributing copies of software that include a patented data structure
ImportingBringing in hardware manufactured abroad that infringes an apparatus claim

Indirect infringement

Under Section 114A and analogous provisions elsewhere, contributory liability attaches to supplying a means for working the invention where the supplier knows the means are intended for infringing use.

Enforcement routes

Civil — injunction, damages or account of profits, delivery-up of infringing goods.
Administrative — customs seizure of infringing goods under the Customs Act, 1962.
Criminal — generally not available: patent infringement is a civil wrong. Criminal sanctions for software piracy arise under copyright, not patent.

Limitations · 1 of 2

Six statutory exceptions that cut into the monopoly

1 · Exhaustion — first sale

Once a patented product is lawfully sold by or with the patentee’s consent, rights in that article are exhausted. The buyer may use, resell or repair it freely.

A customer who buys a patented microprocessor may install it in any device without further licence.

2 · Research and experimental use

Section 47(3) exempts acts done for experimental purposes relating to the subject matter.

Narrower in India than Europe. In the US, Madey v. Duke University (2002) held that even non-profit university research with commercial implications falls outside the exception.

3 · Bolar exception

Section 47(1)(d) exempts acts reasonably related to obtaining regulatory approval before expiry — preventing de facto term extension through regulatory lag.

From Roche Products v. Bolar Pharmaceutical (1984). Formulated for drugs, but the Indian statutory language is not so limited.

4 · Prior user rights

Section 47(2) protects a person in bona fide use of the invention in India before the priority date, who continues to use it.

Vital to software, where trade-secret developers frequently claim they were already practising the algorithm privately.

5 · Government use

Section 47(1)(a)–(c) and Chapter XVI permit the Central Government to use a patented invention for its own purposes on specified grounds — a form of compulsory licence.

6 · Compulsory licensing

Section 84 allows any interested party to apply three years after sealing, on grounds of non-working, unreasonable refusal to licence, or public need.

India’s first was granted in 2012 — Natco v. Bayer (Nexavar). Doctrinally relevant to standard-essential patents in ICT.

Limitations · 2 of 2

Three doctrines the courts built

The doctrine of equivalents

Prevents an infringer escaping liability by making insubstantial modifications to a patented invention. Developed in the United States, applied cautiously in India.

Substantially narrowed by Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo (2002), which imposed prosecution-history estoppel on its application.

In computing this arises when a competitor implements the algorithm in a different language or on a different hardware platform.

Inequitable conduct and patent misuse

A patentee who withholds material prior art from the examiner, or who extends the patent beyond its legitimate boundaries — for example by tying sales of patented software to other products — may be barred from enforcement altogether.

Abuse of dominance

Under Article 102 TFEU and Section 4 of the Indian Competition Act, 2002, a dominant holder’s refusal to license standard-essential patents may constitute an abuse.

Huawei v. ZTE (2015) and Microsoft v. Motorola (2013) mark the intersection of FRAND commitments with patent scope.

Note the pattern: statutory limitations protect specific activities; judicial limitations police the patentee’s own conduct. A patent can be perfectly valid and still unenforceable.

Case studies

Four decisions that drew and redrew the boundary

Diamond v. Diehr, 450 U.S. 175 (1981)

Facts: a process for curing rubber using a computer to calculate cure times from the Arrhenius equation.

Held: reciting a mathematical formula does not make a claim unpatentable, so long as the formula is applied in a practical manner producing a transformation.

Significance: established that software-implemented processes with a tangible technical effect are eligible. Still the foundational citation worldwide.

State Street Bank v. Signature Financial (1998, CAFC)

Held: business methods implemented on a computer are patentable provided they produce a “useful, concrete and tangible result”.

Significance: opened the floodgates to US business-method patents in the late 1990s — and was subsequently limited by Bilski (2010) and Alice (2014).

Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank, 573 U.S. 208 (2014)

Facts: a computer-implemented method for mitigating settlement risk through a third-party intermediary.

Held: invalid under the two-step test — directed to an abstract idea (intermediated settlement) with no inventive concept.

Significance: narrowed software eligibility sharply; used to invalidate hundreds of subsequent claims.

Novartis AG v. Union of India (2013)

A pharmaceutical case, but instructive: Section 3(d) denied a patent for evergreening — minor variations of a known substance without enhanced efficacy.

Commentators apply the reasoning by analogy to argue that incremental software updates contributing no technical effect should likewise fail the inventive-step test.

Part 2 begins

A patent is property. Property can be moved.

The economic rationale is simple: an inventor’s commercial capacity may differ from their inventive capacity. A university laboratory may have no manufacturing, marketing or distribution infrastructure. A multinational may lack the research base to invent the algorithm but can scale and monetise it.

Transfer is the mechanism that bridges invention and commercialisation.

Assignment

Voluntary transfer of ownership by act of the parties.

Succession

Transfer of ownership by operation of law — death, insolvency, restructuring.

Licensing

Grant of permission to use, ownership retained. Lecture 2 of this unit.

Why this matters acutely in computing: software patents often originate in academic research; open-source ecosystems coexist with proprietary patent strategies; and collaborative university–industry R&D requires structured transfer mechanisms from the outset.

Transfer · 1 of 4

Assignment — transfer of title

An assignment is the transfer of the entire ownership of a patent, or an undivided share in it, from the assignor to the assignee. It is, in essence, a transfer of title. Governed in India by Section 68 of the Patents Act, 1970.

When an assignment may occur

  • Before grant — assignment of the right to obtain a patent
  • After grant
  • For the entire term, or a portion of it
  • Of the entire patent, or an undivided share in it

Complete versus partial

Complete assignment — the entire right, title and interest passes. The assignor retains no residual rights.

Partial assignment — only an undivided share (say 50 percent), or rights limited to a specific territory, field of use or period.

Partial assignments are common in computing, where joint inventions leave multiple inventors holding undivided shares.

Assigning the right to obtain a patent — before it exists

Common in employment agreements (inventions vest in the employer), sponsored research agreements, and pre-grant assignment to a startup founded by the inventor.

Such assignments are conditional: they become absolute only on grant. Until then the assignee holds a contingent right.

Bishwanath Prasad Radhey Shyam v. Hindustan Metals (AIR 1979 SC 1519) establishes the interpretive rule: an assignment is construed strictly according to the language used. Rights not expressly assigned remain with the assignor. Draft for what you mean to transfer, and say so.

Transfer · 3 of 4

Succession — transfer without agreement

Succession is the transmission of patent rights by operation of law, without any voluntary act between parties.

Death of the patentee

Transmission to legal heirs under personal law or by will.

Insolvency

Rights vest in the official assignee or trustee in bankruptcy.

Corporate restructuring

Mergers, acquisitions, demergers, spin-offs and transfers of undertakings.

Compulsory acquisition

By the State, under specific statutory provisions.

Succession by inheritance

Rights devolve on the legal representatives under the applicable personal law — the Hindu Succession Act, the Indian Succession Act, Muslim Personal Law.

  • Heirs need no fresh application; the patent number and term are unchanged
  • The succession must be recorded with the patent office to confer enforceable rights
  • Until recorded, the heirs cannot sue for infringement

Succession by corporate restructuring

Patents transfer automatically on merger, acquisition, demerger or transfer of undertaking. No separate deed of assignment is required — but the transfer must still be notified to the patent office for recordal in the Register.

Transfer · 4 of 4

Five formalities — and what happens if you skip them

a · Written instrument

Section 68 requires every instrument dealing with property in a patent to be in writing and signed.

b · Stamp duty

Assignments are treated as conveyances under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 or state legislation. An inadequately stamped instrument may be inadmissible in evidence.

c · Registration

Lodged with the CGPDTM, typically within six months of execution, extendable by a further six.

d · Register of Patents

Recorded in the Register — a public document and prima facie evidence of the particulars entered.

e · Official Journal

Published, giving constructive notice to the world.

Documents required

Original assignment deed or certified copy · Form 16 — application for direction for substitution of applicant or patentee · power of attorney, if filed by an agent · proof of payment of fees · affidavit verifying the facts, in cases of succession.

Consequences of non-compliance

  • The assignment is void against third parties — bona fide purchasers for value without notice
  • The assignee cannot institute or defend infringement suits until recordal
  • The assignee will not receive renewal notices or official communications
  • In priority disputes, an unrecorded assignment is not recognised

But: as between the parties themselves, the contract remains valid as a private obligation.

Applied context

Four complications specific to software patents

Modular nature

A software patent may cover several components. An assignment may be of a specific module only — which makes subject-matter specificity harder to draft.

Open-source conflict

Assigning patents covering open-source code may conflict with the licence — for example the GPL’s patent retaliation clauses.

Inventorship disputes

Collaborative coding environments generate frequent disputes about who the true inventor is, which complicates every downstream assignment.

Standard-essential patents

In 5G, Wi-Fi and video codecs, transfer must respect FRAND commitments — they travel with the patent.

Employee inventions

Under Section 6 and the surrounding jurisprudence: an invention made in the course of employment generally vests in the employer; an invention outside the scope of employment but using employer resources leaves rights with the employee, who may owe compensation.

Software engineers routinely sign proprietary information and inventions assignment agreements (PIIAAs) on joining, prospectively assigning future inventions.

University–industry transfers

Three routes: exclusive licensing; assignment of applications in exchange for equity or milestone payments; and spin-off formation with rights assigned to the new company.

Shaped in India by CSIR, IIT and related policies; globally by the Bayh-Dole Act (1980), which let US universities retain title to federally funded inventions and catalysed the TTO model.

Case studies

Three cases on who actually owns the invention

Smith International v. Hughes Tool (Fed. Cir. 1983)

Issue: validity of an assignment executed by an employee shortly after the application was filed.

Held: the assignment was upheld. A valid assignment of an invention may be made even before the application is filed, provided it clearly identifies the subject matter and the intent to assign future rights.

Relevance: confirms the enforceability of pre-grant assignments in university–industry collaboration.

Stanford v. Roche, 563 U.S. 776 (2011)

Issue: whether an automatic assignment clause in a university research agreement beats a later assignment the researcher signed with a third party.

Held: an automatic present-assignment clause does not automatically vest title in the university. The researcher must execute a subsequent assignment; until then, the researcher holds legal title.

Relevance: reshaped university technology transfer practice worldwide.

Bishwanath Prasad Radhey Shyam v. Hindustan Metals (1979)

Issue: scope of assignment and interpretation of the deed.

Held: an assignment must be construed strictly according to the language used. Rights not expressly assigned remain with the assignor.

Relevance: the foundational Indian authority on reading assignment deeds.

Stanford v. Roche is the one to remember. The words “agrees to assign” and “hereby assigns” are not equivalent, and a university that used the first lost the patent to a company that used the second.

Seven takeaways

  • Claims define the scope — s.10(5), Art. 69 EPC, §112 all agree
  • Scope is bounded on three axes: claims, territory, term
  • A patent is a negative right — it excludes, it does not authorise
  • Section 48 gives product and process limbs; Art. 28 TRIPS mirrors it
