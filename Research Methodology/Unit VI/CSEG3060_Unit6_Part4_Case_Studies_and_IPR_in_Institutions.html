Case 1

Novartis AG v. Union of India (Supreme Court, 2013)

What happened

Novartis sought an Indian patent on the beta crystalline form of imatinib mesylate, marketed as Glivec. The application was refused, and the refusal was ultimately upheld by the Supreme Court.

The Court applied Section 3(d): a new form of a known substance is not an invention unless it shows enhanced efficacy. On the evidence, improved bioavailability was not accepted as establishing enhanced therapeutic efficacy for this purpose.

The principle it settled

India may set its own patentability standard within TRIPS, and Section 3(d) is a deliberate barrier to “evergreening” — a chain of minor variants extending protection on an old molecule.

Discuss: does a higher bar reduce incentives to develop incremental improvements that genuinely help patients — or does it keep essential medicines affordable? Both claims are empirical. What evidence would settle them?

Methodological link: the case turned on what counts as evidence of efficacy — a construct-validity question of exactly the kind Unit II taught you to ask.

Case 2

Natco v. Bayer (2012) — India's first compulsory licence

What happened

Bayer held an Indian patent on sorafenib (Nexavar), a kidney and liver cancer drug. Natco applied for a compulsory licence three years after grant.

The Controller granted it, finding that the reasonable requirements of the public were not being met, that the drug was not available at a reasonably affordable price, and that it was not being worked in India. Natco was permitted to sell a generic version against a royalty to Bayer. The decision was upheld on appeal.

The principle it settled

An Indian patent is conditional on the invention being worked and reasonably available. The monopoly is a means, not an end — the bargain from Unit V Part 1, enforced.

Discuss: what would a firm do differently, knowing this rule exists? Notice that both “price higher elsewhere” and “licence granted here” can be true at once.

This is also why the statement of working exists: without data on whether patents are being worked, the provision could not be applied at all.

Case 3

Turmeric, neem and basmati — and the library that answered them

What happened

Patents granted abroad over uses of turmeric for wound healing and over a neem-based fungicide were challenged on the ground that the knowledge was long-standing and public in India. The turmeric patent was revoked; the neem patent was revoked after opposition. A dispute over basmati rice claims followed a similar path.

The obstacle was practical: the prior art existed in Sanskrit, Hindi, Urdu and Tamil texts that foreign examiners could not search.

The response, and the principle

India built the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library, documenting traditional medicine in formats and languages patent examiners can search, and made it available to offices under access agreements.

The principle: something is not novel merely because the examiner could not find it. Prior art is what exists, not what is indexed — and making knowledge findable is itself a policy instrument.

Methodological link: this is the Unit III search problem at national scale. A literature that cannot be searched behaves, in practice, as if it did not exist.

Case 4

Two computing cases: Ferid Allani and the SEP disputes

Ferid Allani (Delhi High Court, 2019)

A computer-related application had been refused under Section 3(k). The Court directed attention to the words “per se” and to whether the invention demonstrated a technical effect or contribution, rather than treating any involvement of software as fatal.

Why it matters to you: it is the reason the framing exercise in Part 3 is worth doing — and the reason the technical effect must be in the specification from the start.

Standard-essential patent litigation in India

Indian courts have dealt with disputes between telecommunications patent holders and handset makers over FRAND licensing: whether royalties should be computed on the chipset or the device, what a fair rate is, and when an injunction is appropriate.

Why it matters to you: in a standardised field, freedom to operate can dominate novelty as a commercial question.

Both areas are still developing. Cite them as illustrations of how the questions are framed, not as fixed rules — and check the current position before relying on either.

Method

A repeatable way to analyse any IPR case

1

Facts

Who, what right, what act, in which country, when. Strip the commentary away first.

2

Legal question

Which provision, and what exactly had to be decided? Most cases turn on one clause.

3

Decision and reason

What was held, and on what reasoning — not merely who won.

4

Both sides

State the losing argument in its strongest form. If you cannot, you have not understood the case.

5

Consequences

Who changes behaviour, and how? That is where the policy argument actually lives.

6

Your project

One sentence: what would you do differently tomorrow because of this case?

This is the structure expected in the Case Studies 2 assessment. Steps 4 and 5 carry most of the marks — summarising a case is description; arguing it is analysis.

Institutions

Why universities have IP policies at all

What an institutional IP policy covers

  • Ownership of inventions, software, copyright and data created by faculty, staff and students
  • Disclosure: the duty to report an invention before publishing it
  • Decision process: who decides to file, and who pays
  • Revenue sharing between inventors, department and institution
  • Publication rights and the maximum delay for protection
  • Conflict of interest where staff hold equity in a licensee
  • Consultancy and sponsored research: who owns what in each case

Why it exists

To make ownership predictable before a dispute, so that a licensee can be told truthfully who owns what.

To ensure the institution's investment — space, equipment, supervision, funding — is reflected, while rewarding inventors enough that they disclose rather than quietly take an invention elsewhere.

And to protect the academic mission: publication delayed briefly, never blocked.

Student inventors are normally included in both the disclosure duty and the revenue share. Find your policy, read the ownership and sharing clauses, and know them before your viva.

Institutions

What an IP cell actually does with your disclosure

1

Receive

An invention disclosure form: what it is, who contributed, what has been published already

2

Search

Prior art, as in Part 2 — usually the step that decides everything

3

Assess

Patentability, market, cost, and whether anyone would license it

4

Decide

File, defer, or release the rights back to the inventors

5

Manage

Prosecution, renewals, marketing, licensing — for twenty years

What to bring to a first meeting

Your one-sentence invention, the feature list, your prior-art search log and matrix, evidence that it works, the list of everyone who contributed, and every disclosure already made — dates included, even the ones you regret.

What makes them say no

Already published. No identified user. A claim that reads as a computer programme as such. Contributions from a company or hospital with no written agreement. Nobody willing to do the development work.

Institutions

Four ownership situations, and who typically owns what

SituationTypical positionWhat to check first
Your own project, university facilitiesInstitution owns or co-owns the invention under the IP policy; you are named as inventor and share revenueThe policy's ownership and sharing clauses
Funded project (agency grant)Governed by the grant terms — which may require protection, reporting, or Indian manufactureThe sanction letter and the scheme's IP conditions
Industry-sponsored projectNegotiated: the sponsor may take ownership, exclusive rights, or a licence; publication may be delayedThe agreement's IP, publication and confidentiality clauses before you start work
Internship or collaboration elsewhereThe host's policy and your signed agreement govern; work done there is usually theirsWhat you signed on day one — and whether it reaches work done back at university
The pattern: ownership follows the agreement, not the effort. The time to read the agreement is before the work, because afterwards you are only discovering what you already agreed.

For your own project

The IP checklist to run before you submit anything

Before you publish or demo

  • Is there anything here that might be patentable? If unsure, ask the IP cell — the conversation is free.
  • Have you already disclosed it? Write down every date: poster, repository, seminar, demo.
  • Does a funder or sponsor agreement require you to notify them first?
  • Are all contributors identified, including interns and collaborators?
  • Is any third-party code, dataset or model in your work, and what does its licence require?

Before you sign anything

  • Publisher copyright forms: what rights do you keep — preprint, thesis, teaching use?
  • Internship agreements: do they claim work done outside the internship?
  • Collaboration agreements: is there an IP clause at all? Silence is the problem, not the solution.
  • Open-source releases: approved by your institution, and compatible with what you intend later?
  • Data agreements: the ethics obligations from Unit III travel with the data.
Two habits carry almost all of this: keep a dated record of everything you create and disclose, and ask before you sign or publish.

Try it · 25 minutes · assessed case study

Argue a case, then audit your own position

Case panel (15 min)

Each team takes one case and prepares both sides using the six-step method from slide 7.

  • A · Section 3(d) and evergreening — Novartis
  • B · Compulsory licensing — Natco v. Bayer
  • C · Traditional knowledge and searchable prior art
  • D · Software patentability under Section 3(k)

Three minutes each: facts, question, decision, the strongest losing argument, consequences, and one implication for your project.

Self-audit (10 min)

Answer for your own project, in writing.

  1. Who owns each output, and under which document?
  2. What has already been disclosed, and when?
  3. Is anything worth protecting — and if so, by which instrument?
  4. What third-party licences are you relying on?
  5. What is the one action you will take this week: talk to the IP cell, read the policy, check a licence, or file nothing and publish openly?

“File nothing and publish openly” is a legitimate, considered answer — provided it is chosen rather than defaulted into.

From a research result to a right somebody uses

And the whole course in one line: Unit I found a problem worth solving, Unit II chose evidence that could settle it, Unit III read the field honestly and treated data ethically, Unit IV wrote and defended it, and Units V–VI decided who may use it. Each stage constrains the next — which is why methodology is not a preliminary to research, but the thing itself.
