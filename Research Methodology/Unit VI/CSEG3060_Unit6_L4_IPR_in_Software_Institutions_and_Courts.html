Foundations

Three structural reasons software resists classification

1 · Textually a literary work

Source code is written in formal languages that resemble, in expressive character, technical writing or music. That makes it a natural subject for copyright.

2 · Functionally an invention

A program causes a machine to perform novel operations, solves technical problems, and may produce patentable technical effects. That brings it within patent law.

3 · Dual-layered

Expressive elements (source code, UI design) and functional elements (algorithms, architectures, behaviour) coexist in a single artefact. No single IP regime is fully adequate.

Four balances the policy has to strike

Incentives for innovation and R&D investment

Access to foundational computing knowledge for cumulative research

Disclosure of inventions against protection of trade secrets

Standardisation for interoperability against exclusion for proprietary control

WIPO and TRIPS left most of this balance to national legislatures and courts — which is precisely why the jurisdictional divergence you met in Unit V and Lecture 3 exists at all.

Copyright · 1 of 2

Copyright — automatic, cheap, and limited to expression

In India computer programs are protected as literary works under Section 13(1)(a) of the Copyright Act, 1957. Section 2(ffc) defines a computer programme as “a set of instructions expressed in any form… capable of causing an electronic device to perform a specific function”. Internationally: the Berne Convention and, explicitly, the WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996).

What is and is not protected

Protected — source code, object code, the specific arrangement and selection of code, screen displays in some jurisdictions, and preparatory design material.

Not protected — the underlying algorithm, the idea, the function performed, the system architecture as an abstract concept, and methods of operation.

The idea–expression dichotomy traces to Baker v. Selden, 101 U.S. 99 (1879).

Rights and their limits

The owner may reproduce the program in any material form · issue copies to the public · perform it in public · communicate it to the public · translate, adapt or arrange it — which covers porting, converting and extracting parts.

Fair dealing under Section 52 permits private and personal use including backup copies · non-commercial research, criticism or review · testing, debugging and ensuring interoperability — reverse engineering under specified conditions · use by educational institutions and libraries.

Term: life of the author plus 60 years (Section 22). For anonymous, pseudonymous or government works, 60 years from publication.

Copyright · 2 of 2

What copyright buys you, and what it cannot

Four advantages

  • Automatic acquisition — no registration required; protection arises on creation and fixation
  • Low cost — substantially cheaper than patent prosecution
  • Long effective term — coverage far exceeds the commercial life of most software
  • Broad subject matter — almost any software qualifies

Three limitations

  • Does not protect ideas, algorithms or functional concepts — competitors may independently develop functionally equivalent code
  • Cannot prevent independent creation, only copying
  • Difficulty proving copying of non-literal elements — structure, sequence and organisation

The consequence, stated plainly

If a competitor reads your published paper, understands your algorithm, and writes their own implementation from scratch, copyright gives you nothing. They have copied the idea, which copyright does not protect, and not the expression, which it does.

That gap is the entire commercial case for a software patent — and it is why the next two slides matter.

Patents

Patent — expensive, uncertain, and reaches the idea

Under the Indian Patents Act, Section 3(k) disallows patents on “a computer program per se or algorithms”. But amendment and judicial interpretation permit patenting where software produces a technical effect or constitutes a technical solution to a technical problem. In the US, the Alice/Mayo two-step test governs eligibility.

What a patent covers

  • The novel, non-obvious, useful method or process implemented by the software
  • The technical effect produced — improved database query performance, an image processing method, an encryption technique
  • The system architecture when tied to a specific technical implementation

Not covered: pure algorithms or abstract mathematical methods · business methods on a generic computer · computer programs per se with no claimed technical contribution.

Five requirements

  1. Novelty — not anticipated by prior art
  2. Inventive step — not obvious to the person skilled in the art
  3. Industrial application — capable of industrial use
  4. Disclosure — sufficient to enable the skilled person to perform it
  5. Exclusion avoidance — must not fall within Section 3(k)

Three advantages

Protects the functional and conceptual aspects copyright cannot · allows claims against independent re-implementations · a strong barrier to entry and a source of licensing revenue.

Four risks

Cost and time — prosecution is expensive and slow · uncertain scope under s.3(k) and Alice · disclosure obligation, which may benefit competitors · validity challenges — software patents are frequently litigated and sometimes invalidated.

Licensing · 1 of 3

Seven commercial software licensing models

Proprietary

Licensor retains all rights; limited permission to use; source not disclosed. Terms typically prohibit modification, redistribution and reverse engineering. Windows EULA, Adobe Photoshop.

Freeware

Distributed at no cost, source not disclosed, modification and redistribution generally prohibited. Licensor retains copyright and full commercial rights.

Shareware

Try before you buy — a licence fee is expected after a trial period. WinZip historically.

Commercial / enterprise

Volume, per-seat, per-CPU or subscription licences. Typically include SLAs and indemnification clauses.

SaaS

Software accessed over the internet without local installation. The licence grants access rather than ownership, and the economics shift from licence fees to recurring subscription revenue. Salesforce, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace.

Dual licensing

The same software under two licences: one open-source, one commercial. Use it freely under the open licence, or buy a commercial one to incorporate it into a proprietary product. MySQL under GPL and an Oracle commercial licence; Qt.

Creative Commons

Designed for creative works. Sometimes adapted for documentation and ancillary content, but not recommended by the FSF for code itself.

Recall the Lecture 1 distinction, which applies identically here: assignment transfers ownership; licensing grants permission and retains it. In employment and academic research contexts, licensing is generally preferred because it preserves residual rights for the creator.

Licensing · 2 of 3

Two movements, one ecosystem

The Free Software Movement

Richard Stallman, Free Software Foundation, 1983. Defines software freedom in ethical terms, through four essential freedoms:

Freedom 0 — run the program for any purpose

Freedom 1 — study how it works and adapt it to your needs

Freedom 2 — redistribute copies, so you can help others

Freedom 3 — improve it and release improvements to the public

The Open Source Initiative

Founded 1998. Defines open source by ten operational criteria in the Open Source Definition, focusing on collaborative development and business-friendly licensing rather than on ethical freedom.

The shift in vocabulary was deliberate: “open source” was chosen partly because “free software” was being misread as meaning zero-cost.

The distinction is philosophical, not technical

Free software is a matter of user liberty. Open source is a matter of development methodology.

The licence lists overlap almost entirely — but the two movements disagree about why the licences matter, and that disagreement surfaces every time a project changes licence.

Licensing · 3 of 3

Seven licences, on a spectrum from copyleft to permissive

GPL — strong copyleft

v2 (1991) and v3 (2007). Any derivative work distributed to others must also be GPL, and source must be disclosed for distributed copies. Linux kernel, GCC, WordPress.

LGPL — weak copyleft

Allows linking from proprietary code without forcing that code open. The GNU C Library and many others.

AGPL — network copyleft

Closes the SaaS loophole: software accessed over a network — not distributed — still triggers source disclosure.

MIT — permissive

Use, modify and redistribute in proprietary software, provided the copyright notice is preserved. Very short and business-friendly.

BSD — permissive

2-clause and 3-clause variants. Similar to MIT; widely used in academic and embedded systems software.

Apache 2.0

Permissive with an explicit patent grant from contributors. Requires preservation of notices and a NOTICE file. Hadoop, Kafka, Spark.

MPL 2.0

File-level copyleft. Modifications to MPL files must stay MPL, but combining with proprietary code is permitted.

Copyleft against permissive — the practical trade

Copyleft (GPL, AGPL) preserves openness downstream, ensuring improvements return to the community. Critics argue it discourages commercial adoption. Permissive (MIT, BSD, Apache) encourages adoption but allows proprietary forking. Critics call that free-riding on community labour. Hybrids (LGPL, MPL) balance the two at library or file level.

Practice

Four issues that bite real projects

A · Licence compatibility

Not all open-source licences are compatible. GPLv2 and GPLv3 are incompatible in some combinations. Code under GPL cannot be relicensed under Apache 2.0 alone.

Incompatibility is a major source of legal and engineering complication in large projects — it constrains which dependencies you may adopt at all.

B · Patent retaliation and patent peace

Apache 2.0 and GPLv3 contain patent retaliation clauses that terminate the licence if the licensee initiates patent litigation against contributors.

The mechanism is deliberate: it buys patent peace inside open communities by making litigation self-defeating.

C · Trademarks are a separate regime

Open-source licences do not grant trademark rights. Linux, Firefox and OpenOffice all rely on separate trademark policies to prevent misuse of names and logos.

You may fork the code freely and still be unable to call the result by its original name.

D · SaaS and the AGPL question

The SaaS delivery model reopened the question of what counts as “distribution” in the internet age. Running modified GPL software as a hosted service distributes nothing — so the disclosure obligation never triggers.

The AGPL was written precisely to close that gap.

Part 2 begins

Where does university research actually go?

Three reasons IPR matters inside an educational institute:

Public funding and accountability
Most academic research is publicly funded, and funders expect returns to society

Industry–academia partnership
Collaborative research requires clarity on IP before work begins, not after

The global knowledge economy
Countries compete on innovation indicators, and academic patenting is one of them

The instrument that resolves all three is a written institutional IP policy, operated by a technology transfer office. Without both, ownership of your project is decided retrospectively, by argument.

Institutions · 1 of 3

Seven components of an institutional IP policy

A governing document, typically approved by the Board of Governors or Senate, establishing the rules for creation, ownership, protection and exploitation of IP. It serves four purposes: clarity about who owns what, motivation through revenue share and recognition, compliance with funder terms, and strategy aligned to institutional mission.

1 · Scope and applicability

Which activities and persons are covered — faculty, research scholars, post-docs, students, visiting researchers, staff. Most policies apply to all persons using institutional resources.

2 · Ownership principles

Default: IP created using institutional resources belongs to the institution. Exceptions: scholarly works (textbooks, articles, lectures) usually stay the author’s; IP developed entirely outside duty hours and without institutional resources; IP governed by specific funding agreements.

3 · Invention disclosure

Mandates that all potentially patentable inventions be disclosed to the TTO before any public disclosure — including conference presentations and preprint uploads.

4 · Evaluation and patenting decision

Criteria: patentability · market potential and freedom to operate · alignment with institutional strategy · available budget.

5 · Revenue sharing

A typical model: inventors 30–50% of net royalties · department or lab 10–20% · the remainder to a central fund supporting further research and TTO operations.

6 · Conflict of interest

Requires disclosure of consulting arrangements, equity in start-ups, and external engagements that may conflict with institutional IP.

7 · Enforcement

Consequences for non-disclosure or unauthorised commercialisation.

Institutions · 2 of 3

Three models, and India's policy framework

United States — Bayh-Dole

The Bayh-Dole Act, 1980 let universities retain title to inventions arising from federally funded research. Ownership default: the university retains.

It fundamentally changed the relationship between publicly funded research and industry, and created the modern TTO — the number worldwide grew rapidly after 1980.

European Union

Varies by country. Many national laws vest ownership in the inventor or the employer according to domestic employment law, rather than automatically in the institution.

The so-called “professor’s privilege” survives in some jurisdictions and has been abolished in others.

India

The institution retains, facilitated by Section 30(4) of the Patents Act, 1970.

The National IPR Policy 2016, notified by DPIIT, promotes a culture of innovation and IP awareness in academia, strengthens the ecosystem through TTOs, IP facilitators and incubators, and encourages IP-based start-ups through schemes such as ATAL and TIDE 2.0.

Indian TTOs you should know by name

SINE — Society for Innovation and Entrepreneurship, IIT Bombay · IIT Madras Research Park and its IP Cell · IPTeL — Intellectual Property and Technology Licensing, IISc Bangalore · FITT — Foundation for Innovation and Technology Transfer, IIT Delhi · IIIT Hyderabad’s Tech Transfer Office, specialising in software and AI innovations.

In smaller institutions, TTO functions are usually consolidated within an incubation centre or research park rather than housed separately.

Institutions · 3 of 3

Eight functions of a technology transfer office

1 · Disclosure management

Receives invention disclosures from faculty, students and staff; logs them centrally.

2 · Evaluation

Conducts prior-art searches; assesses novelty, non-obviousness and industrial applicability.

3 · Patent prosecution

Decides whether to file, drafts the specification with inventors and attorneys, files at the IPO and/or under the PCT, and prosecutes through examination.

4 · Marketing and outreach

Identifies potential licensees; prepares non-confidential summaries for marketing.

5 · Licensing negotiation

Negotiates scope, exclusivity, geography, royalties, milestones and sub-licensing rights — the clauses of Lecture 2.

6 · Start-up support

Facilitates spin-off formation by inventors; negotiates the equity and licence terms.

7 · Royalty and compliance

Manages revenue flow and ensures compliance with funding-agreement obligations.

8 · Policy advisory

Advises on institutional IP policy and on funding-agreement templates.

Who staffs one

A director with both scientific and business judgement · patent attorneys or agents who draft and prosecute · licensing associates who evaluate and negotiate · business development staff who liaise with industry · technology analysts running market and prior-art searches · administration and finance for royalty accounting.

The two-step framework, assembled

Mayo (2012) supplied the structure and Alice (2014) applied it to software: (1) are the claims directed to a patent-ineligible concept? (2) if so, do the additional elements transform the claim into a patent-eligible application?

In Alice: intermediated settlement was a fundamental economic practice, and generic computer implementation was insufficient to transform it.

The Indian counterweights

Novartis applied Section 3(d) to refuse a patent for evergreening — a new form of a known substance without enhanced efficacy.

Bayer v. Natco (2012) was India’s first compulsory licence, granted under Section 84 for the cancer drug Nexavar.

The courts · 2 of 3

FRAND in the courts — what a standard patent is worth

Microsoft Corp. v. Motorola, Inc. (2012–13) — the FRAND standard

Facts: Motorola held patents declared essential to the H.264 video codec and the 802.11 Wi-Fi standard — both implemented in Microsoft’s Xbox and Windows products. Microsoft sought a declaratory judgment on the FRAND obligations Motorola owed as an SEP holder.

Issue: what terms constitute a fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory royalty for standard-essential patents?

Held (Judge Robart): a reasonable SEP royalty must be calculated by reference to the value of the patented technology’s contribution to the standard — not the overall value of the standard-compliant product. The court applied the Georgia-Pacific factors.

Significance: anchored the SSPPU approach to FRAND royalty calculation — the concept you met in Lecture 2 — and showed the commercial stakes of contributing to a standards body.

Ericsson v. Lenovo (2024)

Ericsson sued over SEPs essential to 5G, Wi-Fi and H.264, disputing the appropriate FRAND rate and whether Lenovo was entitled to a licence.

Significance: FRAND remains a contested frontier in the 5G era, and global disputes increasingly require coordinated resolution across jurisdictions.

Smith International v. Hughes Tool (1983)

The implied licence doctrine, and the enforceability of assignments executed before filing — the case you met in Lecture 1.

The courts · 3 of 3

Three cases about what copyright reaches in software

Lotus v. Borland (1996)

Facts: Lotus sued over Borland’s copying of the Lotus 1-2-3 menu command hierarchy in Quattro Pro. Borland argued the hierarchy was a method of operation, excluded under 17 U.S.C. §102(b).

Held: the First Circuit held the menu structure not copyrightable. The Supreme Court affirmed by an equally divided court — leaving the issue unresolved as binding precedent.

Functional interface elements sit at the boundary of copyright.

Apple v. Microsoft (1994)

Facts: Apple alleged the Windows GUI — overlapping windows, trash icons — infringed its copyrights. Microsoft prevailed on summary judgment; the case settled.

Significance: confirmed that GUI elements necessary to functional operation are not copyrightable, and set the stage for look-and-feel litigation through the late 1990s.

Oracle v. Google (2021)

Facts: Oracle sued over Google’s copying of the structure, sequence and organisation of the Java APIs in Android — roughly 11,500 lines of declaring code.

Held: the Supreme Court found Google’s use was fair use.

The decisive modern authority on APIs and interoperability.

Read the three together and a pattern emerges: American courts have repeatedly declined to let copyright reach the functional interface — the menu hierarchy, the GUI convention, the API declaration. That reluctance is what makes reimplementation legal, and it is the legal foundation of a great deal of the software industry.

Applied

What this means for your own work

Invention disclosure comes first

Before publication or open-source release, conduct an invention disclosure assessment. Even an academic paper establishes prior art that prevents later patenting — including your own.

The institutional policy on slide 13 requires it; the arithmetic of absolute novelty from Unit V explains why.

Claim drafting after Alice

Software claims should:

  • Recite a specific technical improvement — for example “reduced memory consumption by X percent”
  • Avoid purely functional or business-method language
  • Tie the claim to a concrete implementation rather than a generic computer

The decision sequence for a student project

1 · Copyright
You already have it, automatically, on the code you wrote.

2 · Disclose
To the TTO, before any paper, poster, demo or public commit.

3 · Choose a licence
Copyleft to keep derivatives open; permissive to maximise adoption.

4 · Consider a patent
Only if there is a demonstrable technical effect and commercial value.

Steps 2 and 3 conflict more often than students expect: a public GitHub release is a disclosure. If a patent is plausible, the release must wait for the filing.

Consolidation · end of Unit VI

What you must be able to do

Seven takeaways

  • Software is dual-layered — expressive and functional — so no single regime fits it
  • Copyright protects expression, automatically and cheaply; it cannot stop independent creation
  • A patent reaches the idea — and so reaches independent re-implementation — at high cost and uncertain scope
  • Section 3(k) and Alice both turn on a demonstrable technical effect
  • Copyleft preserves openness; permissive maximises adoption; compatibility is a real engineering constraint
  • Institutional policy plus a TTO decides ownership before the dispute, not after

  2. The idea–expression dichotomy, with a software example
