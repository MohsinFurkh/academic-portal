Scope

What the patentee may stop — and what stays free

The exclusive rights

For a product patent: to prevent others from making, using, offering for sale, selling or importing the product in India.

For a process patent: to prevent others from using the process, and from doing those same acts with a product obtained directly by the process.

Scope is defined by the claims, as read by a court. Everything from Unit V Part 2 about claim breadth becomes money here.

What the right does not reach

  • Acts done for research or experiment on the invention, including teaching
  • Use of a patented invention by or for the government in prescribed circumstances
  • Acts needed to obtain regulatory approval in certain fields
  • Anything outside India — the patent is territorial
  • Anything outside the claims, however similar it looks in a paper
Note the research exception carefully: it lets you study and improve on a patented invention in the lab. It does not let a university sell a product covered by someone else's claims.

Transfer

Four ways a patent right moves

Assignment

Ownership changes hands. The assignee becomes the patentee. Must be in writing and recorded on the register to be fully effective against others.

Irreversible: you cannot license out what you no longer own.

Licence

Permission, not ownership. The patentee promises not to sue the licensee for defined acts, in a defined field and territory, for a defined time.

The normal route for university technology.

Transmission

By operation of law — inheritance, merger, insolvency. Worth knowing because it explains why patent ownership chains sometimes look strange in databases.

Compulsory licence

Granted by the State, without the patentee's consent, on stated grounds and terms. The subject of slide 8.

All voluntary transfers should be in writing, signed, and recorded. An unrecorded assignment is a standard cause of failed due diligence when a start-up tries to raise money.

Licensing

Exclusive, sole, non-exclusive — the difference is who else may practise

TypeWho may practise the inventionTypical use
ExclusiveOnly the licensee — not even the patenteeA single company commercialising a university invention that needs heavy investment
SoleThe licensee and the patentee, nobody elseThe university keeps the right to use it for its own research or clinical service
Non-exclusiveThe licensee, the patentee, and anyone else licensedPlatform technologies, standards, software tools — maximises adoption
Cross-licenceTwo parties license each other's portfoliosWhere both sides would otherwise block each other
Sub-licenceThe licensee may license onward, if permittedMust be expressly granted — and the revenue share for sub-licences stated

Universities usually prefer non-exclusive for broadly useful tools, because the mission is diffusion — and because an exclusive licence to a company that then does nothing kills the technology.

Investors usually demand exclusive, because nobody funds a costly clinical validation that a competitor can free-ride on. The compromise is exclusivity with diligence milestones — slide 7.

Money

How the payment is actually structured

Upfront fee

Paid on signature. Covers past patent costs and signals that the licensee is serious.

Milestone payments

On defined events — prototype, regulatory clearance, first commercial sale. Aligns payment with risk being retired.

Running royalty

A percentage of net sales of the licensed product. Define “net sales” carefully — deductions are where value quietly disappears.

Minimum annual royalty

A floor payable whether or not there are sales. It is what makes shelving the technology expensive.

How rates are argued

By comparison with licences in the same sector; by the share of the product's value the invention contributes; and by how much development risk remains. An early-stage university patent with no clinical validation commands far less than a proven, regulator-cleared product — which is, again, the argument for doing the pilot.

Who gets the money

Institutional IP policies normally share net revenue between the inventors, their department and the institution, after recovering patent costs. Ratios differ — read yours, and know that student inventors are usually included.

The State's lever

Compulsory licences: when the monopoly is overridden

The grounds, in outline

After three years from grant, any interested person may apply on the grounds that:

  • the reasonable requirements of the public have not been satisfied;
  • the invention is not available at a reasonably affordable price; or
  • it is not worked in the territory of India.

There are also provisions for national emergency, extreme urgency and public non-commercial use, and for export to countries with insufficient manufacturing capacity.

The case everyone cites

Natco v. Bayer (2012) — India's first compulsory licence, over the cancer drug sorafenib (Nexavar). The Controller found the public's reasonable requirements unmet and the price not reasonably affordable, and granted a licence to a generic manufacturer against a royalty. It was upheld on appeal.

Whatever view you take of it, it is the clearest demonstration that in Indian law a patent is conditional on use, not merely on grant.

This is why the working statement exists, and why the bargain from Unit V Part 1 — disclosure for a limited monopoly — has teeth on the public's side as well as the patentee's.

How an invention gets out of the university

3

Protection

Provisional, complete, PCT — Unit V Part 4

4

Marketing

Non-confidential summary, target companies, pilots

5

Deal

Licence to an existing firm, or a spin-off company

Licence to an existing company

Faster and lower risk. They have manufacturing, regulatory experience and a sales channel. You keep doing research. Downside: your invention competes for attention inside their portfolio.

Spin-off company

Higher risk, higher involvement. Suitable when the technology is a platform, needs its founders, or no incumbent wants it. Raises questions of conflict of interest — a professor who is also a shareholder must declare it, and should not supervise students on the company's work without safeguards.

Reality check

Six reasons university patents never get licensed

Too early

A method demonstrated once, on one dataset. Nobody buys a certificate; they buy evidence it works in their setting.

No route to a product

Clinical software needs regulatory clearance, integration and support. If nobody has costed that path, the licence conversation stalls.

Broken chain of title

An intern who never signed an assignment, a collaborator whose institution also claims ownership, an unrecorded transfer.

Claims that do not cover the product

Narrowed during prosecution until a competitor can design around them in an afternoon.

Disclosure damage

Published before filing, so the rights exist only in India, or nowhere.

Nobody maintained it

Renewal fees unpaid after the student graduated, and the patent lapsed.

Five of the six are decided before the technology is offered to anyone — which is why this material belongs in a research methodology course rather than a business one.

Try it · 20 minutes

Negotiate a licence for your own invention

Team A · the university IP cell (8 min)

Prepare your position on:

  • Exclusive, sole or non-exclusive — and why
  • The field of use and territory you will grant
  • Three diligence milestones with dates
  • Your publication requirement
  • What happens to improvements made by the research group

Team B · the company (8 min)

Prepare your position on:

  • Why you need exclusivity, and what you will invest
  • Which milestones you can realistically meet
  • The publication delay you want, and why
  • What you will pay: upfront, milestones, royalty, minimums
  • What happens if regulatory clearance takes two years longer
Four minutes to negotiate. Then write the five agreed terms in one sentence each — that is a term sheet, and it is how every real deal starts.

Key takeaways

Five things about transferring patent rights

1

The scope of the right is the claims; research use, government use and anything outside India fall outside it.

2

Assignment moves ownership; a licence moves permission. Put both in writing and record them.

3

Exclusive, sole and non-exclusive differ only in who else may practise — and universities trade exclusivity for diligence milestones.

4

Compulsory licensing makes the Indian patent conditional on the invention being worked and reasonably available.

5

Most transfers fail for reasons fixed long before the deal: maturity, title, claim scope and disclosure.
