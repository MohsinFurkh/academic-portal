Foundations

Permission to use — ownership stays put

Patent licensing is the contractual act of granting permission to use a patented invention. Legally a licence is a personal privilege: it does not transfer ownership. The patentee retains title, and the licensee obtains only those rights expressly enumerated in the agreement.

Statutory basis

Patents are treated as movable property and may be licensed, assigned or mortgaged under:

Indian Patents Act, 1970 — Section 68 onwards
US Patent Act — 35 U.S.C. §261
European Patent Convention — Article 73

Six objectives licensing serves

  • Revenue through royalties and fees
  • Market expansion via the licensee’s distribution channels
  • Risk sharing where commercialisation needs heavy capital
  • Standardisation — coordinated use of patents essential to 5G, Wi-Fi
  • Defensive monetisation to deter litigation
  • Technology diffusion, especially from universities and the public sector

Why licensing dominates in computing

Software-related inventions, communication protocols and hardware designs are typically patented but rarely commercialised through in-house production alone. The value is in adoption across an ecosystem, which is exactly what licensing enables and assignment does not.

Types · 1 of 4

Exclusive — one licensee, and the licensor steps back

An exclusive licence grants the licensee the sole right to practise the patent within a defined territory and field of use, to the exclusion of all other parties — including the licensor. Ownership stays with the patentee, but the licensor is contractually barred from exercising those rights or granting further licences.

Five characteristics

  • Only one licensee holds the right
  • The licensor is excluded from commercial use within the agreed scope
  • The licensee is usually required to meet performance milestones to keep exclusivity
  • The licensee typically has the right to sue infringers in its own name
  • Most valuable to the licensee, and therefore the most expensive to obtain

Where it is used

University spin-out commercialisation · pharmaceutical licensing of a blockbuster molecule where one licensee manufactures globally · semiconductor foundries licensing process patents from a fabless design house.

In computing: common in specialised embedded systems, AI accelerators and proprietary database engines.

The characteristic risk

Shelving. An exclusive licensee who lacks incentive to commercialise broadly can simply sit on the invention — which is why performance milestones and minimum annual royalties exist, and why reversion clauses matter to a university licensor.

Types · 2 of 4

Non-exclusive — the model that makes standards work

A non-exclusive licence permits the licensee to use the patent while the licensor retains the right to grant similar licences to others and to use the patent itself. There is no limit on the number of licensees.

Four characteristics

  • Any number of licensees may hold parallel rights
  • The licensor continues to practise the invention
  • Royalty rates are lower — there is no exclusivity premium
  • Used where the patent covers an industry standard, or where the licensor wants maximum diffusion

Where it is used

Standard-essential patents licensed through patent pools — MPEG-LA, Sisvel · codecs, image formats and communication protocols — H.264/AVC, H.265/HEVC, 4G LTE · open specifications where multiple implementations must coexist.

The FRAND connection

Non-exclusive licensing is the dominant model for standard-essential patents precisely because interoperability requires that everyone can implement the standard. The FRAND commitment — fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms — is what a patent holder gives in exchange for having its technology written into the standard.

Example: IBM historically generated over $1 billion annually from patent licensing by offering non-exclusive licences across its semiconductor and software portfolio.

Types · 3 of 4

Sole — the hybrid, and the three compared

Sole licence

A hybrid. The licensor grants the licensee the right to practise, retains its own right to practise, but undertakes not to grant any further licences to third parties.

Only two parties may practise the patent: the licensor and the sole licensee. The licensee cannot exclude the licensor.

Where it is used

Joint research arrangements where one party retains background rights and grants a sole licence over foreground developments.

Manufacturing arrangements where the patent owner also produces competing goods and is unwilling to exit the market.

The distinguishing question is simply: can the licensor still practise? No for exclusive, yes for sole and non-exclusive. The second question — can the licensor license again? — separates sole from non-exclusive.

Types · 4 of 4

Four further structures you will meet in ICT

Cross-licence

Two or more parties exchange licences to their respective patents. Widespread in high technology where firms hold dense, overlapping portfolios — the smartphone “peace” agreements are the canonical example.

Compulsory licence

Imposed by the State in defined public-interest circumstances — non-working in the territory, semiconductor technology under specific national laws, or a government-use notification.

The COVID-19 vaccine landscape brought these powers to international attention.

Patent pool licence

A single licence bundling patents owned by different entities. Typical for standards-implementing technologies, and the market’s answer to negotiating with fifty patentees separately.

Sublicence

A licence granted by an existing licensee to a third party. It requires explicit authorisation in the primary licence — it is never implied.

Why pools exist at all

A modern smartphone reads on tens of thousands of patents held by dozens of parties. Negotiating bilaterally with each is impossible, and each demanding a percentage of the handset price produces royalty stacking — a combined burden exceeding what the product can bear. The pool, the aggregate royalty cap and the SSPPU concept on slide 11 are all market responses to that arithmetic.

The agreement · 1 of 2

Recitals, the grant clause, and consideration

Recitals and definitions

Establishes the parties, the background and intent, and a definitions subsection.

Definitions must be unambiguous and exhaustive — particularly “Licensed Product”, “Licensed Process”, “Territory”, “Field of Use”, “Net Sales”, “Affiliate” and “Effective Date”.

“Net Sales” deserves particular care: it is the base on which every running royalty is computed.

The grant clause — the operative provision

It must specify:

  • Which patents are licensed, by number, country and family
  • Whether the licence is exclusive, sole or non-exclusive
  • Whether sublicensing rights are granted, and on what conditions
  • The field of use — “consumer electronics but not automotive”
  • The territory — “India and Bhutan”, or “worldwide”

The drafting note that matters most

The grant clause is the most heavily litigated provision in any licence. And under customary principles of contract interpretation, ambiguity in the scope of the grant almost always favours the licensee.

If you are the licensor, every word you leave vague is a word a court will read against you.

The agreement · 2 of 2

Seven clauses that protect the licensor

Performance obligations

Minimum sales or production volumes by set dates · commercially reasonable efforts · minimum annual royalties · periodic progress reports. Failure typically triggers reversion of rights.

Improvements and derivatives

Who owns improvements: licensor ownership (licensee assigns) · licensee ownership with a licence-back · joint ownership, common in university–industry work.

Reporting and audit

Periodic royalty reports showing units sold, net revenue, royalties due and currency. The licensor retains audit rights, usually annual, to verify accuracy.

Warranties and indemnities

Warranty of title — the licensor owns the patent. Warranty of non-infringement of third-party rights — often heavily contested and frequently disclaimed.

Term and termination

Duration, renewal, termination for breach or insolvency, and post-termination rights — including sell-off periods for inventory already manufactured.

Confidentiality

Protects the know-how transferred alongside the patent, which is frequently worth more than the patent itself and is not published.

Governing law and disputes

Choice of law, forum, and whether disputes go to arbitration — the norm in cross-border technology agreements.

Read the list as a risk allocation. Performance clauses guard against shelving; audit rights against under-reporting; improvement clauses against losing the next generation of the technology to your own licensee.

Royalties · 1 of 3

Six royalty structures — and why most deals combine them

1 · Lump sum

A single fixed payment at the outset or on a milestone.

+ Certainty; no ongoing accounting
− Does not scale with success; overpayment risk if the product fails
Used in university licensing, one-time software access fees

2 · Running royalty

A fixed percentage of net sales per period.

+ Scales with revenue; aligns incentives
− Requires monitoring and audit
Typical range in software and ICT: 1–12% of net sales

3 · Per-unit royalty

A fixed amount per unit sold — USD 0.50 per device, INR 1.00 per chip.

+ Simple; easy to verify
− Misses value from price inflation or premium positioning
Used in codec licensing, semiconductor IP

4 · Milestone payments

Linked to defined events — regulatory approval, first commercial sale, sales thresholds, development stages.

Requires clear, objective milestones, or the clause becomes the dispute.

5 · Minimum annual royalty

A floor the licensee pays regardless of actual sales.

Characteristic of exclusive licences, and the principal defence against under-utilisation.

6 · Combination models

Most contemporary agreements are hybrid: a small upfront lump sum, plus milestone payments, plus a running royalty, plus a minimum annual floor.

Royalties · 2 of 3

The arithmetic, and the two problems it creates

The basic running royalty

R = S × r

R total royalty owed · S net sales of the licensed product in the period · r royalty rate as a decimal, e.g. 0.05 for five percent.

Where a minimum floor applies, the period royalty becomes Rt = max( r · St , Rmin )

Problem 1 — the royalty base

When the licensed product incorporates many technologies, on what do you charge the percentage? The whole handset, or the chip?

The answer developed by the market and the courts is the smallest saleable patent-practising unit (SSPPU) — which limits the licensor’s claim to the contribution of its own technology alone.

Problem 2 — royalty stacking

Where many licensors each demand a percentage of the total product price, the combined burden can exceed commercially sustainable levels — and the product becomes unmakeable.

Three market responses: patent pools, aggregate royalty caps, and the SSPPU base.

Royalties · 3 of 3

Three calculations you should be able to do cold

1 · Running royalty

A firm licenses a patented compression engine from a university. Non-exclusive, 4% of net sales. It sells 250,000 units at a net price of INR 800.

R = 250,000 × 800 × 0.04 = INR 8,000,000

2 · Per-unit with a floor

A chipset maker licenses error-correction technology at INR 1.20 per unit, with a minimum annual royalty of INR 5,000,000. It produces 3,500,000 units.

Computed = 3,500,000 × 1.20 = 4,200,000 Minimum = 5,000,000 Due (higher) = INR 5,000,000

3 · Combination

A robotics start-up licenses an obstacle-detection patent: INR 4,000,000 upfront; INR 2,000,000 on first commercial sale; 3% running royalty. Year 1: milestone triggered, 50,000 units at INR 25,000.

Milestone = 2,000,000 Running = 50,000 × 25,000 × 0.03 = 37,500,000 Year 1 total = INR 39,500,000
Note what example 2 teaches: the minimum floor bit — the licensee paid 800,000 more than its sales warranted. That is the floor doing exactly the job the licensor drafted it to do.

Negotiation

Ten factors that set the rate — none of them statutory

Royalty rates are negotiated outcomes, not statutory values. There is no table to look up. These ten determinants are what the negotiation is actually about.

Technology-side factors

  • Nature of the technology — foundational technologies command more
  • Stage of development — early-stage, unproven technology commands less
  • Available alternatives — the easier the design-around, the lower the rate
  • Standards-essentiality — FRAND-constrained, so capped by the commitment

Deal-side factors

  • Exclusivity — exclusive licences command higher royalties
  • Field of use — broad-field licences command higher rates
  • Market size and growth prospects
  • Risk and development cost borne by the licensee
  • Strategic significance — e.g. blocking a competitor
  • Comparable industry benchmarks

Tax treatment — a practical footnote

Royalties are treated as royalty income in the country of receipt and are subject to withholding tax at source. International licensing is affected by Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements. Whether a payment is classified as “royalty” or “business income” materially changes the tax and is frequently contested in audits — so the characterisation belongs in the agreement, not in hindsight.

Part 2 begins

Licensing is one mechanism. There are eight.

The lifecycle of an invention does not end at grant. The patent is merely the legal instrument that lets the owner control how the invention enters the market. The actual movement from laboratory to productive commercial use is technology transfer.

Technology transfer is the silent engine behind nearly every product you use: the video codec in your streaming service, the instruction set in your processor, the cryptographic primitives securing your transactions, the machine-learning libraries powering your applications.

Most originated in research environments and reached the public through deliberate transfer mechanisms — not by accident.

TT
Conveying technical knowledge, skills, processes or materials between organisations for development or commercialisation

TTA
The binding contract defining the terms of that transfer

Know-how
Unpatented, often tacit technical knowledge supporting practical application

TRL
A 9-point scale of technology maturity, from basic research to proven deployment

Technology transfer · 1 of 2

Four mechanisms that move ownership or rights

1 · Licensing — the most widely used

The owner retains title and grants permission to make, use, sell or import, in exchange for consideration. Exclusive, sole, non-exclusive or cross — as analysed in the first half of this lecture.

IBM historically earned over $1 billion a year from non-exclusive licensing of its semiconductor and software portfolio.

2 · Assignment — outright sale

Ownership transfers permanently; the original owner retains nothing. Under Section 69, assignments must be registered with the Controller to be valid against third parties — an unregistered assignment is void against a later assignee for consideration.

Common in acquisitions of startups, inheritance and restructuring, and university-to-startup transfers where the startup buys the patent outright.

3 · Joint ventures and strategic alliances

Two or more parties pool resources. Used where the technology requires complementary assets, where neither party can commercialise alone, or where risk-sharing is wanted.

Intel and Micron co-developed 3D XPoint memory through the IM Flash Technologies joint venture, with cross-licensed patents enabling the partnership.

4 · Spin-offs and start-ups

A new company founded to commercialise a specific technology, usually out of a university lab. The founding institution typically holds equity, licenses the relevant patents to the spin-off, and provides initial funding and incubation.

Google grew out of Stanford’s PageRank research; subsequent patents shaped its commercialisation.

Technology transfer · 2 of 2

Four mechanisms that transfer access or knowledge

5 · Contract and sponsored research

An external sponsor funds specific research in exchange for defined rights to the resulting IP. Terms — typically negotiated by the TTO — cover ownership of foreground IP, license-back rights to the institution, and publication rights.

The publication-rights clause is the one academics must read: a sponsor can lawfully delay your paper.

6 · Material transfer agreements

Govern the transfer of tangible materials or restricted datasets under defined conditions. Relevant in biotechnology and, increasingly, in software research where the “material” is a proprietary dataset or model. Frequently accompany patent licences.

7 · Franchising and technical assistance

Franchising transfers a complete business model — patents, trademarks and know-how together. Technical assistance agreements provide consulting and engineering support, usually combined with a licence.

8 · Open source and patent pools

Open-source licensing operates on copyright, but many projects depend on patents for technical implementation. Patent pools aggregate complementary patents for collective licensing.

Standard-essential patents for 4G LTE, 5G NR and Wi-Fi 6 are commercialised through FRAND pools. The MPEG LA pool licenses the patents essential to H.264, used across the streaming industry.

The agreement

The technology transfer agreement, clause by clause

Parties

Transferor — often a TTO, corporation or individual inventor — and transferee. For university patents the transferor is the institution, with the inventor named in a recital.

Subject matter and definitions

Precise identification of the patents, know-how and materials transferred.

Grant of rights

Scope, exclusivity, field of use, territory — as in any licence.

Consideration

Upfront payment · milestones · running royalties, commonly 2–8% in software · minimum annual royalties · sublicensing revenue share.

Improvements and foreground IP

Who owns inventions the licensee makes during the term that rely on the licensed technology: sole licensee ownership, joint ownership, or mandatory licence-back.

Sublicensing and milestones

Whether sublicensing is permitted — common in distribution-heavy industries, heavily restricted in exclusive university licences. Performance milestones with reversion on failure.

Infringement and enforcement

Who has the right and the duty to sue infringers, who bears the cost, and how recoveries are split.

Boilerplate that is not boilerplate

Warranties and indemnities · term and post-termination rights · confidentiality · dispute resolution · compliance with law, including export control.

The royalty formula recurs here in its general form: Rt = max( r · St , Rmin ) — the licensee pays the greater of the computed royalty and the contractual floor.

Commercialisation · 1 of 3

Technology readiness — when is it ready to transfer?

The TRL framework, developed by NASA and now standard at research-funding agencies including India’s DST, gives a common language for maturity.

Research — TRL 1–3

1 Basic principles observed
2 Technology concept formulated
3 Experimental proof of concept

Development — TRL 4–6

4 Validated in the laboratory
5 Validated in a relevant environment
6 Demonstrated in a relevant environment

Deployment — TRL 7–9

7 System prototype demonstration
8 System complete and qualified
9 System proven in an operational environment

The commercialisation window

Commercialisation typically begins in earnest at TRL 4–6 — when the invention is mature enough to interest a licensee, but before the institution has sunk the capital that industry is better placed to provide.

Below TRL 4 there is nothing to demonstrate; above TRL 6 the institution has usually spent money it will struggle to recover through royalties alone.

Commercialisation · 2 of 3

Sizing the market, then pricing the patent

Market assessment — four questions

  • Who is the customer? End user, OEM or integrator
  • What is the value proposition? What problem does it solve better than the alternatives
  • What is the addressable market? Quantified as TAM (total addressable), SAM (serviceable addressable) and SOM (serviceable obtainable)
  • What is the competitive landscape? Existing patents, prior art and substitute technologies

Three valuation methods

(a) Cost approach — the cost to develop a substitute. Useful for early-stage technology where revenue is uncertain.

V_cost = C_R&D + C_patent + C_legal

(b) Market approach — comparable transactions in the same technology class. Difficult in software because of rapid obsolescence.

(c) Income approach — the most rigorous: project future cash flows attributable to the patent and discount to present value.

V_income = Σ FCF_t / (1+WACC)^t − V_tangible
In practice royalties are often benchmarked with the 25% Rule: the licensee should retain roughly 75% of the operating profit attributable to the licensed technology, and the licensor takes the remaining quarter. It is a rule of thumb, not law — but it anchors many negotiations.

Commercialisation · 3 of 3

Five routes to market, ranked by capital and risk

01

Out-licensing to an established firm — low capital requirement, faster market entry. The default for a university with no manufacturing capacity.

02

Spin-off formation — higher potential return, but higher risk and capital requirement. Chosen when no incumbent will take the technology at TRL 4.

03

Cross-licensing with a partner — useful when the technologies are interdependent and neither party can ship without the other.

04

Patent assertion — aggressive, litigation-driven monetisation, characteristic of non-practising entities. Effective, and reputationally costly for an academic institution.

05

Standards contribution — embedding the patented technology into an industry standard, generating FRAND royalty streams at scale. The highest-leverage route in ICT, and the slowest.

The choice is driven by three variables: how much capital the owner can deploy, how mature the technology is, and whether an incumbent already wants it. A university at TRL 4 with no industry interest is choosing between route 2 and shelving the invention.

Consolidation

What you must be able to do by Lecture 3

Seven takeaways

  • A licence is a personal privilege — permission to use, with ownership retained
  • Exclusive, sole, non-exclusive differ on two questions: can the licensor practise, and can it license again
  • Non-exclusive + FRAND is what makes interoperability standards possible
  • The grant clause is the most litigated provision, and ambiguity favours the licensee
  • Royalty structures combine; the minimum floor is the licensor’s defence against shelving
  • SSPPU and patent pools are the market’s answer to royalty stacking

Exam focus

  1. Licence versus assignment — the ownership distinction
  2. The five-dimension comparison of exclusive, sole and non-exclusive
  3. Principal clauses of a licence agreement; why the grant clause matters most
  4. Compute a royalty under each structure, including a minimum floor
  5. Royalty stacking and SSPPU — the problem and the responses
  6. The eight transfer mechanisms, with an example of each

Practise this

A university licenses your project’s algorithm at 5% of net sales with a INR 200,000 minimum annual royalty. Year 1 sales: 3,000 units at INR 1,200. Compute the royalty due, then decide whether the licence should be exclusive and justify it in two sentences.
