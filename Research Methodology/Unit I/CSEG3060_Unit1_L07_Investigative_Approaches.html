The error this hour exists to prevent

Choosing a method because it is familiar, rather than because it answers the question.

It is the same disease as the method-first error from Lecture I.5 — but one level down, and much harder to spot, because by now you have a legitimate question. You then reach for the technique you know: train a model, run a benchmark, build an app.

The discipline: the approach is determined by the kind of claim you intend to defend. Read your hypothesis first; the method follows from it, not from your skill set.

Hook · part 1 of 2

They tested billions of cases. The bug was still there.

The Pentium FDIV bug · 1994

Intel's new Pentium used a lookup table to speed up floating-point division. A handful of entries in that table were wrong. The result: for a very small set of operand pairs, the processor returned a slightly incorrect quotient — on the order of one in billions of random divisions.

It was not found by Intel's enormous test suites. It was found by Thomas Nicely, a mathematician computing sums over twin primes, who noticed his results did not match theory.

Intel first offered replacements only to users who could prove they were affected, then reversed to a full recall, and took a charge on the order of $475 million.

“Testing shows the presence, not the absence of bugs.” — E. W. Dijkstra

Discuss · 60 seconds

Intel ran more test cases than your entire degree will ever run. Why did that not settle the question?

1:00
Because the claim was universal: “for all operand pairs, the quotient is correct.” Testing is sampling, and sampling cannot establish a claim about all inputs unless the sample covers the space. The failing inputs were a vanishingly small region — random testing was almost certain to miss them.

The fix was a change of evidence type, not more of the same evidence. After 1994 Intel invested heavily in formal verification of floating-point hardware — machine-checked proofs about all inputs, which is the analytical approach.
Hook · part 2 of 2

Now the mirror case — where a proof is worth nothing

A claim no theorem can settle

“Sorting this workload is faster with merge sort than with insertion sort on our target device.”

Asymptotically, the answer is settled and has been since the 1960s: Θ(n log n) beats Θ(n²). And every serious sorting library still switches to insertion sort below a few dozen elements.

Why? Constant factors, cache lines, branch prediction, instruction-level parallelism, allocation cost. None of these appear in the asymptotic model — and on a real machine they decide the outcome.

The crossover point is not derivable from the analysis you were taught. It has to be measured, on the machine you care about.

Four approaches at a glance

Approach Evidence is… Answers claims of the form Strength What it costs you
ANALYTICAL a proof or a derived bound “for all inputs / no algorithm can…” Universal; no dataset to argue about; does not age Needs a formalisable model. The assumptions carry the whole argument.
EXPERIMENTAL measured behaviour under controlled variation “X beats baseline B on metric M, under condition C” External validity — it worked on the real thing Bound to the artefacts and settings you measured; ages with hardware
SIMULATION behaviour of a model of the system “at scale S / under scenario Z that we cannot stage” Total control; cheap parameter sweeps; impossible scenarios Validity is inherited from the simulator. Fidelity must be demonstrated
DESIGN SCIENCE an artefact plus a rigorous evaluation of it “outcome O is achievable subject to C₁ and C₂ — at what cost?” Answers questions no existing system can answer Slides easily into “we built a thing”. The evaluation must be able to fail

Read the fourth column as promises and the fifth as prices. You do not get to pay none of them.

APPROACH 1  Analytical / theoretical

Evidence is a proof, or a bound you derived

What it looks like in computing

  • Complexity results — an Ω(n log n) lower bound for comparison sorting.
  • Impossibility results — no deterministic protocol solves consensus in an asynchronous system if even one process may fail (FLP).
  • Correctness proofs — loop invariants, refinement, model checking a protocol.
  • Guarantees — convergence of an optimiser, an approximation ratio, a privacy budget.
  • Information-theoretic limits — no scheme can compress below the entropy.

Its unique power: the conclusion covers inputs that do not exist yet, on hardware not yet built. No experiment can do that.

The characteristic failure — and it is fatal

A valid proof of an unrealistic model tells you nothing about the world.

Nobody catches you on the algebra. They catch you on the sentence before it: "assume messages arrive in order", "assume the cost model is unit-time", "assume the data is i.i.d." — when your deployment satisfies none of these.

Therefore, in a student project, one extra obligation: a short section that states each assumption and gives evidence that it holds in your setting — measured, cited, or explicitly flagged as a limitation.

Realistic scope for a B.Tech project: a derived cost model, a small correctness argument, or a bound on your own algorithm — usually as one part of a hybrid, not the whole study.

APPROACH 2  Experimental / empirical

Evidence is measured behaviour — under variation you control

The non-negotiables

  • A named baseline, tuned as carefully as your own method.
  • A held-out test set, separated before modelling and touched once.
  • Controlled variables — one thing changes at a time, or a factorial design.
  • Three seeds minimum, and report mean ± standard deviation. One run is an anecdote.
  • Effect size, not just a p-value: a significant 0.2% gain is a fact about your sample size.

This is the default approach in machine learning, systems and software engineering — and the one nearly all of you will use.

Where student experiments actually die

The weak baseline. Your method gets 200 tuning trials; the baseline gets its defaults. You have measured your own effort, not your method.
The single seed. The gap between your method and the baseline is smaller than the gap between two runs of your own method.
Leakage. Preprocessing on the full dataset, shuffling time-ordered data, near-duplicates across splits. Silent, and it invalidates everything downstream. (Lecture I.8 in detail.)

Note the shape: all three make results look better. Errors that flatter you are the ones to hunt for hardest.

APPROACH 3  Simulation-based

Evidence is the behaviour of a model — when the real thing is out of reach

Use it when the real system is…

  • Too large — a protocol at 10⁶ nodes (ns-3).
  • Not yet built — a cache hierarchy or an accelerator you are designing (gem5).
  • Too expensive or slow — a year of scheduling decisions in a data centre.
  • Too risky — failure injection in a payment system; a robot near people.
  • Impossible to stage — a region-wide power event, an epidemic, a market crash.

What it buys: total observability and cheap sweeps. You can run 10,000 configurations and see every internal variable — something no production system will ever let you do.

The rule that separates a study from a video game

Your result is only as good as the fidelity you can demonstrate.

Validation is part of the work, not an optional extra. The minimum acceptable form: take a small slice you can run for real, run it in both the real system and the simulator, and show the error between them. Then argue your conclusions survive that error.

Then a sensitivity analysis: sweep the parameters you had to guess. If your conclusion flips when the assumed loss rate moves from 1% to 2%, your conclusion is about the simulator, not the world.

State the fidelity limits in your own words, before a reviewer states them for you: “no thermal effects, no OS jitter, perfect clock synchronisation.”

APPROACH 4  Design science / constructive

Evidence is an artefact — plus an evaluation that could have failed

The five-step structure

Problem relevance→ Design→ Demonstration→ Evaluation→ Contribution

The artefact can be a system, an algorithm, a language, a framework, a method or a tool. What makes it research rather than a project is that the artefact embodies a claim, and the evaluation is designed so the claim could turn out false.

Claim form: “Outcome O is achievable subject to constraints C₁ and C₂ — and here is the cost.”

Demonstration is not evaluation

Demonstration Evaluation
“It works — here is a video.” “On 200 real cases it succeeded 91% of the time; here is where it fails.”
Shows the artefact exists Tests the claim the artefact embodies
Cannot fail Could have failed
Compared with nothing Compared with current practice, or a documented baseline

This is the single most common failure in B.Tech projects — an excellent artefact, a demo, and no evidence. Recall Lecture I.1: a project asks “does it work?”; research asks “how do you know, and compared to what?”

The honest answer to “which one should I use?”

Strong computing work is almost always a hybrid

One question, four kinds of evidence

“Can a scheduler cut p95 job latency in a shared cluster without starving long jobs?”

D Build the scheduler — the artefact embodies the claim.
A Prove the starvation bound — a guarantee no amount of measurement can give.
E Measure it against the default scheduler on a real trace, three seeds, variance reported.
S Simulate ten times your cluster size, to show the result does not depend on scale you could not test.

Each approach answers a question the others cannot. That is what a hybrid is for — not decoration, not thoroughness theatre.

The rule for choosing a second approach

Add a second approach only if you can finish this sentence: “It answers ___, which my first approach structurally cannot.”

Cannot finish it? Then the second approach is scope creep with an academic accent — and you priced that in Lecture I.6.

Realistic for one semester: one primary approach done properly, plus a small second component. A measured study with a derived cost model. A built artefact with a real evaluation. A simulation validated against a small real trace.

Two approaches done badly is worth less than one done carefully. Reviewers do not add; they take the minimum.

Four ways a study can be wrong, whatever the approach

Internal

Did the thing you changed cause the effect — or something else?

Confounds, unequal tuning, a warm cache, a different machine, an ordering effect.

External

Does it hold outside your sample?

One dataset, one device, one department, one week of traffic. Your scope statement is the honest answer here.

Construct

Did you measure the thing you named?

“Code quality” as comment density. “Usability” as macro-F1. The validity gap from Lecture I.4.

Conclusion

Do the numbers support a claim that strong?

One seed, no variance, no test, a 0.2-point difference described as “significantly outperforms”.

Step 2 · Pair challenge (6 minutes, 3 each way)

The challenger picks one threat and attacks with it — specifically, not generally:

  • A “Which assumption fails in a real deployment?”
  • E “Is your baseline tuned as hard as your method?”
  • S “What real measurement did you validate the simulator against?”
  • D “What result would have made you say the artefact failed?”

The eight lines that must survive this hour

Summary

  1. The approach follows the kind of claim, never the method you already know.
  2. All inputs → analytical. Real conditions → experimental. Unstageable scale → simulation. Buildability → design science.
  3. Testing is sampling; it cannot settle a universal claim. Pentium, 1994.
  4. A proof cannot settle a claim about a real machine. Insertion sort still wins below 30 elements.
  1. Experimental work needs a fairly tuned baseline, held-out data, ≥3 seeds and variance.
  2. A simulation inherits its validity from the simulator: validate it, then sweep it.
  3. Demonstration is not evaluation. The evaluation must be able to fail.
  4. Add a second approach only when it answers something the first structurally cannot.

Every method answers some question well. Your job is to check it is yours.
