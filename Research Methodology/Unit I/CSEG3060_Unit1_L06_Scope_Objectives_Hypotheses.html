The question this hour answers

“How will I know when I am finished?”

Every student project that runs to the last night has the same defect: nobody ever wrote down what was not included. Without that line, there is always one more feature, one more baseline, one more dataset — and the report is written in a panic at 3 a.m.

Scope is not paperwork. It is the mechanism by which a project can terminate.

Hook · part 1 of 2

Talent, money and seven years — with no line drawn anywhere

Chandler · Open Source Applications Foundation · 2002–2009

Founded by Mitch Kapor — the person behind Lotus 1-2-3, one of the most successful applications ever written. Funded generously. Staffed with senior engineers. The goal: an open-source personal information manager that would reinvent how people handle email, calendar and tasks.

The project was documented from the inside by a journalist for three years, and the book that resulted — Dreaming in Code (2007) — is largely the story of a team that could not settle what the product was not.

Version 1.0 arrived in 2008, years late and far smaller in ambition than the vision. Development wound down shortly after.

The engineers were excellent. The money was there. The vision was inspiring.

Hook · part 2 of 2

The same era. A boundary so sharp that 40,000 teams could aim at it.

The Netflix Prize · announced October 2006

  • One task: predict user ratings of films.
  • One dataset: ~100M ratings, released with the competition.
  • One metric: RMSE. One baseline: Cinematch, at 0.9525.
  • One threshold: a 10% improvement — RMSE ≤ 0.8572. Not “better”. A number.
  • One prize, one leaderboard, one public deadline.

Result: entries from tens of thousands of teams across the world, a winning solution in 2009, and a body of published knowledge on ensembles and matrix factorisation that outlived the contest.

Two honest footnotes — both are scope lessons

1 · The winning ensemble was never fully deployed. Netflix reported that the engineering cost of the full solution outweighed the benefit. The research question ("can RMSE be cut by 10%?") was answered exactly as posed — it simply was not the same question as "should we ship this?" A scope is a promise about what you will answer, not a promise that the answer will be adopted.

2 · The data had a limitation nobody declared. In 2007 researchers showed that "anonymised" ratings could be re-identified by matching against public reviews. A planned sequel was cancelled. An undeclared limitation does not stay undeclared — it is found by someone else, later, publicly.

Chandler had unlimited ambition and no boundary, and produced almost nothing. The Prize had a narrow boundary and produced a field's worth of knowledge. The boundary is not what limits the contribution — it is what makes one possible.

The distinction students merge — and examiners never do

Three different things with three different jobs

SCOPE — what is in

Chosen by you. Stated as inclusions: which population, which tasks, which datasets, which time window, which platforms.

“Three crops — tomato, potato, maize. Field-captured images, Jan–Mar 2026. Android 11+.”

Job: tells the reader what the claim will cover.

DELIMITATIONS — what is out, on purpose

Chosen by you, with a reason. The exclusions that make the project finite.

“No multi-disease co-infection — no labelled data exists. No yield prediction — outside the question.”

Job: the wall that stops your supervisor, your examiner and you at 2 a.m. from expanding the project.

LIMITATIONS — what is imposed

Not chosen by you. Constraints you must live with: sample size, hardware, data access, one institution's data, a single annotator.

“One district only; n = 30 by ethics approval; a single GPU, so no model above 3 B parameters.”

Job: declared, they protect the claim. Hidden, they destroy it at the viva.

The one-line test: Scope = “we study …” · Delimitation = “we deliberately do not study …, because …” · Limitation = “we could not …, therefore the claim holds only for …”

The hardest of the three to write

A delimitation without a reason is just a gap in the work

The two-part form — exclusion + justification

WEAK

“We will not consider multilingual inputs.”

Reads as an oversight. An examiner asks: did you decide this, or did you forget?

↓

STRONG

“We exclude multilingual inputs. No annotated corpus exists for the target dialect, and building one would consume the full project budget. The question we ask is about lighting robustness, and language does not bear on it.”

Three ingredients: what is excluded · why · why the exclusion does not damage the claim.

The 2 a.m. test

It is week nine, 2 a.m., and you think of a brilliant addition: “what if we also tried it on X?”

Open your delimitation box. If X is listed there with a reason, you are done thinking; go to sleep. If it is not listed, you will spend three days on it, and those three days come out of your writing week.

That is what the box is for. It is not for the examiner. It is a pre-commitment device you write while calm, to be obeyed while panicking.

Same logic as pre-committing your metric in Lecture I.5 — decisions made before the pressure arrives are the honest ones.

From boundary to plan

One general objective. Three to five specific ones.

General objective — the question, restated as an intention

“To measure and explain the accuracy degradation of a PlantVillage-trained classifier on field-captured images for three Uttarakhand crops.”

One sentence. One per project. If you need two, you have two projects — pick one and delimit the other away.

Specific objectives — the steps that discharge it

Three to five. If all of them are completed, the general objective is necessarily achieved. If that is not true, the set is wrong: something is missing, or something does not belong.

The worked set — carried from your I.5 example

# Specific objective Artefact it produces
O1 Assemble a field-captured evaluation set of 1,200 images across three crops Table 1 — dataset summary
O2 Reproduce the published baseline within 2 points of the reported accuracy Table 2 — reproduction check
O3 Quantify the top-1 accuracy drop from lab to field images Figure 1 — drop by crop
O4 Attribute the drop across background clutter, illumination and blur Table 3 — ablation
O5 Measure on-device latency on a sub-$80 handset Figure 2 — latency profile

One objective → one experiment → one table or figure. An objective that produces no artefact is a wish.

Click a verb.

Use these: measure · compare · quantify · characterise · evaluate · attribute · benchmark · replicate · classify. Each names an observable action with an output.

Never these: understand · explore · study · investigate · look into · gain insight into · learn. Each names a state of mind, and no examiner can check a state of mind.

Interrogative · neutral · answerable

Research questions: neutral by construction

Three properties

  • Interrogative. It ends in a question mark. If it does not, it is an objective or a claim wearing a disguise.
  • Neutral. It does not assume its own answer. “How much does our method improve accuracy?” has already decided that it does. Ask “does it, and by how much?”
  • Answerable with the evidence you can obtain. Every noun in it must be something you can measure, sample or observe this semester.

One main question, two or three sub-questions

Sub-questions are not decoration: each one should correspond to a specific objective, and therefore to an experiment.

The set, on our running example

RQ (main): How much accuracy does a PlantVillage-trained classifier lose on field-captured images of three Uttarakhand crops, and what accounts for the loss?

RQ1: What is the top-1 accuracy gap between lab and field images, per crop?
RQ2: How much of the gap is attributable to illumination, clutter and blur respectively?
RQ3: Does illumination normalisation recover a measurable share of the gap, and at what latency cost on a low-end handset?

Notice: RQ1 asks how much (descriptive), RQ2 asks why (explanatory), RQ3 asks what if (interventional). Three different kinds of evidence — which is exactly what Lecture I.7 is about.

Interactive · click to strengthen

A hypothesis is a bet you can lose

Question vs hypothesis

Question Interrogative · neutral · takes no risk
Hypothesis Declarative · specific · takes a risk, and names in advance the result that would defeat it

A directional hypothesis ("will be lower by more than 10 points") is stronger than a non-directional one ("will differ"), because it can be wrong in a specific way. The more ways a claim can fail, the more it is worth when it survives.

Where statistics enter: state H₀ (no difference) and H₁ (your directional claim), the test you will use, and the threshold — before you run anything. Writing the hypothesis after seeing results has a name, HARKing, and it is the E7 error from last class in a lab coat. Unit III returns to it.

The three rules

  • No objective without a question.
  • No method without an objective.
  • No claim without a metric.

How to test it in 60 seconds

Start at the right. Take your claim and walk backwards, saying out loud which metric supports it, which data produced the metric, which method produced the data. The first step where you have to say “well, sort of” is your broken link.

Why examiners love it

Every viva question is secretly a link in this chain. “How do you know?” is metric → claim. “Why this experiment?” is objective → method. “Would this hold elsewhere?” is data → claim.

Four ways an objective set fails

1 · Activities, not outcomes

“Study the literature.” “Learn PyTorch.” “Collect data.” These are things you do on a Tuesday, not results anyone can check. Repair: name the outcome the activity produces — “characterise the reported accuracy of five published models on X (Table 1)”.

2 · More objectives than weeks

Seven specific objectives in a twelve-week project, each needing an experiment and a write-up. Repair: keep three, and move the rest into your delimitations — where they become evidence of judgement rather than evidence of over-reach.

3 · An objective the method cannot discharge

The objective says attribute or explain, but the plan only measures an overall difference. Explanation needs an ablation or a controlled intervention. Repair: either add the ablation, or downgrade the verb to quantify — honestly.

4 · A scope with no wall

Inclusions listed, delimitations absent. The project has no state in which it is finished. Repair: write three exclusions with reasons, today, before the work starts. This is the Chandler failure, in miniature.

The eight lines that must survive this hour

Summary

  1. Scope is what you include; delimitations are what you exclude on purpose, with reasons; limitations are what is imposed on you.
  2. The delimitation box is a pre-commitment device — written while calm, obeyed while panicking.
  3. Declared limitations protect a claim. Hidden ones destroy it — publicly, and later.
  4. One general objective; three to five specific ones that together discharge it.
  1. Measurable verbs only. One objective → one experiment → one table or figure.
  2. Questions are neutral; hypotheses take a risk. Directional beats non-directional.
  3. Pre-commit the hypothesis, metric and threshold with a date, before running anything.
  4. The chain problem → question → objectives → hypotheses → method → data → metric → claim must hold at every join.
