Hook · part 1 of 3 · the reveal

Now plot them

Anscombe's quartet, 1973. Same means, same variances, same correlation, same fitted line — four completely different phenomena.

A: a genuine linear relation. B: a curve that no linear model should be fitted to. C: a perfect line plus one outlier. D: one point creating the entire correlation — remove it and there is no relationship at all.

The discipline this buys you

Look at your data before you model it. Distributions, not just means. Plots, not just tables.

Most defects in a student dataset — a broken sensor, a duplicated export, a class that is 97% of the data, a timestamp parsed as a string — are visible in a histogram in the first ten minutes. They are invisible in a table of means for four months.

Hook · parts 2 and 3

Two thousand COVID-19 models. None fit for clinical use.

Roberts et al., Nature Machine Intelligence, 2021

A systematic review of machine-learning models built during 2020 to detect or prognosticate COVID-19 from chest X-rays and CT scans. Thousands of papers screened; dozens analysed in depth. The conclusion: none of the models reviewed was of potential clinical use, because of methodological flaws and biases.

Two failures they document repeatedly, both about data, not models:

  • Frankenstein datasets — public datasets assembled from other public datasets, so the "same" images appear in training and test under different names.
  • Wrong control group — using a paediatric chest X-ray set as the "healthy" class against adult COVID patients. The model separates children from adults, and reports it as diagnosis.

And the confound that looks exactly like success

Zech et al. (2018) showed a pneumonia detector performing excellently within a hospital and degrading across hospitals — because it had partly learned to detect which scanner and department produced the image. Sicker patients were imaged with portable scanners; the marker was in the image.

None of these teams were dishonest. They were competent people whose results looked excellent — which is precisely the problem. Errors that flatter you do not feel like errors.

Everything in the rest of this hour exists to catch this class of failure before the number appears, not after.

Where the evidence comes from

Primary and secondary data in computing

Primary — you generate it

  • Benchmark runs and instrumented executions
  • System logs and traces you collect yourself
  • User studies, task observations, interviews
  • Surveys and questionnaires
  • Expert annotation of a corpus you assembled

Cost: time, ethics approval, participants, and your own labour. Benefit: it is measured exactly for your question, and nobody else has it.

Secondary — it already exists

  • Public datasets and benchmark suites
  • Repository mining — GitHub, Stack Overflow, package registries
  • Public traces and archives; administrative data
  • Published results tables (for meta-analysis or replication)

Cost: it was collected for someone else's question — so its population, labels and definitions are theirs, not yours. Read the datasheet before the download finishes.

Ask of every secondary source: who collected it, when, for what purpose, under which licence, and what is missing from it?

The word students skip, and examiners circle

Four sampling methods — and the one you are actually using

Method What it means Typical computing use What it costs you
Random Every unit has an equal chance of selection Sampling 5,000 repositories from a registry Needs a real sampling frame — a list of everything
Stratified Random within groups you care about Equal numbers per language, per crop, per device class Needs the strata defined in advance, and justified
Purposive Deliberately chosen cases that fit a criterion The ten most-starred projects; three failure cases Not generalisable by design — say so, and say why it is right anyway
Convenience Whoever or whatever was reachable Your classmates. Your own laptop. The first dataset on Kaggle. Unknown bias in an unknown direction

Most student projects use convenience sampling. That is acceptable. Hiding it is not.

Declare it in your limitations, and state which direction the bias probably runs: “our 30 participants were CSE students, who are more comfortable with command-line tools than the target population, so our completion times are likely optimistic.”

Sample size, honestly

You will not get n = 1,000. You can still be rigorous: report the n you have, report variance, avoid claims your n cannot support, and prefer within-subject designs (each participant does both conditions) which need far fewer people to detect the same effect.

Before the first row of data exists

Four things that must be fixed in writing, in advance

1 · The metric and the threshold

Decided in Lecture I.4, written in your notebook with a date. Choosing the metric after seeing results is metric shopping — the E7 error.

2 · The test split

Separated before any modelling and touched once. Not once per week. Once.

3 · Provenance

For every source: where it came from, when, under what licence, and with what consent. Recorded as you go — reconstructing it in December is impossible.

4 · The written procedure

Enough detail that a stranger could repeat the collection and get a comparable dataset. If it lives only in your head, it is not a method.

Notice what these four have in common: each is a pre-commitment that removes a decision you would otherwise make after seeing results — which is exactly when your judgement is least trustworthy, and least defensible.

The loudest error in empirical computing

Data leakage: when the answer is already in the question

The four flavours

  1. Preprocessing leakage. Scaling, imputing, feature selection or vocabulary building on the full dataset before splitting. The test set has already influenced training.
  2. Temporal leakage. Shuffling time-ordered data, so the model trains on the future and predicts the past. Fatal for logs, prices, traffic, health records.
  3. Group leakage. Near-duplicates or the same entity — patient, user, repository, document — appearing on both sides of the split.
  4. Test-set tuning. Choosing epochs, thresholds or hyperparameters by test performance. Your test set is now a validation set, and you have no test set.

Why it deserves its own slide

Leakage is silent, always flattering, and invalidates everything downstream.

There is no error message. Your accuracy goes up. Your supervisor is pleased. The defect surfaces only when someone else runs the system on genuinely new data — often after you have graduated, and always in public.

The habit that prevents it: split first, then do everything else — and write the split to disk with a fixed seed, so it is a file, not a decision you repeat.

Analysis · step one, always

Describe before you model — the ten-minute audit

What to look at, in this order

  1. Shape — how many rows, columns, classes, groups?
  2. Distributions — plot every numeric column. Skew, bimodality, impossible values.
  3. Missingness — how much, and is it missing at random? Missingness is often the most informative column in the dataset.
  4. Class balance — a 97 / 3 split means 97% accuracy is the do-nothing baseline.
  5. Duplicates and near-duplicates — the leakage check, done with your own eyes.
  6. Outliers — real phenomenon, or broken sensor? You cannot tell from the number alone; go and look at the record.
  7. Units and types — timestamps parsed as strings, latencies in mixed ms and µs.

Why this is not optional bookkeeping

Every hour spent here saves a week later — and it is the only stage where problems are cheap to fix.

Anscombe's point, applied: a mean is a summary, and a summary is a lossy compression of your data. You decide what to throw away when you choose the summary. Look at the raw thing first, so you know what you are compressing.

Report it too. A dataset description table — n, classes, balance, missingness, source, licence — is Table 1 of your report, and it is the table reviewers read most carefully.

A rough two-sample screen, not a substitute for the right test on your data. Its purpose is to build the instinct: compare the gap to the noise, always.

What to report, every time

  • ≥ 3 seeds, and mean ± standard deviation. One run is an anecdote.
  • Effect size, not only a p-value. A significant 0.2% gain is a fact about your sample size, not about your method.
  • A confidence interval, which says more than a yes/no test.
  • The test you used and its assumptions — and whether you checked them.
  • A correction when you compare many configurations. Twenty comparisons at p < 0.05 will hand you a "significant" result by construction.

Language discipline: “outperforms” needs evidence of a gap; “significantly outperforms” needs a test; “substantially outperforms” needs an effect size. Match the adverb to the arithmetic.

Not everything worth knowing is a number

Qualitative data is analysed, not merely quoted

Where it appears in computing research

  • Interviews with developers, users, administrators
  • Open-ended survey responses
  • Code review comments, issue threads, commit messages
  • Think-aloud transcripts from usability sessions
  • Failure reports and post-mortems

The amateur move is to pick three quotes that support your point. That is illustration, not analysis — and a reviewer sees it instantly.

The disciplined version

  1. Coding. Assign labels systematically to every unit, not only the interesting ones.
  2. Codebook. Written definitions, so a second person can apply the same labels.
  3. Two coders on a subset, and report inter-rater agreement — Cohen's κ. Raw percentage agreement flatters you, because chance agreement is high.
  4. Themes built from codes, with counts: “7 of 12 participants mentioned X”.
  5. Disconfirming evidence reported — the cases that did not fit your theme.

Where most of the damage is done

Interpretation: four disciplines

1 · Answer the question you asked

Not the one your results happen to support. If your data answers a different question, that is a finding — report it as a different question, and say the original one remains open.

2 · Correlation is not causation

Only a controlled intervention licenses a causal verb. Without one, use “is associated with” and mean it. This single word choice is the most common place a careful study becomes an overclaim.

3 · State your limitations first

Tie each to the scope and delimitations you wrote in Lecture I.6. A limitation you declare is a boundary on your claim; the same limitation found by a reviewer is a hole in it.

4 · Close the so-what chain

What should a reader now do differently — and what evidence would change their mind back? An interpretation that ends at “this shows our method is good” has not started.

Negative and null results are results. “Method X did not beat the baseline on Indic code-mixed data, and here is the analysis of why” is publishable, useful, and honest. A well-run experiment that refutes your hypothesis has done its job — the hypothesis was supposed to be able to lose. Suppressing it is the same failure as metric shopping.

Swap · the only review criterion (4 minutes)

“Could I execute this plan on Monday morning?”

Not “is it impressive”. Not “is it interesting”. The reviewer marks any line they could not act on without asking a question — and every such line is a defect the author must fix tonight.

Three questions that find most defects: Where exactly does the data live? What happens if access is refused? What is your do-nothing baseline number?
