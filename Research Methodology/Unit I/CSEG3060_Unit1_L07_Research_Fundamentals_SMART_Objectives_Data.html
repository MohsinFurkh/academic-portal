Hook · same topic, three different things

Only one of these is research

1  “AI coding assistants help students write code faster.”

An opinion. Nobody counted anything. It cannot be wrong, because nothing was claimed precisely enough to check.

2  “In a survey of 400 UPES students, 78% report using an AI assistant at least weekly.”

A fact about a sample. True, useful, checkable — and it explains nothing. This is descriptive work, and it is a legitimate first step.

3  “Students using an assistant finished the debugging tasks 22% faster (n = 120, 3 tasks, counterbalanced) but left 1.6× more silent defects; the speed gain did not survive when the defect-repair time was added back.”

A research claim: population, comparison, measure, magnitude, and a result that could have come out the other way.

Three sentences about one topic. The difference is not the subject and not the technology — it is how the sentence was produced and what could refute it.

Part A · Fundamentals · definition

Defining research

How the standard sources put it

  • Redman & Mory — research is a “systematized effort to gain new knowledge”.
  • Kothari — a scientific and systematic search for information on a defined topic; movement from the known to the unknown.
  • OECD (Frascati Manual) — creative and systematic work undertaken to increase the stock of knowledge and to devise new applications of it.

Notice the word every definition contains: systematic. Not clever, not novel, not difficult — systematic.

The working definition for this course

Research is a systematic, documented investigation that produces a claim about the world which someone else could check — and which could turn out to be wrong.

This is the same falsifiability test from Lecture I.1. Sentence 1 on the previous slide fails it; sentence 3 passes it.

Six features that make an investigation research

1 · Systematic — a stated procedure, not a wander.

2 · Empirical or logical — settled by data or by proof, never by authority.

3 · Bias-controlled — the design tries to prove you wrong.

4 · Replicable — a stranger with your write-up can repeat it.

5 · Cumulative — it stands on named prior work.

6 · Communicable — unpublished research is not yet research.

Part A · why research is done

The objectives of research

Two different questions hide under the word objective. Keep them apart.

Objectives of research (as an activity)

Kothari's four classical groups — a study usually sits mainly in one:

  1. Exploratory / formulative — gain familiarity, achieve new insight into a phenomenon you do not yet understand.
  2. Descriptive — portray accurately the characteristics of a group, situation or system.
  3. Diagnostic — determine how often something occurs, or what it is associated with.
  4. Hypothesis-testing — test a stated causal relationship between variables.

In four verbs, research exists to describe, explain, predict and design — computing does a great deal of the fourth.

Your research objectives (as a promise)

The three to five sentences in your proposal that say what this study will produce. They are:

  • a contract with your supervisor and examiner;
  • the table of contents of your results chapter;
  • the thing you are marked against — not your effort, not your code.

Part B of this hour is entirely about writing these so they can be ticked off.

The link between the two: the kind of objective you have (explore / describe / diagnose / test) decides the type of research you must run — which is the next slide.

Part A · types of research

Five types you are expected to name and tell apart

Exploratory

“What is going on here?”

No hypothesis yet. Small, flexible, mostly qualitative. Output = sharper questions, categories, a hypothesis worth testing.

CS: 12 interviews with clinic staff before you design a triage tool.

Descriptive

“What is the case?”

Measures and characterises without manipulating anything. Surveys, measurement studies, repository mining.

CS: mining 50,000 GitHub repositories to report how often CI is left red, and for how long.

Analytical

“What does it mean?”

Takes facts or data already available and analyses them critically to find relationships and evaluate claims.

CS: re-analysing those same repositories to test whether long red-CI spells predict defect density, controlling for team size.

Theoretical

“What must be true?”

Abstract and formal. Proofs, bounds, models, frameworks. No data collection at all; the warrant is the argument.

CS: proving no deterministic protocol achieves consensus under asynchrony with one crash fault.

Empirical

“What actually happens?”

Data-based and experience-based. Ideally you manipulate something, control the rest, and repeat.

CS: two schedulers, same workload, 3 seeds, reported as mean ± SD with an effect size.

Exploratory work finds the question. Descriptive work measures. Analytical work relates. Theoretical work proves. Empirical work tests.

Part A · the comparison you will be examined on

Side by side

Type Question Evidence Typical output Where it goes wrong
Exploratory What is going on, and what should I even ask? Interviews, field notes, pilot probes, open-ended responses Categories, themes, a testable hypothesis Reported as if it proved something; n = 8 becomes “users prefer…”
Descriptive What is the case, and how much of it? Surveys, censuses, measurement studies, mined repositories Distributions, rates, characterisations, a public dataset Slides into causal language: “because”, “leads to”, “improves”
Analytical What relationships hold in data I already have? Existing data + statistical or critical analysis Associations, models, a critical evaluation of prior claims Correlation read as causation; confounders never named
Theoretical What must hold for every input, always? Definitions, axioms, derivation, proof Theorems, bounds, impossibility results, formal models A valid proof of an assumption that no real system satisfies
Empirical What happens when I change this and hold the rest fixed? Controlled experiments, benchmarks, A/B tests, user studies Comparative results with variance and effect size Weak baseline, one seed, tuned on the test set

Two more pairs you will meet in the literature and should be able to place: basic vs applied (is the goal knowledge or a use?) and qualitative vs quantitative (is the evidence meaning or magnitude?). Neither is a sixth type — they are different axes cutting across the five above.

Part A · the point most students miss

Real projects use four of the five

They are not mutually exclusive

  • Exploratory is a stage — it comes before the others, whenever you do not yet know what to ask.
  • Descriptive vs analytical is about what you do with data: report it, or relate variables in it.
  • Theoretical vs empirical is about where the warrant comes from: argument, or observation.

So “is this descriptive or empirical?” is often the wrong question. A measurement study is both. Ask instead: what would make this claim wrong?

One project, four types

“Does an AI assistant help first-year students debug?”

  • Exploratory — sit with 8 students, watch them debug, find out what “stuck” looks like.
  • Descriptive — survey 400: who uses what, how often, for which tasks.
  • Empirical — 120 students, 3 counterbalanced tasks, assistant on/off, measure time and defects.
  • Analytical — re-analyse the logs: does prior CGPA moderate the effect?
  • Theoretical — (not used here; it would be a formal model of the debugging search)

The type follows the question, and the question changes as the project moves.

Part B · setting objectives

Aim, general objective, specific objectives

Aim (1 sentence)

The direction of travel, in plain language. Not measurable, and that is allowed.

“To make automated crop-disease diagnosis usable on real farms in Uttarakhand.”

General objective (1)

The research question restated as an intention. Bounded, but still one sentence.

“To measure and explain the accuracy degradation of a PlantVillage-trained classifier on field-captured images for three Uttarakhand crops.”

Specific objectives (3–5)

The steps that, if all completed, discharge the general objective. Each one is SMART.

assemble · reproduce · quantify · attribute · measure

The test that catches almost every weak objective set: if I completed all your specific objectives and nothing else, would your general objective be answered?

If the answer is “no, you would also need to…”, that missing thing is an objective you forgot. If the answer is “yes, and more besides”, you have an objective that belongs to a different project.

Part B · the SMART criteria

SMART — and the test that decides each letter

S

Specific

Names the population, task, baseline and setting. No umbrella nouns.

Test: two strangers reading it design the same study.

M

Measurable

Names the metric and the threshold — not just “accuracy” but “top-1, ≥ baseline + 5 pp”.

Test: you can state, today, the number that would count as failure.

A

Achievable

Fits your data, compute, skill, access and calendar — which multiply; any zero is fatal.

Test: a crude end-to-end version exists within one week.

R

Relevant

Serves this research question, and someone would act differently on the answer.

Test: the “so what” chain ends at a named person or group.

T

Time-bound

Carries a date or week number from your work plan, not “by the end”.

Test: it appears as a bar on your Gantt chart with a finish week.

One worked objective, all five letters visible: “Quantify the top-1 accuracy drop (M) of the ResNet-50 PlantVillage baseline (S) on 900 field-captured images of tomato, potato and maize collected in Dehradun (S), reporting mean ± SD over 3 seeds against the published 99.2% figure (M), using the annotation set already collected (A), to establish whether the deployment gap reported by extension officers is real (R), by week 7 (T).”

Part B · repair clinic

Four objectives from real student proposals

As written What fails Repaired
“To study deep learning techniques for medical image analysis.” S, M, T — a syllabus, not an objective; no observable at all. “To compare U-Net, nnU-Net and SAM-Med2D on the BUSI breast-ultrasound set, reporting Dice and HD95 over 5 folds, by week 6.”
“To improve the accuracy of our classifier.” M — improve from what, to what? No baseline and no threshold, so no result can fail. “To raise macro-F1 on the held-out Hinglish test split from the 0.71 BERT baseline to ≥ 0.78, over 3 seeds, by week 9.”
“To develop a fully autonomous campus delivery robot.” A — hardware, permissions, safety approval and six person-months you do not have. “To evaluate three path-planning policies in a Gazebo model of the campus loop on 50 scripted pedestrian scenarios, reporting collisions per km, by week 8.”
“To count the weekly commits in our project repository.” R — perfectly specific, measurable, achievable and dated, and it answers nothing. “To test whether weekly commit frequency predicts post-release defect count across 200 repositories, controlling for team size, by week 10.”

The fourth row is the one to remember: SMART is a filter, not a generator. A worthless objective can pass all five letters. Relevance is the letter students score themselves highest on and reviewers score them lowest on.

Part B · the mechanics

Every objective owes you an artefact

Verbs decide whether it can be checked

Use Avoid
measure · quantify · compare · characterise · evaluate · attribute · implement · validate · replicate · derive understand · study · explore · look into · investigate · analyse (when unqualified) · gain insight into · work on

The right-hand column names states of mind. You cannot show an examiner that you have understood something; you can show them a table.

The artefact rule. Each specific objective maps to one experiment, one table or figure, and one subsection of your report. An objective that produces no artefact is a wish.

Two traps SMART does not catch

1 · Measurable but meaningless. The metric drifts from the construct you actually care about — you promise “95% accuracy” on a set where the majority class is 94%. Passes M; means nothing. (The validity gap, Lecture I.4.)

2 · Achievable but trivial. You shrink until the objective is certain to succeed. If no possible result would surprise anyone, you have written a task, not a research objective. (Lecture I.2's task/project/research distinction.)

Calibration: three to five specific objectives for a semester. More objectives than working weeks is the single most common failure in Assignment 2 — and the one supervisors spot in ten seconds.

Part C · data fundamentals

Primary vs secondary data

Primary — you generate it, for this question

  • Benchmark runs and instrumented systems you built
  • Logs and telemetry you chose to collect
  • Questionnaires, interviews, think-aloud sessions
  • Observations, expert annotation, labelling you commissioned

Buys you: exact fit to your objective, known provenance, control over quality, and a contribution in its own right.
Costs you: time, money, ethics approval, recruitment, and the risk that the whole semester depends on people replying to you.

Secondary — it already exists, collected for someone else

  • Public datasets and benchmarks (ImageNet, GLUE, PROMISE, MIMIC)
  • Repository and forum mining (GitHub, Stack Overflow, npm)
  • Government and administrative data, NPCI/UPI statistics, census
  • Results tables in published papers; your organisation's existing logs

Buys you: scale, speed, low cost, history you could never collect yourself, and comparability with prior work.
Costs you: a fit gap, unknown collection bias, licence limits, stale definitions, and no way to add the variable you wish someone had recorded.

Always search secondary first. The cheapest primary study is the one you did not have to run — and the reviewer will ask why you collected data that already exists. Then ask the two questions that decide whether you may use it: what was it collected for, and who is missing from it? Plus the licence, always.

Part C · the second axis

Qualitative vs quantitative — and the 2 × 2 they make

Qualitative — meaning

Words, behaviour, artefacts. Answers why and how. Small purposive samples, flexible instruments, analysis by coding and themes, agreement reported with a statistic such as Cohen's κ. Generalises to theory, not to a population.

Quantitative — magnitude

Numbers on a defined scale. Answers how much, how often, is the difference real. Larger samples, fixed instruments, analysis by descriptive then inferential statistics, reported with variance and effect size.

Quantitative Qualitative
Primary Your own latency traces; a 400-response Likert survey; timings from a controlled task study Your interviews with clinic staff; think-aloud transcripts; structured observation field notes
Secondary ImageNet; NASA/PROMISE defect data; monthly UPI transaction volumes; a Stack Overflow dump Existing issue-tracker discussions; published incident post-mortems; policy documents; archived interview transcripts

Mixed methods is the normal case, not a compromise: qualitative work tells you what to measure; quantitative work tells you whether it holds.

Part C · collection tool 1 of 3

Questionnaires

Use when you need self-reported facts, opinions or experiences from more people than you could ever sit with.

Writing the items

  • One construct per item. “Is the app fast and easy?” is two questions and cannot be answered.
  • Closed items to measure; two or three open items to catch what you failed to anticipate.
  • 5- or 7-point Likert with every anchor labelled, not just the ends.
  • No leading wording (“How much did you enjoy…”), no jargon, no double negatives.
  • Reverse-code a few items to catch straight-lining.
  • Demographics last; keep the whole thing under 10 minutes.

Sampling and running it

  • State the population, the sampling frame and the method — random, stratified, purposive or convenience.
  • Convenience sampling is what most student projects use. Declare it as a limitation; never hide it.
  • Report the response rate. Non-response bias is the default failure mode: the people who reply are not the people who did not.
  • Pilot with 5–10 people first. Time it, and rewrite every item they misread.

Before you press send

  • Every item maps to an objective. If an item maps to nothing, delete it.
  • You have written down how each construct will be scored — before seeing any data.
  • Multi-item scales: plan to report Cronbach's α.
  • Consent statement, purpose, anonymity vs confidentiality, right to withdraw, contact address.
  • You collect no identifier you do not need.
Part C · collection tool 2 of 3

Observation logs

Use when what people do differs from what they say they do — which is almost always.

Kinds

  • Structured — a fixed coding scheme, producing counts and durations. Quantitative output.
  • Unstructured — open field notes when you do not yet know what matters. Exploratory output.
  • Participant vs non-participant — are you inside the workflow or outside it?
  • Think-aloud — the standard instrument for interfaces: the user narrates while working.

Guidelines

  • Write the coding scheme before you observe, with an inclusion and an exclusion example for every code.
  • Keep observation and interpretation in two separate columns. “Paused 14 s at the OTP field” is data; “was confused” is not.
  • Time-stamp events; log verbatim quotes, not paraphrases.
  • Two observers on at least 20% of sessions, and report inter-rater agreement (Cohen's κ).
  • Observe until saturation — new sessions stop producing new codes — and report how many that took.
  • Name the observer effect and what you did about it.

A log line that works

ts | participant | task | code | verbatim | observer note

Example
00:04:12 | P07 | book-lab | BACKTRACK | “wait, which slot did I pick?” | returned to step 2, third time

Six columns, one line per event. A log you cannot analyse mechanically is a diary — and a diary is not data.

Part C · collection tool 3 of 3

System monitoring & instrumentation

Computing's own instrument: the apparatus that produces your numbers. In this field it replaces the psychologist's questionnaire as the default tool.

What it covers

  • Application logs and event streams
  • Metrics and telemetry (counters, gauges, histograms)
  • Distributed traces and profilers
  • Benchmark suites and measurement harnesses
  • Hardware counters, energy meters, network captures

Guidelines

  • Fix the metric and the aggregate before collecting — p50 / p95 / p99, not just the mean.
  • Use a monotonic clock; one clock source across machines.
  • Sample rather than log everything — then measure the probe overhead and report it.
  • Discard warm-up runs, and say how many you discarded.
  • Record versions, seeds, hardware and OS with every result.
  • Keep raw per-run data, not only averages. You cannot recompute a distribution from a mean.

Threats specific to this tool

  • Probe overhead — the instrument became part of the system under test.
  • Thermal throttling — everything measured late looks worse.
  • Cold caches — run 1 is not run 10.
  • Log loss under load — you stop recording exactly when it matters.
  • Survivorship — you only logged the requests that finished.

Privacy: a log becomes personal data the moment it holds a user id, an IP or content. Minimise fields, pseudonymise, set a retention period — and put all three in your ethics section.

4:00
Consolidation

Ten things that must survive this hour

  1. Research is a systematic, documented investigation producing a claim that could be wrong.
  2. Research exists to describe, explain, predict and design — computing does a lot of the last one.
  3. The five types answer five different questions: find · measure · relate · prove · test.
  4. Types are axes, not boxes. Most real projects use three or four of them, in stages.
  5. One aim, one general objective, three to five specific objectives — and they must discharge the general one exactly.
  1. SMART = Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — each with a test you can apply in ten seconds.
  2. SMART is a filter, not a generator: a worthless objective can pass all five letters. R is where students over-score themselves.
  3. Measurable verbs only, and every objective owes you one artefact.
  4. Search secondary data first; for anything you reuse, ask what it was collected for and who is missing from it.
  5. The instrument follows the question — questionnaire for self-report, observation log for behaviour, system monitoring for machine truth.

If you remember one sentence: an objective nobody can tick off, and data you cannot actually obtain, are the two ways a good research question still dies.
