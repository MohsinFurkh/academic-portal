Hook · part 1 of 2

Your measurement apparatus has opinions

Mytkowicz, Diwan, Hauswirth & Sweeney · ASPLOS 2009

The paper's title is the finding: “Producing Wrong Data Without Doing Anything Obviously Wrong!”

They showed that measured performance in C benchmarks shifts measurably when you change things that have nothing to do with the program:

  • The size of your UNIX environment variables — which changes stack alignment.
  • The link order of your object files — which changes code and data layout, and therefore cache behaviour.

The bias was large enough that a comparison between compiler optimisation levels could come out either way, depending on an environment variable nobody thought to record.

Discuss · 60 seconds

You ran the benchmark correctly. The code was right. The statistics were right. The conclusion was still wrong.

Where, in the chain from Lecture I.6, does this failure live — and which validity does it threaten?

1:00
Between method and data — the instrument. It is an internal validity threat: something other than the thing you changed caused the difference you measured. And note it is invisible in your code, your notebook and your plots. Only the protocol catches it — randomise the setup, repeat, report spread.
Hook · part 2 of 2

A famous result, and a graduate student who asked for the file

Growth in a Time of Debt · 2010 → 2013

A widely cited economics paper reported that national growth falls sharply once public debt passes about 90% of GDP. The finding was quoted in policy debates across several countries.

In 2013 a graduate student, Thomas Herndon, tried to reproduce it as a course assignment and could not. He requested the original spreadsheet. With it, he and his co-authors identified a spreadsheet range that omitted several countries, along with selective exclusions and an unusual weighting — and showed that correcting them materially changed the headline number.

Nobody could see any of this for three years, because nobody had the file.

Two lessons, and they are both about instruments

1 · Your tools are part of your method. A spreadsheet range is as much an instrument as a profiler or a benchmark harness — and it can be wrong in ways no reader can detect from the printed table.

2 · The working standard for "done" is not "it ran on my machine". It is someone else can reproduce it — which, from Lecture I.1, is exactly the row that separates a research result from a project.

And the reproducer here was a student on a course assignment. That is a genuinely available research contribution — the replication source from Lecture I.2.

The syllabus phrase, translated

“Necessary instrumentation” — what that means for us

In the social sciences an instrument is a questionnaire or a scale. In computing it is the entire apparatus that produces your numbers:

  • Benchmark suites and harnesses — the workload and the driver
  • Profilers, timers and counters — including which clock you called
  • Logging and tracing — and the overhead they add
  • Measurement rigs — latency, memory, energy (a shunt, a power meter)
  • Annotation tools and guidelines — for labelled data
  • Survey and interview instruments — where humans are involved
  • The software stack itself — libraries, versions, compiler flags, seeds, container image, OS, hardware

The claim students find hardest to accept

Your software stack is part of your instrument.

A different BLAS build, a different CUDA version, a different Python minor release, a different random seed, a laptop on battery instead of mains — each can move your numbers by more than the effect you are claiming.

Which is why "we used PyTorch" is not a method section, and torch==2.3.1+cu121, pinned and reported, is the beginning of one.

Test the instrument before you trust the reading

Three questions to ask of any instrument

Reliable?

Does measuring the same thing twice give the same answer?

Measure your run-to-run variance first, before comparing anything. If repeated runs of the same configuration differ by 3%, then a 2% "improvement" is not a result — it is your noise floor.

Valid?

Does it measure the construct you named?

Wall-clock time on a shared server measures the server's other users too. Lines of code measures typing. Construct validity, from Lecture I.7 — now applied to the apparatus rather than the study.

Unbiased?

Does the act of measuring change the reading?

Instrumentation overhead, warm-up and JIT effects, cache state, thermal throttling, background processes — and, with people, the fact that they know they are being watched.

The line to remember: a benchmark that is faster on the second run is telling you about the cache, not about your algorithm.

The protocol that survives review

How to measure something, properly

The protocol

  1. Warm up, and discard the warm-up runs — saying how many you discarded and why.
  2. Repeat: at least 10 measurement runs for timing, at least 3 seeds for anything stochastic.
  3. Randomise or counterbalance the order of configurations, so drift and heat do not align with one arm.
  4. Quiet the machine: no other load, fixed CPU governor, mains power, cooling between runs.
  5. Report median and spread (IQR or SD), never a single best number.
  6. Record the environment: CPU, GPU, RAM, OS, kernel, driver, library versions, compiler flags, container digest.

The two habits that separate careful from careless

1 · Measure your noise floor first.

Run the identical configuration ten times and look at the spread. That number is the smallest difference you are entitled to talk about — write it in your notebook before you run any comparison.

2 · Report the best number nobody asked for.

The variance, the failed runs, the outlier you cannot explain. A methods section that mentions its own noise reads as competent; one that reports only wins reads as marketing — and reviewers respond accordingly.

The working definition of reproducible: a stranger with your repository, your README and no access to you can regenerate every table and figure in your report with one command — and get the same numbers, or a documented reason why not.

This is also the cheapest insurance you will ever buy against your own laptop dying in week eleven.

Annotation, surveys and studies with people

When the instrument is a person

Annotation is an instrument — calibrate it

  • A written annotation guideline with definitions and edge cases, produced before labelling begins.
  • Two annotators on an overlapping subset, with Cohen's κ reported. Low agreement is not a nuisance — it is evidence that your construct is not yet defined.
  • A documented adjudication rule for disagreements.
  • Labels are data with provenance: who labelled what, when, under which guideline version.

Survey and study design — four quick killers

  • Leading questions. “How much did the new interface improve your experience?” has decided the answer.
  • Double-barrelled items. “Was the tool fast and easy to use?” cannot be answered.
  • Misused Likert data. Ordinal responses averaged as if they were metres.
  • Missing consent and anonymity. Participation information, the right to withdraw, and no identifiers you do not need.

Unit II designs these properly; Unit III takes the ethics further.

A method, not an anecdote

Case study: an in-depth investigation in real context

Use it when…

…the boundary between the phenomenon and its context is exactly what is interesting, and a controlled experiment is impossible.

  • One organisation's migration to microservices
  • One team's adoption of a new tool, over six months
  • One deployment's failure, and how it was diagnosed
  • One open-source project's governance change

You cannot randomise companies. You can study one deeply, and honestly.

What makes it rigorous rather than a story

  1. An explicit case-selection rationale. Why this case? Typical, extreme, critical, or simply available — say which.
  2. Multiple sources of evidence. Interviews and repository data and documents and incident logs.
  3. Triangulation. Where sources disagree, report the disagreement — do not pick the convenient one.
  4. A chain of evidence. A reader can trace every conclusion back to a specific observation, and back further to the question.
  5. Honesty about generalisation. Which brings us to the next slide.
The most misunderstood sentence in qualitative work

What one case can and cannot tell you

Statistical generalisation — what a case study cannot do

“We studied one company, therefore Indian software companies behave this way.”

A sample of one supports no inference to a population. No amount of depth repairs this; depth and breadth are different currencies.

Analytic generalisation — what it can do

“In this case, migration failed for reasons X and Y — which the theory predicts, and which suggests the theory should be extended to cover Z.”

The case generalises to theory, not to a population: it can support, extend, or refute a general explanation. A single well-documented case that contradicts a widely held claim is a substantial contribution.

The practical rule for your report: write the sentence “this study supports conclusions about ___, and does not support conclusions about ___.” Reviewers who see it stop looking for the overclaim — because you have already ruled it out.

Unit I review

Ten things that must survive this unit

  1. A research problem is a gap you could be wrong about; if nothing could refute it, it is not one.
  2. Task, project and research problem differ on one axis: is the outcome already known?
  3. Problem-finding is a search, not a spark — five sources, a repeatable procedure.
  4. The best gaps are assumptions; problems sitting in two sources are strongest.
  5. Narrow with the funnel and five knobs until ≈1 project fits.
  1. FINER + M multiplies. Any single 1 is fatal; feasibility is relative to you.
  2. Four repairs only: narrow · re-anchor · re-measure · abandon. Hoping is not one.
  3. Objectives use measurable verbs; each maps to one experiment and one artefact.
  4. The approach follows the kind of claim, not the method you already know.
  5. Interpretation is where honesty is tested: negative results are results, and you state your limitations first.

If you remember one sentence from Unit I: a reviewer only ever gets the sentence — so everything that matters must be in it.

Your one-page research statement

Eight things on one page

  1. Problem statement — six moves, under 200 words (I.3, I.5)
  2. FINER + M card — with one line of evidence in every row (I.4)
  3. Scope · delimitations · limitations — 3 / 3 / 2 (I.6)
  4. One general + three specific objectives, each naming its artefact (I.6)
  5. A directional hypothesis and its refutation condition (I.6)
  6. Named approach + the evidence package it requires (I.7)
  7. Data plan — source, access route, sampling, split rule (I.8)
  8. One sentence naming your biggest risk as a criterion — “my weakest row is F, because access to the hospital data is unconfirmed.” (I.4, I.5)

Unit I · done when…

  • Your problem statement has at least three dated versions.
  • Someone else's name appears in your notebook at least twice.
  • You have killed at least one idea, with a written reason.
  • You can name your approach, your metric, your baseline and your refutation condition without looking.
  • You can say, in one sentence, what would make you abandon this project.

You began Unit I able to build things. You end it able to decide what deserves to be built, and to defend that decision to someone who is not obliged to agree with you.
