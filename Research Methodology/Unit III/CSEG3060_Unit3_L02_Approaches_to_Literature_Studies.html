The Approach Is Chosen by the Question

One topic — machine learning for intrusion detection — and five different review questions. Each one requires a different study design, and picking the wrong design cannot be repaired later by working harder.

“What has been tried in this area, roughly, and what should I read first?”

→

Narrative review

“Does deep learning detect intrusions more accurately than feature-engineered methods?”

→

Systematic literature review

“What is the structure of this field — which sub-problems, which venues, which evaluation types?”

→

Systematic mapping study

“How large, on average, is the accuracy gain, across all comparable published experiments?”

→

Meta-analysis

“Who are the leading groups, which papers are seminal, and which sub-topics are emerging?”

→

Bibliometric analysis

Notice what is constant: the topic. What changes is the question, and with it the protocol, the effort and the kind of claim the finished review is entitled to make. A student who says “I am doing a literature review” has not yet said which of these five things they are doing.

A Spectrum from Judgement to Protocol

The approaches differ along one underlying dimension: how much of the selection is decided in advance, and therefore how much of it a reader can check.

AUTHOR'S JUDGEMENT

DOCUMENTED PROTOCOL

SLR

Explicit, piloted protocol.

Moving right buys reproducibility.

Another researcher can re-run the search and reach your set. That is the only thing that makes a review's coverage claim checkable rather than asserted.

Moving right costs months.

A full SLR in software engineering is typically a multi-person, multi-month project. That cost is the honest reason most student reviews are narrative — and saying so is better than pretending otherwise.

Right is not automatically better.

An SLR answering a question nobody asked is worse than a good narrative review that orients a reader. Match the design to the question, then execute that design properly.

PART 2 · NARRATIVE AND SYSTEMATIC

The Narrative (Traditional) Review

DEFINITION

A qualitative summary of the literature on a topic, organised by the author's judgement, without a formally specified and reproducible search protocol.

PURPOSE

To give a broad overview of an area, its background, or its historical development.

To orient a reader who does not yet know the vocabulary or the main families of approach.

To set up an argument: the review exists to justify the specific problem the paper then attacks.

STRENGTHS

Flexible and fast — days, not months, which is why almost every paper's related-work section is one.

Good for a wide topic that no protocol could bound sensibly.

Allows genuine expert insight and interpretation, which a mechanical protocol suppresses.

WEAKNESSES

Selection bias: the author chooses the papers that suit the argument, usually without intending to.

Not reproducible — nobody else can arrive at the same set, because no method was stated.

Coverage cannot be verified, so a claim that “no prior work addresses X” cannot be checked.

Use in CS: the “Related Work” section of a conference paper, and the background chapter of a B.Tech or M.Tech project report. This is the design you will use most often — which is exactly why its weaknesses are worth knowing.

PART 2 · NARRATIVE AND SYSTEMATIC

A Narrative Review Done Honestly

“Narrative” does not mean “unmethodical”. Four cheap disciplines remove most of the design's weakness without turning it into an SLR.

DISCIPLINE 1

State the scope

Say what you covered and what you did not.

WHY IT WORKS

“This section covers deep-learning intrusion detectors published 2019–2025; signature-based systems are outside scope.” One sentence, and the reader now knows the boundary of your coverage claim.

DISCIPLINE 2

Say where you looked

Name the databases and the terms, even informally.

WHY IT WORKS

Two lines in the text or a footnote. It costs nothing and converts an unverifiable claim into a partially verifiable one — which is the whole difference the SLR is trying to buy.

DISCIPLINE 3

Include what contradicts you

Especially the strongest opposing result.

WHY IT WORKS

A reviewer who knows the field will notice the missing paper. Including and answering it is far stronger than omitting it, and it is the single fastest way to look competent.

DISCIPLINE 4

Organise by idea

Themes and families of method, never one paragraph per paper.

WHY IT WORKS

This is what makes it a review rather than a list, and it is independent of whether the search was systematic. Lecture 3 treats it in detail as synthesis.

None of these four requires a protocol, two reviewers, or a PRISMA diagram. They require one paragraph of honesty about how the reading was done.

PART 2 · NARRATIVE AND SYSTEMATIC

The Systematic Literature Review

DEFINITION

A review that identifies, evaluates and synthesises all available research relevant to a set of pre-defined research questions, using an explicit, documented and reproducible protocol designed to minimise bias.

  1. 1
    It is a piece of research, not a preliminary to one.

An SLR has research questions, a method, a population (the primary studies), a data-extraction instrument and a synthesis. It is reported, reviewed and cited as a study — because it is one.

  1. 2
    The standard reference in computing is Kitchenham.

Kitchenham and Charters, Guidelines for Performing Systematic Literature Reviews in Software Engineering (2007), adapted the medical evidence-based-practice methodology to software engineering. Name it in an answer.

  1. 3
    The protocol is written and piloted before the search.

Everything that could be chosen to favour a conclusion — sources, strings, criteria, quality checklist, extraction fields — is fixed in advance and recorded. That is the entire mechanism by which bias is controlled.

  1. 4
    It normally requires at least two reviewers.

Screening decisions are made independently and compared, with disagreements resolved by discussion or a third reviewer. Inter-rater agreement is reported, often as Cohen's kappa.

PART 2 · NARRATIVE AND SYSTEMATIC

Kitchenham's Three Phases

Ten steps in three phases. Note that the whole of Phase 1 happens before a single database is opened — the same ordering, and for the same reason, as Unit II's evaluation procedure.

PHASESTEPSTHE BIAS IT CONTROLS
1 · Planning
① Identify the need for the review — is there already a recent SLR? ② Formulate the review questions (RQ1, RQ2, …). ③ Develop and pilot the review protocol: sources, search strings, inclusion and exclusion criteria, quality criteria, data-extraction form, synthesis method.
Selection bias, at its root. Criteria written before you see the results cannot be tuned to produce the conclusion you prefer.
2 · Conducting
④ Execute the search across the chosen databases. ⑤ Select the primary studies by applying the criteria to titles, then abstracts, then full texts. ⑥ Assess study quality against a checklist. ⑦ Extract data into the structured form. ⑧ Synthesise, quantitatively or qualitatively.
Reviewer bias and inconsistency. Two independent screeners, a fixed checklist and a fixed extraction form make the decisions comparable and auditable.
3 · Reporting
⑨ Write the review and report the PRISMA flow. ⑩ State threats to validity and archive the protocol.
Reporting bias. The flow diagram makes every exclusion visible and countable, so a reader can see what was dropped and why.

The examinable structure is plan → conduct → report. If you remember nothing else, remember that the protocol precedes the search, and that the PRISMA flow is what makes the selection auditable.

What Is Actually in a Review Protocol

A protocol is a short document — five to ten pages — written and piloted before the review begins. It is what distinguishes an SLR from thorough reading.

1

Background and need

Why this review, now. What existing reviews cover, and what they leave open.

2

Review questions

RQ1, RQ2, RQ3, in answerable form, usually derived through PICOC.

3

Search strategy

Databases, the exact Boolean string for each, the date range, and the snowballing procedure.

4

Selection criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria, written so that two people applying them agree.

5

Quality assessment

The checklist and scoring rule. Whether low-quality studies are excluded or weighted.

6

Data extraction

The fields to be recorded per study — the columns of the literature matrix.

7

Synthesis method

Thematic, comparative, or statistical; and if statistical, the model and the effect measure.

8

Validity and timetable

Known threats to the review's own validity, the reviewers, and the schedule.

Protocols are increasingly registered in advance — PROSPERO for health, and the Open Science Framework for software engineering — which makes the commitment public and dated. Registration is the review's version of pre-registration in Lecture 5.

PART 2 · NARRATIVE AND SYSTEMATIC

What an SLR Buys, and What It Costs

Both halves belong in an answer. A student who lists only the strengths has not understood why most reviews are not systematic.

WHAT IT BUYS

Reproducibility

Another team following the protocol reaches substantially your set. The coverage claim becomes checkable.

Transparency

Every inclusion and exclusion is counted and justified. A reader can disagree with a criterion and see its effect.

Bias control

Pre-stated criteria and independent screening remove the two largest sources of selective reading.

Strong evidence

A synthesis over all comparable studies is stronger than any single study, which is the point of the design.

A publishable contribution

SLRs are published in major journals and are heavily cited. The review is the research output.

WHAT IT COSTS

Months of work

Search, screening, quality assessment and extraction over hundreds of records. Typically three to twelve months.

At least two reviewers

Single-reviewer screening reintroduces exactly the bias the design exists to remove.

A narrow question

Exhaustiveness is only possible if the question is tightly bounded. Broad questions do not admit this design.

Dependence on the published record

It can only be as good as the evidence that exists — and publication bias means what exists is skewed.

Rapid obsolescence

In a fast field the search is stale within a year, which is why updated SLRs are themselves a publication type.

The Systematic Mapping Study

Broader and shallower than an SLR. It does not answer a question about the world; it answers a question about the literature.

THE DIFFERENCE IN ONE LINE

An SLR asks “what is the answer?”
A mapping study asks “what has been asked, and where is nothing?”

Explainability

The empty and near-empty cells are the point. Explainability has barely been evaluated at all, and nothing at all has been written about signature-based explainability — which is a gap you can see rather than assert.

  1. 1
    It classifies rather than concludes.

Papers are coded along two or three dimensions and counted: how many per year, per venue, per sub-topic, per research type.

  1. 2
    The research-type facet is standard.

Evaluation research, validation research, solution proposal, philosophical paper, experience report, opinion paper — Wieringa's classification, widely used in software engineering.

  1. 3
    The output is a map, not an answer.

A classification scheme plus a bubble plot: two facets on the axes, bubble area proportional to the number of papers in that cell.

  1. 4
    Use it when the area is new or wide.

When you do not yet know what exists, a mapping study is the correct first move — and it frequently identifies the narrow question that a later SLR then answers.

Read only the abstract and, where it is uninformative, the introduction and conclusion. Nothing more at this stage.

Pull the terms and concepts that describe what the paper does. Record them verbatim, without tidying.

Categories must be exhaustive and usable. Every included paper must land somewhere, and two coders must land it in the same place. Pilot the scheme on twenty papers and measure agreement before coding the rest.

The count is the finding. A mapping study's results section is a set of distributions. Resist the temptation to draw SLR-style conclusions from them — the reading was too shallow to support one.

PART 3 · MAPPING, POOLING, COUNTING

Meta-Analysis

DEFINITION

A meta-analysis statistically combines the quantitative results — the effect sizes — of several independent studies of the same question, to produce a single, more precise pooled estimate.

ELEMENT 1

The effect size

A standardised measure of the size of a difference — not of whether it is significant.

Cohen's d for a difference of means, odds ratio for a binary outcome, correlation r for an association. Standardising is what makes studies with different scales comparable at all.

ELEMENT 2

The weighting

Studies are weighted by precision, usually by the inverse of their variance.

A large, tight study counts for more than a small, noisy one. This is why the pooled estimate is not the arithmetic mean of the effects — see the next slide.

ELEMENT 3

Heterogeneity

Are these studies even estimating the same quantity?

Reported as Q and I². High heterogeneity means a fixed-effect model is wrong; you move to a random-effects model, or you conclude that pooling was not appropriate.

ELEMENT 4

The forest plot

One line per study: its effect and confidence interval, with the pooled estimate at the foot.

The standard presentation. It shows at a glance which studies disagree, how precise each is, and whether the pooled interval excludes zero.

In computing it is used mainly in empirical software engineering and HCI — pooling, for example, the effect of pair programming on defect density. It is rare in systems and theory, because experimental settings are almost never comparable enough to pool.

Worked Example: Why Pooling Is Not Averaging

Question: “Three controlled experiments compare pair programming with solo work. How large is the effect on defect density?”

  1. 1
    The tempting summary

THE THREE STUDIES

study n d SE
---------------------------
A 40 0.62 0.32
B 120 0.28 0.18
C 25 0.95 0.41

Average the three effect sizes. (0.62 + 0.28 + 0.95) / 3 = 0.62 — a moderate-to-large effect, and a headline finding.

  1. 2
    What that ignores

Study C has 25 participants and a standard error of 0.41; study B has 120 and a standard error of 0.18. The simple mean gives the noisiest study the same vote as the most precise one.

THE TWO SUMMARIES

weights w = 1/SE²
A = 9.8 B = 30.9 C = 6.0

simple mean of d = 0.62
weighted pooled d = 0.44
95% CI = [0.15, 0.72]

  1. 3
    The correct summary

Weight each study by 1/SE², the inverse of its variance. Study B now carries 30.9 of the 46.7 total weight, and the pooled estimate falls to d = 0.44 with a 95% interval of [0.15, 0.72].

  1. 4
    What the interval tells you

It excludes zero, so the effect is unlikely to be nothing — but it spans small to moderate. “Pair programming reduces defects, by an amount this evidence cannot pin down closely” is the defensible sentence.

The structure of the error is exactly Unit II's wrong-mean example: an unweighted average of quantities that carry different amounts of information produces a confident number that overstates the result.

Publication Bias: The Defect Pooling Cannot Fix

A meta-analysis synthesises the published record. If the record is a biased sample of the experiments that were run, then a more precise estimate of the record is a more precise estimate of the wrong thing.

Experiments are run

Some find an effect. Some find nothing.

Null results are written up less

Authors judge them uninteresting, or run one more variant instead.

Null results are accepted less

Reviewers ask “what is the contribution?” — and in computing, often reasonably.

The record over-represents effects

What survives to publication is skewed towards positive and towards large.

Pooling inherits the skew

The pooled estimate is biased upwards, with a confidence interval that does not know it.

DETECT IT

Plot effect size against precision — the funnel plot. Under no bias the points form a symmetric funnel; missing small studies with small effects cut one corner out of it. Egger's test puts a number on the asymmetry.

SEARCH FOR THE UNPUBLISHED

Theses, technical reports, registered-but-unreported trials, and direct contact with authors. This is one of the strongest arguments for including grey literature in an SLR.

REPORT IT AS A THREAT

Every meta-analysis states publication bias under threats to validity. Stating it is not a formality: it bounds how strongly the pooled estimate can be read.

Bibliometric and Scientometric Analysis

A quantitative study of the literature itself rather than of its content: counting publications, citations, co-authorship links, keyword co-occurrence and citation networks.

TECHNIQUEWHAT IT MEASURESWHAT IT REVEALS
Co-citation analysis
How often two earlier papers are cited together by later ones.
The intellectual base of a field: which classics the community treats as belonging together, and therefore what the sub-communities are.
Bibliographic coupling
How many references two papers share.
Which current papers are working on the same thing, even when they do not cite each other — a good way to find your true competitors.
Co-word / keyword co-occurrence
Which terms appear together in titles, abstracts and keyword lists.
The topical structure of the field, and — by comparing time slices — which topics are emerging and which are being abandoned.
Co-authorship analysis
Who publishes with whom, and across which institutions and countries.
The leading groups and their collaboration structure. Useful when choosing where to apply, and when identifying likely reviewers.

TOOLS

VOSviewer and CiteSpace for mapping and visualisation · Bibliometrix (the R package, with its Biblioshiny interface) for the full analysis pipeline · Publish or Perish for citation harvesting. All four take Scopus or Web of Science exports directly.

Worked Example: Reading a Keyword Co-Occurrence Map

Situation: you export 2,400 records from Scopus, load them into VOSviewer, and get a map with four coloured clusters.

A cluster is a sub-community, not a truth.

Clusters are produced by a clustering algorithm with a resolution parameter. Change the parameter and you get a different number of clusters. Report the settings, exactly as you would report a hyper-parameter.

Node size is frequency, not importance.

A term is large because many papers use it. “Machine learning” will dominate any map in this area and tells you nothing. The informative nodes are mid-sized ones at the boundary between clusters.

Distance is co-occurrence, not similarity of meaning.

Two terms sit close together because papers use them together. Terms that ought to be related but never co-occur are often the interesting finding — it means two communities are not talking.

Overlay by year, then read it again.

The average publication year per term is the single most useful overlay. It converts a static picture of the field into a picture of where the field is moving, which is what you actually want to know.

Bibliometrics is descriptive. It tells you what the literature looks like, never whether any of it is correct — so it complements an SLR and cannot replace one. Used alone as a “review”, it is a picture of a field nobody has read.

PART 4 · COMPARISON AND CHOICE

Five Further Named Approaches

Each has a defined meaning. Using the right name in an answer, and in a paper, signals that you know which design you are claiming to have executed.

Integrative review

Combines findings from studies using different methodologies.

Experiments, case studies and qualitative work synthesised together, to build a conclusion no single method could support.

Theoretical / conceptual review

Examines the theories and models proposed in an area.

Compares their assumptions, scope and explanatory power rather than their empirical results. Common in HCI and in foundations work.

Rapid review

An SLR with deliberately simplified steps.

Fewer databases, single screening, no quality assessment. Completeness is traded for speed, and the simplifications are declared.

Umbrella / tertiary review

A systematic review of systematic reviews.

Used when an area already has many SLRs. The primary studies are the reviews themselves, and the same protocol discipline applies.

Grey-literature review

A systematic study of non-peer-reviewed practitioner sources.

Important in software engineering, where industry practice leads publication by years. Often combined with an SLR as a multivocal review.

Scoping review

Maps the extent, range and nature of the evidence.

Close cousin of the mapping study, with the term more common outside computing. Often run to decide whether a full SLR is feasible.

PART 4 · COMPARISON AND CHOICE

Choosing an Approach: Four Questions

Work down the list. The first question that gives a clear answer usually decides it.

1

Is your question about the world, or about the literature?

About the world — does X work better than Y — points to an SLR or a meta-analysis. About the literature — what has been studied, by whom, where are the holes — points to a mapping study or a bibliometric analysis.

2

Do comparable numbers exist across studies?

Only if several studies measure the same outcome, on comparable subjects, with reported variances, can you pool. In most of computing they do not, and a meta-analysis is simply not available. Say so rather than forcing it.

3

Can the question be bounded tightly enough to be exhaustive?

Exhaustiveness is what an SLR promises. If the honest scope is “everything about machine learning in security”, no protocol can deliver it — narrow the question, or run a mapping study instead.

4

What resources do you actually have?

Two reviewers and three months permits an SLR. One student and three weeks permits a rapid review or an honest narrative review with a stated scope. Claiming a design you cannot execute is the worst of the options.

Whichever you choose, name it in the paper and describe how you executed it. A review that does not say what kind of review it is will be read as a narrative one — and judged by an SLR's standards anyway.

How Each Approach Fails

Every design has a characteristic failure. Knowing yours is how you defend against it — and how you recognise it in somebody else's work.

APPROACHITS CHARACTERISTIC FAILURETHE DEFENCE
Narrative review
Cites the papers that support the intended conclusion, and is silent about the rest — usually without the author noticing.
State the scope, name the databases, and deliberately include the strongest contrary result you can find.
Systematic review
A protocol executed faithfully over a question that did not need answering, or over a literature too heterogeneous to synthesise.
Check for an existing recent SLR first. Pilot the protocol on twenty papers and see whether a synthesis is even possible.
Mapping study
Counts papers and calls the counts findings, then draws conclusions the shallow reading cannot support.
Report distributions as distributions. If you want a conclusion about effectiveness, run an SLR on the narrow question the map revealed.
Meta-analysis
Pools studies that are not measuring the same thing, producing a precise number for a quantity that does not exist.
Report heterogeneity. If I² is high, either model it with random effects or state that pooling is inappropriate and synthesise narratively.
Bibliometric analysis
Presents cluster diagrams as a review, when nobody has read the papers and no claim about their content is warranted.
Use it to orient and to find seminal work, and pair it with a design that actually reads something.

RECAP · UNIT III · LECTURE 2

Key Takeaways

  1. 1
    “Literature review” names several different research designs. The approach is chosen by the question, and the approaches differ along one dimension: how much of the selection is fixed in advance and therefore checkable.
  2. 2
    A narrative review is a qualitative summary organised by the author's judgement, with no stated protocol. It is fast, flexible and biased — and it is what almost every related-work section is. Four cheap disciplines make it honest.
  3. 3
    A systematic literature review answers pre-defined questions using an explicit, piloted protocol. Kitchenham's structure is plan → conduct → report, and the protocol precedes the search. It buys reproducibility and costs months.
  4. 4
    A mapping study classifies rather than concludes: keywording produces facets, coding produces counts, and the output is a map whose empty cells are visible gaps. Use it when the area is new or too wide to bound.
  5. 5
    A meta-analysis pools effect sizes weighted by precision — not an average of effects. It reports heterogeneity, and it cannot repair publication bias, only detect and declare it.
  6. 6
    Bibliometrics studies the literature rather than its content: co-citation, bibliographic coupling, co-word and co-authorship. It is descriptive, it complements a review, and alone it is not one.
