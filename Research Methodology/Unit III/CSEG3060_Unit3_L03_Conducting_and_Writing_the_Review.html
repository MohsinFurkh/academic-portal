Step 1 — Define the Scope and the Review Questions

A research problem is not a review question. The conversion is the first real piece of work, and everything downstream is derived from its output.

P

PICO AND PICOC

Who or what is being studied.

Population

The technique, tool or treatment applied.

Intervention

What it is being compared against.

Comparison

The measured effect that decides the question.

Outcome

The setting. PICOC's addition, and the one that suits software engineering.

Context

1

FOUR RULES FOR THE QUESTIONS

Two or three questions, no more.

Each one must be answerable from published studies. “What is the future of X?” is not a review question, because no study reports it.

Write them before the search.

The search string is derived from the P, I and O terms. If the questions change afterwards, the search must be re-run, and honestly reported as re-run.

State the boundary explicitly.

Years, languages, venue types, application domains. Everything you exclude is a limitation you will later have to declare, so choose deliberately.

Check that an answer would matter.

If both possible answers lead to the same next action, the question is not worth the months. This is Unit I's criterion of a good problem, applied to a review.

The scope statement is also the first thing a reviewer reads. A precise one signals a controlled study; a vague one signals that the search went wherever the results led.

Worked Example: PICOC Applied to a Review Question

Question: “Does static analysis reduce security defects in web applications?”

P

Population

Web applications written in PHP or Java.

1

WHAT EACH ELEMENT BUYS YOU LATER

P and I give the search terms.

Every synonym of “web application” and of “static analysis” goes into the string.

C fixes what counts as a study.

A paper with no comparison group cannot answer this question, so it is excluded — and now you can say why.

O fixes the extraction form.

“Defects found before release” becomes a column. If a paper does not report it, it cannot be included.

Context bounds the claim.

Your conclusion will be about industrial teams in that decade — not about students, and not about 2008.

I

Intervention

Automated static application security testing (SAST).

C

Comparison

Manual code review only.

O

Outcome

Number of security defects found before release.

C

Context

Industrial development teams, 2015–2025.

Notice that the search string almost writes itself from the P, I and O terms — and that the C and O together fix the inclusion criteria.

Step 2 — Build the Search Strategy

Four mechanical steps produce the string. The judgement is entirely in step two — and that is where most searches quietly lose half the literature.

1

List the key concepts

Taken from the review question. Usually three: the population, the intervention, and the outcome. Four concepts joined by AND is normally already too narrow.

2

List synonyms for each

Synonyms, abbreviations, spelling variants, older terminology and related terms. Mine the keyword lists of the papers you already have; that is where the vocabulary you do not know is written down.

3

Combine with Boolean operators

OR between synonyms widens; AND between concepts narrows; NOT excludes and is dangerous. Use truncation with *, quote phrases, and nest with parentheses.

4

Adapt and record

Each database has its own syntax, field codes and truncation rules. Restrict to title-abstract-keyword rather than full text. Record the exact string and the date.

THE TWO FAILURE MODES, AND HOW TO TELL WHICH YOU HAVE

Too narrow — the search returns forty records and you recognise all of them. A concept is over-specified, or a synonym is missing. Test it: does the string retrieve the five papers you already know are central? If not, fix the string, not the expectations.
Too broad — the search returns eleven thousand records and the first page is off-topic. Add the missing concept, restrict to title-abstract-keyword fields rather than full text, or bound the venue type. Do not simply screen fewer of them.

Worked Example: A Search String, and What Citation Chaining Adds

THE STRING

("static analysis" OR "SAST"
OR "static application security testing")
AND ("web application*" OR "web service*")
AND ("vulnerab*" OR "security defect*"
OR "security bug*")

1

WHAT FILLS THE HOLE: CITATION CHAINING (SNOWBALLING)

Backward chaining

Follow the reference lists of the papers you already have. This reaches older, foundational work that uses vocabulary your string does not contain — which is exactly the systematic hole a keyword search leaves.

Forward chaining

Use “Cited by” in Google Scholar, Scopus or Semantic Scholar to reach newer work that builds on a paper. This is the only reliable way to find work published after your string was written.

Author search

Once you know the two or three groups leading the area, read their publication lists directly in DBLP. Groups publish in clusters, and one omitted paper usually means four.

Iterate until closure

Repeat both directions on every newly included paper until a round adds nothing. Reporting that you reached closure is a stronger coverage claim than reporting the string alone.

WHAT THE STRING STILL MISSES

Papers that say “taint analysis”, “code scanning” or “abstract interpretation”; papers about Node.js that never use the phrase “web application”; and every paper whose abstract describes the tool but not the defect class.

A keyword search finds only papers that used your vocabulary; citation chaining follows the citation graph instead. Wohlin's result: chaining alone, from a good seed set, can match a database search — and the two together are markedly better than either.

Step 3 — Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

These are written before screening begins, so that decisions cannot be made to suit a preferred conclusion. That is the whole of their purpose.

Every criterion is a limitation you will declare.

“English only” means your conclusion is about the English-language literature. Say so under threats to validity rather than leaving a reader to notice it.

Criteria must be operational.

“High quality” is not a criterion, because two screeners will disagree. “Reports sample size and a measure of dispersion” is one, because they will not.

Pilot them on twenty records.

Two screeners, independently, then compare. Disagreement above a few per cent means the criteria are ambiguous — fix the wording, not the disagreement.

PART 2 · SCREENING AND RECORDING

Step 5 — Read at Three Levels

Reading 120 papers word by word is impossible, and attempting it is why reviews stall. Keshav's three-pass method allocates attention in proportion to relevance.

FIRST PASS

5–10 minutes

Title, abstract, introduction, section headings, conclusions, figures, references.

DECIDE

Is this relevant at all? Most papers stop here, and that is the point.

ANSWER THE FIVE C'S

Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity.

OUTPUT

An include-or-exclude decision, and a one-line note on why.

SECOND PASS

About an hour

Read properly, but ignore proofs and detailed derivations.

STUDY

Figures, tables, experimental setup, baselines, datasets.

TEST

Could you summarise the paper's claim and its evidence to someone else?

OUTPUT

A completed row in the literature matrix.

THIRD PASS

Several hours

Attempt to re-create the work.

FOLLOW

Every assumption; check each proof step and each experimental choice.

ASK

What would you have done differently, and what would that change?

RESERVE FOR

The handful of papers your own contribution builds on directly.

The discipline is in stopping. A first pass that turns into an hour on a paper you will exclude is the commonest way a screening schedule collapses.

The columns are chosen by the review questions. If a question asks about latency, latency is a column — and a paper that does not report it becomes an exclusion or a documented blank, not a guess.

The Limitation column is where the gap comes from. Read down it once the matrix is complete. A limitation that recurs in every row is not a weakness of one paper; it is a property of the field, and it is your opening.

Filling it is the second pass. The matrix is not written up afterwards. It is the artefact the second pass produces, which is why the reading and the recording cost the same hour.

This table is called the literature matrix or synthesis matrix. Being able to draw it and name its columns earns marks in any question about the analysis of literature — and it is the single most useful practical habit in this unit.

The annotated-bibliography paragraph: one paragraph per paper, each beginning “X et al. proposed …”. It shows reading but not thinking, and it cannot lead to a research gap, because nothing in it is ever compared to anything else. Organise by idea, never by paper.

Critical Appraisal of a Single Study

Ten questions, asked of every paper you include. Most of them are Unit II's standards, turned around and applied to somebody else's work.

1

Is the research question clearly stated? If you cannot find it in one sentence, the paper's own authors may not have had one.

2

Is the method appropriate to the question? Unit II: does the proof method match the kind of claim being made?

3

Are the assumptions stated, and realistic? An unstated assumption is one nobody — including the authors — has checked.

4

Is the dataset or workload representative? And is it public? A private dataset makes every number unverifiable.

5

Are the baselines fair and current? An untuned or outdated baseline manufactures an improvement out of nothing.

6

Are the metrics right, and is statistics reported? A single run with no dispersion is an anecdote with a decimal point.

7

Are the conclusions supported, or over-claimed? Compare the abstract's sentence with what the results section actually shows.

8

Are threats to validity acknowledged? Their absence is informative: it usually means nobody looked for them.

9

Is the work reproducible? Code, data, hyper-parameters, seeds, environment. Any missing one blocks replication.

10

Are there conflicts of interest or funding effects? Who paid, and would a different result have been inconvenient for them?

Six Methods of Synthesis

Pick one as the organising principle of the chapter, and use the others locally. Mixing them at the top level produces a structure a reader cannot follow.

Thematic

Group findings into themes that emerge from the reading.

Feature-engineering approaches, end-to-end deep approaches, hybrid approaches. The default, and usually the right one.

Chronological

Trace how the field evolved over time.

Useful for showing that a problem has been attacked repeatedly, and why the earlier attempts failed. Weak as a sole structure.

Methodological

Group by the technique used.

Natural in computing, and it maps directly onto your Method column. Risks hiding disagreements about results.

Comparative / tabular

The literature matrix, extended with a discussion of the patterns it reveals.

The table is not the synthesis; the paragraph explaining what the table shows is.

Quantitative

Meta-analysis, where comparable numbers exist.

Rare in computing, for the reasons Lecture 2 gave — but state that you considered it and why it was not possible.

Conceptual framework

Draw a taxonomy or tree of the field.

Every cited work sits somewhere on it, and the empty branch is your gap. The strongest option when it is available.

Seven Types of Research Gap

The literature study succeeds only if it ends in a gap. Naming which type yours is forces you to say what kind of evidence would close it.

TYPE OF GAPMEANINGEXAMPLE IN COMPUTING
Knowledge gap
Nothing is known about X.
No study of the energy cost of federated learning on edge devices.
Evidence gap
Published results contradict each other.
Some studies report LSTM beating CNN for intrusion detection; others report the reverse.
Methodological gap
Only one kind of method has been tried.
All studies evaluate offline on captured traces; none in a live network.
Population / context gap
Studied only in one setting.
Models validated only on Western medical imaging datasets.
Practical gap
It works in the lab, not in practice.
99% accuracy with 4-second inference — unusable at line rate.
Evaluation gap
No common benchmark or baseline.
Every paper uses its own private dataset, so no two numbers are comparable.
Theoretical gap
No explanation of why it works.
An empirically strong heuristic with no bound and no analysis.

The type you name determines your contribution. A methodological gap is closed by a different experiment; a theoretical gap is closed by a proof. Naming the wrong type sends you to the wrong Unit II proof method.

Worked Example: Writing the Gap Statement

This is the paragraph the whole literature review exists to produce. Everything before it is preparation; everything after it is consequence.

WEAK

“Not much work has been done in this area.”

STRONG

“Although transformer-based intrusion detectors report accuracies above 99% on CIC-IDS2017 [27, 31], all reported evaluations are offline and none reports inference latency. Since a production IDS must classify at line rate, the practical usability of these models is unknown. This work therefore evaluates accuracy and per-packet latency of a compressed transformer detector on live campus traffic.”

1

It cites the evidence.

Two specific papers, not “several studies”. A reader can check the claim about the state of the art.

2

It names the missing dimension.

Not “more work is needed” but “none reports inference latency” — a precise, falsifiable statement about the literature.

3

It explains why that matters.

A production IDS must classify at line rate. Without this sentence the gap is merely a hole, not a problem.

4

It states what will be done.

The objective follows from the gap, which is what makes the next chapter inevitable rather than arbitrary.

Structure: The Funnel

A literature review narrows from the general field to the single gap that your objective addresses. Each level must be narrower than the one above it, and must follow from it.

Broad context

Why the general area matters

TWO TESTS ON THE STRUCTURE

Can you delete a level and still reach the objective?

If yes, that level is decoration. Cut it. The commonest offender is a first section on the general importance of artificial intelligence.

Does each level's last paragraph set up the next?

The funnel is held together by transitions. If a section could be moved elsewhere without damage, the narrowing is not real — you have a list of sections rather than an argument.

PART 4 · WRITING IT

Eight Rules of Good Practice

1

Every paragraph makes a point. Citations support the point; the point does not exist to introduce the citations. Write the topic sentence first, then find what supports it.

2

Group citations where works agree. “Several authors report the same effect [3, 7, 11]” is one sentence where an annotated bibliography would have used three paragraphs.

3

Compare explicitly. However, in contrast, building on this, similarly. These words are the visible surface of synthesis; a review without them has not compared anything.

4

Be critical but courteous. Criticise the method, never the author. “The evaluation does not report variance” is publishable; “the authors were careless” is not.

5

Use tense deliberately. Present tense for established knowledge — QuickSort has average complexity O(n log n). Past tense for a particular study's actions — Kumar measured throughput on a 40-node cluster.

6

Keep quotations rare. In engineering writing, paraphrase almost always. Quote only a definition or a distinctive phrase that would lose something if reworded.

7

End with the gap. The last paragraph leads directly into your objectives. If a reader could stop at the end of the chapter and not know what you are about to do, it has failed.

8

Keep it current. Re-run the searches before final submission, and record the date you last did so.

PART 5 · PRACTICE

A Checklist Before You Submit the Chapter

✓

The scope and the search are stated. Databases, strings, date range, date last run — in the text, a footnote or an appendix. Without them, no coverage claim is checkable.

✓

Every section is organised by idea. Read your section headings alone. If any of them is an author's name, the structure is wrong.

✓

Every included paper appears in the matrix. And every matrix column is used somewhere in the text. An unused column means you extracted data you did not need.

✓

Contrary evidence is present and answered. Find the strongest published result that undercuts your position. If it is not in the chapter, put it in.

✓

The chapter ends in a gap statement. Cited, specific, explaining why it matters, and stating what you will do. Four elements, one paragraph.

✓

Every citation has been read. Not the abstract — the paper. This is the line between a literature review and a reference list.

RECAP · UNIT III · LECTURE 3

Key Takeaways

  1. 1
    The process is scope → search → screen → read → record → synthesise, and the sequence matters: four of the six steps are complete before you read anything in depth.
  2. 2
    PICOC converts a research problem into answerable review questions, and the Boolean search string is derived from the P, I and O terms. Keyword search always leaves a hole; citation chaining — backward and forward — is what fills it.
  3. 3
    Inclusion and exclusion criteria are written before screening and must be operational enough that two people agree. PRISMA reports the flow — every number, and the reason for every exclusion.
  4. 4
    Read in three passes of increasing depth, and record in a literature matrix as you go. The Limitation column, read down, is where the gap comes from.
  5. 5
    Synthesis is organised by theme, not by paper, and shows relationships: agreement, disagreement, evolution, gaps. The annotated-bibliography paragraph is the defect this replaces.
  6. 6
    The review narrows through a funnel and ends in a gap statement with four elements — evidence cited, missing dimension named, significance explained, and the objective stated. That paragraph is what the whole chapter exists to produce.
