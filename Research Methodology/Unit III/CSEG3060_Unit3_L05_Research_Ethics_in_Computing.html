PART 1 · FOUNDATIONS

What Research Ethics Is

DEFINITION

The branch of applied ethics concerned with the moral principles, norms and standards that govern the planning, conduct, reporting and application of research — so that the rights and welfare of participants are protected, the integrity of the scientific record is preserved, and society's trust in research is maintained.

POINT 1

Three objects of protection

People, the record, and public trust.

Almost every ethical rule in this lecture protects one of the three. Asking which one a rule serves is the fastest way to reconstruct it in an examination.

POINT 2

It is not the same as law

Legal and ethical are different tests, and neither implies the other.

Scraping a public forum may break no law and still be unethical. Ethics is the stricter test in research, because the researcher chooses to run the study.

POINT 3

It is not a form to be signed

Ethical clearance is a checkpoint; ethical conduct is the whole project.

Approval at the design stage does not authorise whatever happens during collection, analysis and publication. Each stage has its own obligations.

Why Research Ethics Exists: A Short History

Every rule in this lecture was written after something went wrong. Knowing which failure produced which rule is what makes the rules memorable rather than arbitrary.

1947

Nuremberg Code

Framed after the trials of the Nazi medical experiments. Established that voluntary informed consent is absolutely essential.

1964

Declaration of Helsinki

World Medical Association; ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects, including independent review. Revised repeatedly since.

1932–72

Tuskegee Syphilis Study

Treatment deliberately withheld from African-American men without their knowledge, for forty years. The public outrage led directly to modern regulation in the United States.

1979

Belmont Report

Codified the three principles now used everywhere: respect for persons, beneficence, justice.

2012

Menlo Report

Adapted Belmont specifically to information and communication technology research — the document most directly relevant to computer science.

Note the pattern: a harm, then a code. Computing's own entries in this list — the Facebook contagion study, the Linux “hypocrite commits”, the Netflix de-anonymisation — are still recent enough that the rules they will produce are being written now.

PART 1 · FOUNDATIONS

The Belmont Principles — and Menlo's Fourth

Three principles that between them cover almost every question about human participants, plus one that computing needed.

BELMONT · PRINCIPLE 1

Respect for persons

Treat individuals as autonomous agents.

IN PRACTICE

Obtain informed consent. Give additional protection to those with diminished autonomy: children, prisoners, patients, employees, and the researcher's own students.

BELMONT · PRINCIPLE 2

Beneficence

Do no harm; maximise benefit, minimise harm.

IN PRACTICE

Carry out an explicit risk–benefit assessment, and minimise risk by design rather than by hoping. A study too small to detect an effect fails this principle, not merely the statistics.

BELMONT · PRINCIPLE 3

Justice

Distribute burdens and benefits fairly.

IN PRACTICE

Do not recruit a vulnerable group simply because it is convenient, and do not exclude a group that would benefit. Who bears the cost of the study, and who gets the result?

MENLO · PRINCIPLE 4

Respect for law and public interest

Menlo's addition, for computing research.

IN PRACTICE

Conduct research lawfully and transparently, and be accountable for its consequences. Directly relevant to network measurement, security testing and web-scale data collection.

The three Belmont principles plus Menlo's fourth are the highest-value four lines in this unit: almost any ethics question can be answered by working through them in order and saying what each one demands of the study in front of you.

Principles of Research Integrity

Beyond the treatment of participants, the researcher owes duties to the scientific record itself. Twelve principles, and every one of them has a corresponding failure mode.

Honesty

Report data, methods, results and status truthfully; do not embellish.

Objectivity

Avoid bias in design, analysis, interpretation and peer review; disclose conflicts.

Integrity

Keep agreements; act consistently with your stated commitments.

Carefulness

Avoid careless error; keep good records of data and of decisions.

Openness

Share data, code and materials; welcome criticism rather than deflecting it.

Respect for IP

Honour patents, copyrights and licences; give credit; never plagiarise.

Confidentiality

Protect participant data, submitted manuscripts and grant proposals.

Responsible publication

Publish to advance knowledge, not to build a CV; avoid duplicate publication.

Responsible mentoring

Educate and support students; do not exploit their labour.

Social responsibility

Consider the effects of the work on society; avoid foreseeable harm.

Non-discrimination

Judge colleagues by competence, not by identity.

Legality

Obey the relevant laws and institutional policies.

These are Shamoo and Resnik's principles, widely reproduced. For a five-mark answer, name six of them with one clause each — and pick the six you can attach a computing example to.

PART 2 · STAGE BY STAGE

Stage 1 — At the Design Stage

Four questions, asked before anything is collected. Three of them look like methodology questions, and they are — which is the point: a badly designed study is an unethical one.

1

Is the research question worth asking?

Wasting participants' time, or public money, is itself unethical. This is Unit I's criterion of a good problem, restated as an ethical obligation rather than a practical one.

WHAT COUNTS AS HUMAN-SUBJECTS RESEARCH

In computing, far more than students expect.

A questionnaire about developer practice. An eye-tracking study of code comprehension. Telemetry from an app. A usability test with five users. Interviews with maintainers. Analysis of commit logs that identify individuals.

Each of these involves people, and each normally requires review and consent — even when no one thinks of it as an experiment on humans.

2

Is the design capable of answering it?

A study too small to detect the effect it is looking for exposes participants to burden for no possible benefit. Under-powered research is not merely weak; it is unjustifiable.

3

Has a risk–benefit assessment been made?

Identify the risks — physical, psychological, social, reputational, legal, economic — estimate their likelihood, and show how the design minimises each. Write it down.

4

Has ethical clearance been obtained?

From the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) or Institutional Review Board (IRB), before any human data is touched. In India, human-participant research follows the ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017) where applicable. The board itself is the subject of the next slide.

PART 2 · STAGE BY STAGE

Ethical Review: The Role of the IRB and the IEC

DEFINITION

An Institutional Review Board (IRB) — called an Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) in India — is a standing committee that reviews research involving human participants before it begins, and satisfies itself that risks have been minimised and are reasonable against the expected benefits.

WHO SITS ON IT

Several members with scientific or methodological expertise.

At least one member whose primary concerns are non-scientific — an ethicist, a lawyer, a social scientist.

At least one member unaffiliated with the institution, so it is not judging only itself.

A chair and a member secretary, with quorum rules and written minutes.

Members recuse themselves from any protocol they are involved in.

WHAT IT REVIEWS

The protocol, and whether the design can actually answer the question.

The risk–benefit assessment, and the steps that minimise each risk.

Recruitment, and whether it is free from coercion or undue inducement.

The participant information sheet and the consent form, in the language they read.

The data-management plan, and any payment offered.

THREE LEVELS OF REVIEW

Exempt — minimal risk in defined categories. The board still issues the determination; the researcher never decides this alone.

Expedited — minimal risk, but identifiable data or direct contact. Reviewed by the chair or one or two members.

Full board — more than minimal risk, vulnerable participants, deception, or sensitive personal data. Reviewed at a convened meeting.

WHAT APPROVAL DOES NOT DO

It is prospective — data collected beforehand cannot be authorised afterwards. It covers only the protocol as submitted; a change of instrument, population or purpose needs an amendment. It does not transfer responsibility from the researcher. And it is no defence to the GDPR, HIPAA or the DPDP Act, which apply independently.

THE COMPUTING FAILURE MODE: “OUR STUDY HAS NO HUMAN SUBJECTS”

The Linux kernel case is exactly this: the university's IRB issued a not-human-subjects determination because the study examined a process. The maintainers were the subjects. Where a study observes, manipulates or deceives people — developers, maintainers, moderators, users — it is human-subjects research, whatever the data format is.

PART 2 · STAGE BY STAGE

Stage 2 — At the Data-Collection Stage: Consent

The obligations here derive almost entirely from Belmont's first principle, and the distinctions are examinable in exactly this form.

Informed consent

Participants must be told, in language they understand: the purpose, the procedure, the duration, the risks and benefits, who is funding it, how the data will be used and stored, and whom to contact. Consent must be documented.

TWO WORDS THAT ARE NOT SYNONYMS

Anonymity

Even the researcher cannot link the data to an individual. Once collected this way, it cannot be undone — and a participant cannot withdraw their data afterwards, because nobody can find it.

Confidentiality

The researcher can link them, but undertakes not to disclose. This is what most studies actually offer, and the undertaking then has to be backed by access control, encryption and a retention policy.

Promising anonymity and delivering confidentiality is a real and common breach.

Voluntary participation and the right to withdraw

No coercion and no undue inducement, and withdrawal at any time without penalty. Take special care where the researcher has power over the participant — a teacher recruiting their own students, a manager recruiting their own team.

Deception and debriefing

Permissible only when the research cannot otherwise be done, the risk is minimal, an ethics committee has approved it, and participants are debriefed afterwards and allowed to withdraw their data.

Vulnerable groups

Children, patients, prisoners, employees, and people with limited literacy or limited autonomy require additional safeguards — often a guardian's consent together with the participant's own assent.

Fair recruitment and compensation

Reimburse costs. Do not offer an amount so large that it overrides free choice: an inducement that nobody in the target population could refuse is a form of coercion.

PART 2 · STAGE BY STAGE

Stage 2 — Privacy, Data Protection and Harm

The obligations that survive after consent has been obtained — and the ones that a computing project is most likely to breach without noticing.

OBLIGATION 1

Data minimisation

Collect the minimum necessary — no more.

IN PRACTICE

Every extra field is a field you must secure, justify and eventually delete. “We might need it later” is not a purpose, and under most data-protection law it is not a lawful basis either.

OBLIGATION 2

Security and pseudonymisation

Store securely and encrypted; restrict access; pseudonymise early.

IN PRACTICE

Pseudonymise at the point of collection wherever the design allows, so that the identifying table exists separately and briefly. State a retention and deletion period, and honour it.

OBLIGATION 3

Legal compliance

The DPDP Act, 2023 in India; the GDPR for people in the EU; HIPAA for US health data.

IN PRACTICE

All three apply to research, with limited exemptions, and none is satisfied by a consent form alone. They are compared on the next slide — the regime is fixed by whose data it is, not by where you sit.

OBLIGATION 4

Avoidance of harm

Physical, psychological, social, reputational, legal and economic harm all count.

IN PRACTICE

In computing, the likeliest harms are informational: re-identification, exposure of behaviour, or a published dataset that endangers someone in a jurisdiction you were not thinking about.

A data-management plan is the artefact that ties all four together: what is collected, why, where it lives, who can reach it, how long it is kept, and how it is destroyed. Most funders now require one, and every project benefits from one.

Three Data-Protection Regimes

GDPR — General Data Protection Regulation (EU, 2018). HIPAA — Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (USA, 1996). DPDP Act — Digital Personal Data Protection Act (India, 2023).

BASISGDPR · EUHIPAA · USADPDP ACT · INDIA
What it covers
Personal data of people in the EU, processed by a controller anywhere.
Protected health information (PHI) held by providers, health plans and clearing houses, and their business associates.
Digital personal data processed in India, and processing abroad that offers goods or services in India.
Central mechanism
A lawful basis, purpose limitation, data minimisation, and data-subject rights.
The Privacy Rule for use and disclosure of PHI; the Security Rule for electronic PHI.
Consent-based processing by a Data Fiduciary, with itemised notice and a Consent Manager.
De-identification
Truly anonymous data falls outside; pseudonymous data does not.
Safe Harbor — remove the 18 listed identifiers — or Expert Determination.
Anonymised data falls outside the Act.
Research position
Permitted with safeguards and a lawful basis. Not a blanket exemption.
Research use of PHI needs participant authorisation, or a waiver from an IRB or Privacy Board.
Some research processing may be exempted by notification; consent otherwise required.
Maximum penalty
Up to EUR 20 million, or 4% of global annual turnover.
Tiered civil penalties; criminal penalties for wilful misuse.
Up to INR 250 crore per category of breach.

Which regime applies is fixed by whose data it is and what kind of data it is — not by where you sit. An Indian laboratory analysing European users' data is inside the GDPR; the same laboratory analysing US hospital records is inside HIPAA.

Stage 3 — At the Analysis and Reporting Stage

These are the obligations that protect the second object: the scientific record. Every one of them is a rule about what you do with results you did not want.

1

Report all results, including the negative and the inconvenient. A finding you suppress is a finding somebody else will spend a year rediscovering.

2

Never fabricate or falsify. And never selectively delete inconvenient runs or outliers without a stated, pre-specified rule.

3

Do not p-hack. Searching across tests, subgroups and thresholds until something is significant guarantees that something will be.

4

Do not HARK. Hypothesising After the Results are Known — presenting a post-hoc explanation as if it had been predicted.

5

State limitations and threats to validity. Their absence tells a reader either that you did not look, or that you did and chose not to say.

6

Do not over-claim. Distinguish sharply between what was demonstrated and what is conjectured. Unit II's proof modes are exactly this distinction.

7

Preserve raw data and analysis scripts. For the required retention period, in a form someone else could run. This is what makes a later challenge answerable.

Stage 4 — At the Publication Stage

Authorship is where most real disputes in a research group actually occur, and it is the one most students have never been told the rules for.

AUTHORSHIP — THE ICMJE CRITERIA

An author must satisfy all four: substantial contribution to conception, design, or data acquisition or analysis; drafting or critically revising the work; approval of the final version; and accountability for the work.

Gift / honorary authorship — Adding a senior person who did not contribute.

Ghost authorship — Omitting someone who did contribute — often a student.

Coercive authorship — A supervisor demanding inclusion as a condition.

No duplicate or redundant publication

And no simultaneous submission to two venues. One study, one report — extensions must state what is new.

Conflict of interest

Disclose funding, employment, consultancies, patents or personal relationships that could bias the work.

Peer-review ethics

A reviewer keeps the manuscript confidential, must not use its ideas, reviews promptly and fairly, and declines when conflicted.

The CRediT taxonomy — fourteen contributor roles — is now used by many publishers to record who did what. Agreeing the roles at the start of a project prevents almost all authorship disputes.

Correction and retraction

If an error is found after publication, issue a correction or a retraction. Concealing a known error is misconduct.

Avoid predatory venues and paper mills

Do not buy authorship or citations. Lecture 1's checks apply when submitting, not only when citing.

Research Misconduct: Fabrication, Falsification, Plagiarism

DEFINITION

Fabrication, falsification or plagiarism in proposing, performing or reviewing research, or in reporting research results. It does not include honest error, or genuine differences of opinion.

TYPEMEANINGEXAMPLE IN COMPUTING
Fabrication
Making up data or results, and recording or reporting them.
Reporting accuracy figures for experiments that were never run.
Falsification
Manipulating research materials, equipment or processes, or changing or omitting data so that the record is not accurate.
Dropping the test runs where the system crashed; deliberately leaving the baseline untuned; cropping a figure to hide the region where the method fails.
Plagiarism
Appropriating another's ideas, processes, results or words without credit.
Copying a related-work section, or a competitor's source code. Lecture 4 in full.

The phrase “it does not include honest error” matters. Getting a result wrong is not misconduct; knowing it is wrong and not saying so is. That is why the duty to correct and retract sits in the publication stage rather than here.

PART 3 · MISCONDUCT

Questionable Research Practices

These fall short of formal misconduct, no committee investigates them, and they corrode the record. In computing they are common — and most of them were introduced in Unit II as methodological errors.

Cherry-picking Selecting the results, datasets or baselines that flatter the proposed method, and reporting only those.

Unfair baselines Comparing your carefully tuned model against an untuned or outdated competitor. The single most common way an improvement is manufactured.

Test-set leakage Tuning hyper-parameters on the test set, or letting training data contain test items.

Reporting only the best run For a stochastic method, with no variance, no seeds and no count of how many runs were discarded.

HARKing and p-hacking Hypothesising after the results are known, and searching until something reaches significance.

Salami slicing and citation stacking Fragmenting one study across papers; excessive self-citation; reviewers demanding citations to their own work.

Withholding code and data Not releasing what would allow a refutation. Silence here is a choice, and an increasingly visible one.

The distinguishing feature is that each one is defensible in isolation and indefensible as a pattern. The defence against all seven is the same: pre-specify the analysis, and report what you did rather than what worked.

Worked Example: When Data Leakage Becomes Falsification

Situation: a project reports 99.4% accuracy on an imbalanced intrusion-detection dataset. The pipeline looks ordinary.

  1. 1
    Three separate leaks

THE PIPELINE AS WRITTEN

1. load full dataset
2. normalise all features
3. SMOTE to balance classes
4. select top-k features by score
5. train_test_split(0.8 / 0.2)
6. fit model, evaluate on test

Normalising before the split lets test statistics reach the model. Over-sampling before the split copies test rows into training. Selecting features on the whole dataset chooses them using the test labels.

  1. 2
    The number is not a measurement

99.4% is not an estimate of performance on unseen traffic. It is an estimate of performance on data the pipeline has already seen — a quantity nobody wanted to know.

  1. 3
    Why students do not notice

THE PIPELINE AS IT MUST BE

1. train_test_split(0.8 / 0.2)
2. fit normaliser on train only
3. SMOTE on train only
4. select features on train only
5. apply fitted transforms to test

Every individual step is standard, the library calls are correct, and nothing raises an error. The defect is entirely in the order, and the order is invisible in the results table.

  1. 4
    Where it crosses the line

Making the error is not misconduct. Reporting the number as a genuine result, once the error is known — or being told and not correcting the paper — is falsification, whatever the intention was.

In machine-learning coursework, data leakage is the single most frequent integrity failure — and the one students are least likely to recognise as one.

Data, Privacy and Consent in Computing Research

Four problems that a computing project meets and a clinical trial does not.

1

“Public” does not mean “consented”.

Data scraped from social media, forums or public repositories concerns real people who never agreed to be studied. Terms of service, robots.txt and rate limits must be respected, and re-publication of scraped personal data is usually unacceptable.

2

Anonymisation is weaker than it looks.

The Netflix Prize dataset was de-anonymised by cross-referencing public IMDb reviews; the AOL search-log release identified individuals from their queries alone. Consider k-anonymity, differential privacy and aggregation — and test for re-identification before release.

3

Secondary use has its own limits.

Check the licence and the original consent. Many medical and biometric datasets forbid redistribution or commercial use, and a dataset that was lawfully collected for one purpose is not thereby available for another.

4

User studies are human-subjects research.

Interviews, questionnaires, eye-tracking, screen recording and telemetry all involve human participants, and normally require ethics-committee approval and informed consent — including when the participants are your own classmates.

Worked Example: An Ethical Breach in a Medical-AI Project

Scenario: a research student builds a diagnostic model, scrapes patient records from a hospital's open database, and — to be a good open-science citizen — publishes the dataset on GitHub alongside the paper, without removing patient names.

1

WHAT SHOULD HAVE BEEN DONE, IN ORDER

Data-use agreement

Approach the hospital's data-access committee and get it in writing.

Ethics submission first

IEC/IRB approval before collecting anything, with a data-management plan and a consent waiver on record.

De-identify at source

Safe Harbor or Expert Determination; any key held separately and access-controlled.

Store properly

Encrypted, named access, and a stated retention and destruction date.

Release responsibly

Code plus synthetic or de-identified data, or managed access on request.

Declare it

An ethics statement naming the approving body and the approval number.

WHAT WAS BREACHED

Respect for persons

  1. 2
    The patients gave no informed consent to research use — and could not have, because they were never asked.

Ethical review

  1. 3
    No prospective clearance. The data was collected before any board saw the protocol, and that cannot be repaired afterwards.

HIPAA, or the DPDP Act

  1. 4
    Names are the first of the 18 Safe Harbor identifiers. Publishing them is an unauthorised disclosure of PHI; under the DPDP Act, a disclosure with no lawful basis.

The foreseeable harm — reputational, insurance, employment — falls on the patients. The benefit falls on the researcher.

  1. 5
    Beneficence

“Openly accessible” is not “licensed for redistribution”. Scraping against a site's terms is a breach separate from the privacy one.

  1. 6
    Terms of use

Note the trap: the student's motive was a good one. Reproducibility is itself an ethical obligation, and here it collides with privacy. When two obligations collide the answer is never to drop one — it is managed access, synthetic data, or a stated and justified exception.

Ethics in Security Research

The area where the line between research and a criminal offence is sharpest, and where it is drawn by a document rather than by intention.

ISSUE 1

Authorisation

Never test, scan or exploit a system you do not own or have written permission to test.

IN PRACTICE

Written scope and authorisation is the defining line between security research and an offence — in India, under the Information Technology Act, 2000. “I was only looking” is not a defence, and neither is “it was already insecure”.

ISSUE 2

Responsible disclosure

Report privately to the vendor, allow a remediation period, then publish.

IN PRACTICE

Ninety days is the common norm. Avoid releasing a working exploit for an unpatched system. Coordinate with a CERT where one is involved, and agree the timeline in writing before you start.

ISSUE 3

Dual-use research

Techniques for detecting malware also help write it.

IN PRACTICE

Consider whether the publication's benefit outweighs its potential for misuse, and consider staged release: the method now, the artefact later, or the artefact only to verified researchers.

ISSUE 4

Collateral harm

Measurement studies and honeypots can disrupt third parties.

IN PRACTICE

They can also endanger users in restrictive jurisdictions — which is why the Encore web-censorship measurement study, which loaded test requests in ordinary users' browsers, became a landmark ethics debate.

For a student project the rule reduces to one sentence: test only on infrastructure you control or have written permission for, and put that permission in the report.

Ethics of Artificial Intelligence and Data Science

Six concerns, all of which a reviewer of an applied machine-learning paper is now entitled to raise.

Bias and fairness

Models trained on historical data reproduce historical discrimination.

The COMPAS recidivism tool; face-recognition systems with far higher error rates for darker-skinned women. Report disaggregated performance, not only aggregate accuracy.

Transparency and explainability

High-stakes decisions require an explanation and a route to contest it.

Credit, hiring, medical diagnosis, parole. “The model said so” is not a reason a person can argue with.

Accountability

Who is responsible when an autonomous system causes harm?

The answer must be a person or an organisation. A system cannot bear responsibility, which is also why it cannot be an author.

Consent and provenance of training data

Respect for copyright and licences in scraped corpora.

Where the data came from, under what terms, and whether the people in it agreed — questions now being litigated rather than merely discussed.

Environmental cost

Report the compute and the energy used to train large models.

Increasingly expected in venue checklists. It is also a methodological disclosure: it tells a reader what it would cost them to reproduce your result.

Misuse

Deepfakes, automated disinformation, surveillance applications.

Consider a release strategy, and publish a documented statement of intended use — model cards for models, datasheets for datasets.

Reproducibility and Open Science

The newest of these obligations, and the one that has changed fastest: releasing code and data is no longer merely good practice.

1

Artifact evaluation

Major computing conferences now award badges — Artifact Available, Artifact Evaluated (Functional), Results Reproduced — for released code and data, assessed by a separate committee.

THE ETHICAL CLAIM, NOT MERELY THE PRACTICAL ONE

Releasing code and data is not simply good practice. Withholding them, so that a claim cannot be checked, is an ethical failure.

The reasoning connects straight back to Unit II. A claim is scientific because it can be refuted. If the only people who can attempt the refutation are the ones who made the claim, the result has been removed from the process that makes it knowledge.

There are legitimate reasons to withhold — personal data, licence terms, safety. State the reason; do not leave the silence to be interpreted.

2

FAIR principles for data

Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable. A persistent identifier, a licence, documented structure, and a repository that will still exist in five years.

3

Reproducibility checklists

The NeurIPS checklist and its equivalents: report seeds, hardware, the hyper-parameter search, the number of runs, the variance, and the compute budget.

4

Pre-registration

Registering the hypotheses and the analysis plan before collecting data. It structurally prevents HARKing and p-hacking, because the prediction is dated and public.

Four Cases Worth Knowing by Name

2014

Facebook emotional contagion

Researchers altered the news feeds of about 689,000 users to test whether emotional content is contagious, without specific informed consent. The defence was that the platform's terms of service permitted research.

Lesson: agreement to a terms-of-service document is not informed consent for an experiment.

2021

“Hypocrite commits” in the Linux kernel

Researchers deliberately submitted patches containing subtle vulnerabilities, to study whether the open-source review process would catch them. The maintainers were unwitting experimental subjects, and the university's IRB had wrongly deemed it not human-subjects research.

Lesson: in computing, the system is often maintained by people — studying the system can be studying them.

2008

Netflix Prize de-anonymisation

Netflix released 100 million “anonymised” film ratings for a public competition. Researchers matched them against public IMDb reviews and re-identified individual subscribers, exposing viewing histories. A sequel competition was cancelled after litigation.

Lesson: removing identifiers is not anonymisation — test for re-identification before release.

ONGOING

Fabrication and the retraction record

The Schön affair in physics — data fabricated across dozens of papers, all retracted, the doctorate revoked — and the growth of paper mills selling authorship. Retraction Watch documents thousands of retractions annually, a rising share for image manipulation.

Lesson: the record is self-correcting only because misconduct is eventually detected — and detection ends careers.

An Ethical Research Workflow

Ten steps that join the two halves of this unit into one process. Reproducible in an examination answer, and applicable to your own project unchanged.

1

Search. The literature systematically. Record search strings, databases and dates.

2

Screen. Using pre-stated inclusion and exclusion criteria. Report the flow (PRISMA).

3

Extract. Into a literature matrix; synthesise by theme, never paper by paper.

4

State the gap. Explicitly, with citations, and say why it matters.

5

Obtain clearance. Before touching any human data. Prepare consent forms and a data-management plan.

6

Collect. The minimum data necessary; secure it and pseudonymise it early.

7

Analyse. Using a pre-specified plan. Report everything, including the failures.

8

Write. In your own words, citing every borrowed idea. Run — and read — a similarity check.

9

Assign authorship. By contribution. Disclose funding, conflicts, and AI assistance.

10

Release. Code, data and artifacts, so that others can verify. Correct errors publicly if any are found.

Discussion

In pairs, ten minutes. Bring one answer back for class critique.

  1. 1
    Take your own project. Which Belmont principle is most at risk in it, and what specific design change would reduce that risk? If you believe none is at risk, say why — and be prepared to be argued with.
  2. 2
    You want to study how developers respond to code review. Your participants are students in the course you are teaching. Identify every ethical problem this creates, and say how each could be addressed.
  3. 3
    A classmate scrapes 50,000 public GitHub issues and plans to release the dataset with the usernames removed. Which regime applies, is that anonymisation, and what would you advise?
  4. 4
    You discover, after publication, that your reported accuracy was inflated by a leakage bug. Set out exactly what you are obliged to do, in order, and what would turn an honest error into misconduct.

RECAP · UNIT III

Key Takeaways

  1. 1
    Research ethics protects three things: the people the research touches, the integrity of the scientific record, and public trust. Almost every rule in the unit serves one of the three, and asking which is how you reconstruct it.
  2. 2
    The Belmont principles — respect for persons, beneficence, justice — plus respect for law and public interest, added by the Menlo Report for computing research, answer most questions about human participants.
  3. 3
    Ethical considerations run through every stage: clearance and risk–benefit at design; informed consent, voluntariness, confidentiality and data protection at collection; honest and complete reporting at analysis; and correct authorship, disclosure and no duplicate publication at publication.
  4. 4
    The IRB — the IEC in India — reviews the protocol, the risk–benefit case, recruitment, consent documents and the data-management plan, at one of three levels: exempt, expedited or full board. Approval is prospective, covers only what was submitted, transfers no responsibility, and is no defence to a law — the GDPR, HIPAA and the DPDP Act, 2023 apply independently, and publishing a dataset is itself a disclosure.
  5. 5
    Research misconduct is fabrication, falsification and plagiarism — and it excludes honest error. Questionable research practices such as cherry-picking, unfair baselines, data leakage and HARKing are almost as damaging and attract no investigation.
  6. 6
    Computing has its own terrain: the ACM and IEEE codes, scraped and re-identifiable data, authorised testing and responsible disclosure, dual-use research, AI bias and transparency, and the duty of reproducibility through released artifacts.
  7. 7
    Across the unit: a literature study discharges the obligation to know, ending in an explicit gap; ethical conduct discharges the obligation to be honest. Plagiarism is the failure at the junction — and both obligations exist because research is a conversation.
