## Slide 3 — The Four-Tier Regulatory Architecture

**Slide Content**
- **Why architecture matters:** in computing, where algorithms mediate decisions affecting welfare, finance, healthcare and civil liberties, **the gap between ethical aspiration and regulatory enforcement is the practical problem** — this slide names the layers that close it.
- **Tier 1 — international codes:** Nuremberg (1947), Declaration of Helsinki (1964, rev. 2013), Belmont Report (1979). *Moral authority, no direct enforcement.*
- **Tier 2 — professional society codes:** ACM Code of Ethics (2018), IEEE Code of Ethics. *Enforceable through membership and publication venues.*
- **Tier 3 — national law and guidance:** ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017), UGC Regulations (2018), DPDP Act (2023), IT Act (2000, am. 2008), CDSCO, MeitY. *Legally binding; penalties attach.*
- **Tier 4 — institutional bodies:** **Institutional Ethics Committees (IECs)** / IRBs / RECs, Research Integrity Offices, Departmental Research Committees. *Operational gatekeepers — they approve, refuse and monitor.*
- **Key vocabulary:** *IEC* — multidisciplinary body reviewing research involving human participants or sensitive data · *IRB* — the US equivalent, used interchangeably in the literature · *similarity index* — percentage of matched text reported by detection software · *research misconduct* — fabrication, falsification or plagiarism in proposing, performing, reviewing or reporting research (ORI definition).

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Teach the tiers by their *enforcement mechanism* rather than their chronology, because that is what explains why the Belmont Report shapes committee criteria while only the UGC Regulations can cancel a registration. Point out that the tiers interlock — a UPES-IEC decision applies Belmont principles through ICMR procedure, so an institutional refusal ultimately rests on a 1979 American document. Note that Tier 4 is the only layer students will personally interact with as researchers.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Dominant four-tier pyramid, each tier labelled with its instruments on the left and its *enforcement mechanism* on the right. Dock the vocabulary strip beneath as a full-width key-highlight panel.

## Slide 4 — Tier 1: The Foundational International Codes

**Slide Content**
- **The Nuremberg Code (1947):** arising from the trial of Nazi physicians, it established ten principles for human-subjects research, foremost **voluntary informed consent**. Written for biomedical research, its consent principle now undergirds modern data-protection law and CS user studies alike.
- **The Declaration of Helsinki (1964, last revised 2013):** issued by the World Medical Association, it extends Nuremberg and distinguishes **therapeutic from non-therapeutic** research. It is the most widely adopted international standard and is referenced by Indian institutional policy.
- **The Belmont Report (1979):** three principles that now structure most IEC review criteria — **respect for persons** (autonomy and consent), **beneficence** (minimise harm, maximise benefit), **justice** (fair distribution of burdens and benefits).
- **Why Belmont is the examinable one:** IEC review forms are effectively operationalised Belmont — consent procedures test respect for persons, risk–benefit analysis tests beneficence, and recruitment criteria test justice.
- **The transferability question:** all three were written for medical research, yet each applies directly to a CS usability study, a crowdsourced annotation task or an adversarial-testing protocol involving people.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Use the Belmont-to-review-form mapping as the central insight, because it converts three abstract nouns into the actual sections of a protocol students will one day submit. Address the transferability objection head-on — students argue that medical ethics is irrelevant to software, and the answer is that the object of protection is the participant, not the discipline. Note that the three Belmont principles are a near-certain multiple-choice item.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**

## Slide 5 — Tier 2: Professional Society Codes and the Limits of Enforceability

**Slide Content**
- **ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018) — general obligations:** contribute to society and human well-being · **avoid harm** · be honest and trustworthy · be fair and **take action not to discriminate** · respect privacy · honour confidentiality.
- **IEEE Code of Ethics:** commits members to treat all persons fairly and with respect, to refrain from harassment and discrimination, to avoid injuring others, and requires **responsible disclosure of factors that might endanger the public**.
- **Note the structure of both codes:** they impose **positive duties** (contribute, act against discrimination, disclose danger) and not merely prohibitions — an important difference from plagiarism regulation, which is almost wholly prohibitive.
- **Enforceability in practice:** these are professional codes, sanctioned through **membership status, publication venue policy and reputational consequence** rather than by law — which is why a code violation rarely reaches a court but can end a publication record.
- **Why they matter to computing specifically:** the ACM's "avoid harm" and anti-discrimination duties are the instruments under which algorithmic bias and unsafe deployment are argued as *ethics violations* rather than merely as engineering defects.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Emphasise the positive-duty structure, because students read all ethics as a list of prohibitions and consequently believe that doing nothing is always safe — the ACM code says otherwise. Use the enforceability point to set up a genuine essay question: comparing ACM and IEEE codes *on enforceability* is a standard long-answer item, and the honest answer is that both depend on community sanction. Preview that the Tay case on Slide 13 is diagnosed under the ACM "avoid harm" duty.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Two-column comparison of ACM and IEEE duties with shared obligations shown in the overlap. Beneath, an "enforcement reality" strip: membership → venue policy → reputation, with "courts" crossed out. Key-highlight chip: "Positive duties, not just prohibitions."

## Slide 6 — Tier 3: The Indian Regulatory Landscape

**Slide Content**
- **ICMR — National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017):** India's most comprehensive ethical standard and the source of IEC composition and procedure rules. ICMR has additionally issued dedicated ethical guidance on **artificial intelligence in biomedical research and healthcare**, addressing algorithmic transparency, dataset representativeness and bias mitigation — the provisions most directly relevant to CS.
- **UGC:** the **(Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism) Regulations, 2018** define plagiarism levels and penalties for higher-education institutions; **subsequent draft revisions** address similarity thresholds and generative-AI content.
- **MeitY:** issues policy on emerging technology, including the **National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (2018)** and the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** — the operative Indian data-protection statute.
- **CDSCO:** regulates clinical trials and **software as a medical device (SaMD)** — increasingly relevant where CS departments build AI/ML diagnostic tools.
- **IT Act, 2000 (amended 2008):** the legal foundation for prosecuting unauthorised data access, digital fraud and cybercrime — the statute under which a data-handling failure in research becomes a criminal rather than an academic matter.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Make the CDSCO point concrete, since students building a diagnostic classifier for a final-year project rarely realise they may be building a regulated medical device. Stress that the DPDP Act applies to their research data handling directly, not only to companies — personal data processed for a project is personal data processed. Tell them that naming the correct Indian instrument in an answer is a marking criterion, and that confusing UGC (academic integrity) with DPDP (data protection) is a common and costly error.

## Slide 7 — Institutional Ethics Committees: Purpose and Composition

**Slide Content**
- **Definition:** a formally constituted body — IEC, IRB or Research Ethics Board — that reviews research proposals for conformity with ethical standards **before** the research begins.
- **Four purposes:** **protection** of participants' rights and welfare · **assurance** that risks are minimised and reasonable relative to anticipated benefit · **verification** that informed-consent procedures are adequate · **oversight** of ongoing research through continuing review.
- **Composition under ICMR 2017:** multidisciplinary and gender-balanced, **minimum five and maximum twelve members** — a **chairperson external to the institution**, one or two basic medical scientists, one or two clinicians, a **legal expert or retired judge**, a **social scientist or philosopher**, a **layperson representing community interests**, and a **member secretary** handling day-to-day administration.
- **The CS-specific addition:** where proposals involve large-scale personal-data processing, the committee should additionally include a **data protection officer or information-security expert** — without whom the committee cannot competently assess re-identification risk.
- **Why multidisciplinarity is not bureaucratic decoration:** the external chair guards against institutional self-interest, the legal member reads consent documents as enforceable instruments, and the layperson tests whether disclosure is actually comprehensible to a participant.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Teach the composition by the function each member performs, since "why does multidisciplinary composition matter" is a stated examination item and a memorised list cannot answer it. Highlight the external chairperson requirement — a committee chaired from inside the institution reviewing its own institution's research has a structural conflict of exactly the kind Lecture 7 described. Note the layperson's role as the comprehension test from Lecture 7's consent components, now institutionalised.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Committee-table diagram with each seat labelled by role *and* by the question that member is there to ask. Flag the external chairperson seat and the CS-added data-protection seat in a contrasting colour. Key-highlight: "Five to twelve members, multidisciplinary, gender-balanced, externally chaired."

## Slide 8 — Review Levels and the Review Process

**Slide Content**
- **Three-tier review model:**

  | Review level | Trigger | Outcome |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Exempt** | Minimal risk; secondary use of de-identified data; survey of public officials | Notification of exemption |
  | **Expedited** | Minimal risk but not exempt; minor modification to an approved protocol | Approval by member secretary and one member |
  | **Full board** | Greater than minimal risk; vulnerable populations; **novel AI/ML systems involving personal data** | Decision at a convened quorum meeting |

- **Seven-step process:** **1 Submission** — proposal, consent forms, **data management plan**, recruitment materials and risk–benefit analysis · **2 Administrative screening** by the member secretary for completeness · **3 Primary reviewer assignment** to two or more members · **4 Board discussion** at quorum, with the investigator sometimes invited for questions · **5 Decision** — approve, modifications required, defer, or disapprove · **6 Notification** by formal letter, typically within **4–6 weeks** · **7 Continuing review** — annual progress reports, with any amendment requiring resubmission.
- **The examinable distinction:** exemption is a **determination the committee makes, not one the investigator may assume** — self-exempting a protocol is itself a procedural violation.
- **Plan for the timeline:** four to six weeks plus a possible modification cycle means ethics review must be initiated **at proposal stage**, not when data collection is about to begin.
- **Continuing review has teeth:** approval is time-bounded, and a protocol amended mid-study without resubmission is operating without approval.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Lead with the timeline consequence, because the most common practical failure is a student discovering in week ten that a study needed approval they never sought. Stress that novel AI/ML systems touching personal data land in full-board review — the triggering criterion students most need to recognise in their own project plans. Explain that "modifications required" is the most common outcome and is not a rejection, which removes some of the fear that causes people to avoid the process.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Upper half: the three-level table with the full-board row emphasised and the AI/ML trigger highlighted. Lower half: the seven-step process as a horizontal pipeline with the 4–6 week notification window marked and a feedback loop from "modifications required" back to submission.

## Slide 9 — The UPES Institutional Ethics Committee in Practice

**Slide Content**
- **What triggers UPES-IEC review:** research involving **human participants** (surveys, interviews, behavioural experiments, usability studies) · collection of **personally identifiable information** from data subjects · **animal subjects** (rare in CS but required for some HCI work involving living systems) · **environmental samples of biological origin** · **dual-use research of concern (DURC)**.
- **Submission route:** proposals are routed through the online **Research Management System (RMS)** portal rather than by direct approach to committee members.
- **Meeting cadence:** the committee convenes **quarterly**, with provision for **emergency review** of time-sensitive studies — so ordinary projects must be planned around the quarterly cycle.
- **What this means for a CS final-year project:** a usability study, a survey of developers, a crowdsourced annotation task, or an adversarial-testing protocol with human participants all require review; a purely computational study on a public, properly licensed, de-identified dataset generally does not.
- **DURC is listed deliberately:** research with legitimate civilian use that is also weaponisable — vulnerability-discovery tooling, disinformation-capable generative models, surveillance automation — is within the committee's remit even with no human participants at all.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Make the practical determination explicit, because the question students actually have is "does my project need this?" — and the answer turns on human participants, identifiable data, or dual-use potential. Emphasise the quarterly cadence as a planning constraint: a protocol submitted after the cycle closes may wait months, which is a project-management fact rather than an ethics one. Confirm the current RMS route and cadence against the live policy before stating them, since institutional procedures change between academic years.

Left: a decision tree — "Human participants? / Identifiable data? / Dual-use potential?" → IEC review required, else exempt determination. Right: a workflow strip (RMS submission → screening → quarterly meeting → decision) with the emergency-review branch shown as a dashed path.

## Slide 10 — Plagiarism Regulation: UGC 2018 and Its Revisions

**Slide Content**
- **UGC definition (2018):** *"the practice of taking someone else's work or ideas and passing them off as one's own"* — encompassing **verbatim copying without attribution**, **close paraphrasing without acknowledgement**, **uncited use of methodologies, datasets or algorithms**, **self-plagiarism** (recycling one's own published work without disclosure) and **collusion** (coordinated submission of identical work).
- **Note the third clause:** uncited use of *methodologies, datasets or algorithms* places CS artefacts squarely inside the regulation — a borrowed pipeline or dataset is regulated content, not just borrowed prose.
- **Similarity tiers and minimum penalties as set out in the course notes:**

  | Similarity index | Category | Minimum penalty |
  |---|---|---|
  | Up to 10% (excluding quotes, bibliography, small matches) | Minor | Warning; no penalty where properly cited |
  | 10–40% | Moderate | Denial of degree for students; withdrawal of paper for faculty |
  | Above 40% | Major | Expulsion; blacklisting of authors |

- **Proposed revisions under discussion:** exclusion of generative-AI output from similarity detection · **mandatory declaration** of AI-assisted content generation · stricter thresholds for doctoral theses · explicit treatment of **code plagiarism** using MOSS or JPlag.
- **Two structural points to retain:** the exclusions (quotes, bibliography, small matches) mean the *effective* index is what the regulation addresses; and thresholds are **floors for penalty**, so an institution may impose stricter limits than the UGC minimum but not laxer ones.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Before teaching the penalties, verify them against the gazette text of the Regulations, which set out graded levels with their own specified consequences and timelines — students are examined on exact figures, and a deck that diverges from the regulation will cost them marks. Emphasise the exclusions clause, because it connects directly to the effective-index skill taught in Lecture 5 and prevents panic over a raw percentage. Note that the generative-AI provisions are the live area and therefore the likely current-affairs examination item.

## Slide 11 — Ethical Dilemmas Distinctive to Computer Science

**Slide Content**
- **Informed consent in online studies:** participants may not read consent forms · **deception is sometimes methodologically necessary** (e.g. studying phishing susceptibility), requiring debriefing and committee justification · **withdrawal of data after collection is technically difficult** once records are anonymised and aggregated.
- **Data privacy and anonymisation:** datasets from social media, mobile devices and IoT frequently remain **re-identifiable**; simple anonymisation is rarely sufficient, which is why differential privacy, *k*-anonymity and federated learning exist as responses.
- **Algorithmic bias and fairness:** models trained on biased data **perpetuate and amplify** inequity — hiring tools that disadvantage women (the Amazon recruiting tool case, 2018), facial recognition with elevated error rates for women and people of colour, and predictive policing that disproportionately targets minority neighbourhoods.
- **Dual-use research of concern:** legitimate civilian research that is also weaponisable — generative models capable of disinformation, vulnerability-discovery tooling that enables attack, AI that automates surveillance.
- **Three further live tensions:** **authorship and credit disputes** (omitted contributors, honorary authorship, competing priority claims) · **whistleblowing** — loyalty to colleagues against responsibility to the public, with institutional protection aligned to the **Public Interest Disclosure and Protection of Informers Act, 2010** · **reproducibility and fabrication** — publication pressure driving selective reporting, p-hacking and, at the extreme, invented results.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Use the deception problem to show that ethics is not simply prohibition: some valid research questions cannot be studied with full prior disclosure, which is precisely why a committee exists to weigh justification, debriefing and harm rather than to apply a blanket rule. Make the withdrawal-after-anonymisation point explicit, since it is a genuine technical-ethical conflict — the privacy measure that protects participants also defeats their right to withdraw. Note that each dilemma maps to a code: bias to ACM's anti-discrimination duty, DURC to IEEE's public-endangerment disclosure duty.

## Slide 12 — The UPES Plagiarism Policy in Operation

**Slide Content**
- **Tooling:** **Turnitin** licensed for textual submissions, **Ouriginal** for cross-checking, and **MOSS** available for code submissions at the supervising faculty's discretion.
- **Acceptance thresholds by submission type** (excluding bibliography and quoted material): **M.Tech / PhD dissertations ≤ 10%** · **B.Tech project reports ≤ 20%** · **conference or journal publications from UPES ≤ 15%** (excluding self-citation).
- **Five-step workflow:** submit via the LMS integrated with Turnitin → receive the similarity report **within 24 hours** → supervisor reviews marked regions → revise and resubmit if the threshold is exceeded → final submission to the **Departmental Research Committee (DRC)**.
- **Escalating consequences:** **first offence** — written warning and mandatory resubmission · **second offence** — project grade of "F" and **six-month debarment** from re-registration · **third offence or major plagiarism above 40%** — expulsion, and revocation of degree if discovered after graduation.
- **Generative-AI policy:** use of generative AI for **grammar and code suggestion is permitted**, subject to **explicit declaration in the Acknowledgement section**; submitting AI-generated text as one's own original writing constitutes academic misconduct.
- **Worked example — Aditi's B.Tech report.** Initial index **22%**, of which **14%** is a methodology section closely paraphrasing a published survey without quotation marks. Threshold for B.Tech reports is 20%, so the submission fails. She rewrites the section, cites the survey and quotes technical definitions; the revised report returns **9%** and the DRC accepts it. Had she not revised, the project would have received an "F" and the supervisor would have been required to report the incident to the Departmental Disciplinary Committee.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Walk the Aditi example end to end, because it is the concrete answer to the question every student has — what actually happens, and how bad is it. Draw out the instructive detail: the 14% methodology paraphrase was fixable by citation and quotation, which means the offence was one of attribution rather than of writing ability. Confirm these thresholds, tools and the AI-declaration requirement against the current UPES policy before delivery, since operational specifics are revised between academic years and students will act on what the slide says.

## Slide 13 — Six Case Studies: Diagnosis and Lesson

**Slide Content**
- **Facebook emotional contagion (2014).** Cornell researchers with Facebook manipulated the news feeds of ~**689,000 users** to study emotional contagion; no consent was sought, on the basis of the platform's terms of service. *Issues:* absent informed consent, potential psychological harm, insufficient IRB oversight. *Outcome:* public and regulatory scrutiny; US HHS clarified that such social-media research can constitute human-subjects research requiring IRB review; the lead author issued a public apology. **Lesson: manipulating the user environment triggers ethical obligation even when the data already exist.**
- **Cambridge Analytica (2018).** Personal data of ~**87 million** Facebook users harvested via a personality-prediction app and used for political micro-targeting. *Issues:* consent violation, use beyond stated purpose, mass privacy breach. *Outcome:* investigations in the US, UK and EU, congressional hearings, a **$5 billion FTC penalty**, and impetus for GDPR enforcement and for India's DPDP Act, 2023. **Lesson: verify the chain of consent for all secondary data, especially across jurisdictions.**
- **Theranos (2003–2018).** Claimed finger-prick blood diagnostics; whistleblowers alleged non-functional technology and fabricated results. *Outcome:* criminal prosecution of the founders, SEC and FDA investigation, dissolution. **Lesson: reporting model accuracy unsupported by experiment is the same category of misconduct.**
- **Henrietta Lacks / HeLa cells (1951; genome published 2013).** Cells taken without knowledge or consent; the genome later published without consulting the family. *Outcome:* the NIH established a HeLa Genome Data Access Working Group including family members. **Lesson: data derived from identifiable sources retain ethical obligations indefinitely.**
- **Microsoft "Tay" (2016)** — a learning Twitter chatbot driven to offensive output by coordinated adversarial input within ~16 hours; withdrawn with an apology. **Lesson: interactive AI requires red-teaming and pre-deployment safeguards — ACM's "avoid harm" applies at deployment, not only at design.** · **Indian doctoral thesis case (2016)** — approximately **60%** of a thesis at a premier institution found plagiarised via Turnitin and manual review; registration cancelled under UGC Regulations and the supervisory committee scrutinised. **Lesson: supervisors share responsibility, and checks belong throughout the research lifecycle, not only at submission.**

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Run this slide as cold-call diagnosis — give the facts, ask which principle and which regulatory instrument applies, and reveal the lesson afterwards. Draw the connecting thread aloud: in five of six cases the harm was foreseeable and the missing element was an independent review that nobody sought. Spend the extra minute on the Indian thesis case, since it is the one with direct personal consequence for this cohort and it establishes that supervisors are also accountable.

## Slide 14 — Summary & Discussion

**Slide Content**
- **Five takeaways:** research ethics is enforced through a **four-tier architecture** — international codes (Nuremberg, Helsinki, Belmont), professional codes (ACM, IEEE), national frameworks (ICMR, UGC, DPDP Act, IT Act) and institutional bodies (IECs, IRBs, DRCs) · **IECs are the operational pivot**, with composition and procedure set by ICMR standards and implemented at UPES through the UPES-IEC · **plagiarism policy** operates through similarity thresholds and tiered consequences, with generative AI the live area of revision · **CS dilemmas** centre on online consent, re-identification, algorithmic bias, dual use, authorship and reproducibility · **case studies** show that violations are typically foreseeable and that independent review is the missing element.
- **Discussion question 1:** Analyse the Facebook emotional contagion study using the **Belmont framework** — which principles were violated, and how might an IEC review have altered the study design?
- **Discussion question 2:** Compare the ACM and IEEE codes. **To what extent are they enforceable in practice**, and what does that imply for relying on them?
- **Discussion question 3:** Examine Cambridge Analytica under the **DPDP Act, 2023** — what liabilities would researchers face if a comparable incident occurred today at an Indian institution?
- **Discussion question 4:** Can informed consent be meaningfully obtained for large-scale pre-existing datasets, and if not, what should substitute for it? · **Question 5:** Where should the line fall on dual-use research — should a vulnerability-discovery tool be publishable?
- **Closing synthesis:** across eight lectures this unit moved from *why literature matters*, through *how to analyse it*, to *the obligations that make the resulting knowledge trustworthy* — and this lecture supplies the machinery by which those obligations are enforced.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Work discussion question 1 aloud, since it is the archetypal ten-mark item: respect for persons was violated by absent consent, beneficence by unassessed psychological risk, and an IEC would likely have required consent, a debriefing protocol and a harm-monitoring plan. Use question 2 to reward honest argument — the defensible answer concedes limited formal enforceability while arguing that venue and reputational sanction are real. Close by naming the unit's arc so students see eight lectures as one argument rather than eight topics.

## Slide 15 — Exam Preparation & References

**Slide Content**
- **Memorise the numbers:** UGC and UPES similarity thresholds with their penalty tiers · IEC size (**five to twelve members**) · the notification window (**4–6 weeks**) · the UPES submission thresholds by degree type. These are asked as exact figures.
- **Know the named documents cold:** **Nuremberg Code (1947)** · **Declaration of Helsinki (1964, rev. 2013)** · **Belmont Report (1979)** and its three principles · **ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017)** · **UGC Regulations (2018)** · **DPDP Act (2023)** · **ACM Code (2018)** · **IT Act (2000, am. 2008)**.
- **Master the distinctions:** exempt vs expedited vs full-board review · Nuremberg vs Helsinki · plagiarism vs self-plagiarism vs collusion · IEC vs IRB (jurisdictional, not substantive).
- **Practise case analysis in four moves:** identify the ethical issue → **name the governing code or regulation** → state what review or disclosure was owed → recommend the remedy. Always link a dilemma to a specific instrument — Tay to ACM's "avoid harm", Cambridge Analytica to DPDP/GDPR, the thesis case to UGC 2018.
- **Expect a current-affairs slant** on generative AI and DPDP Act enforcement, and be able to explain **why IEC multidisciplinary composition matters** rather than merely listing its members.
- **References:** ICMR (2017) · UGC Regulations (2018) · WMA Declaration of Helsinki (2013) · The Belmont Report (1979) · ACM Code of Ethics (2018) · DPDP Act (2023) · UPES Research Ethics and Academic Integrity Policy · Kramer, Guillory & Hancock (2014), *PNAS* 111(24), 8788–8790 · Cadwalladr & Graham-Harrison (2018), *The Guardian*.
