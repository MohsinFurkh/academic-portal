PART 1 · WHY WE REVIEW

Research Is Cumulative — and That Creates Two Obligations

No result is created from nothing. Every contribution is built on work that already exists, and that single fact generates the whole of this unit.

OBLIGATION 1

To know

Before claiming a contribution, you must know what the community already knows.

Discharged through a literature study — Lectures 1 to 3.

OBLIGATION 2

To be honest

You must credit what you took, report what you actually found, and cause no harm to the people and systems your research touches.

Discharged through ethical conduct — Lectures 4 and 5.

WHY THESE TWO SIT IN ONE UNIT

Research is a conversation, and a conversation requires that you listen before you speak and that you speak truthfully. The two obligations are one principle seen from two sides.

Plagiarism is precisely the failure that occurs at the junction of the two. It is simultaneously a failure of literature practice — you used a source — and a failure of ethics — you hid it. That is why it cannot be taught as a separate topic from either.

PART 1 · WHY WE REVIEW

What a Literature Review Actually Is

DEFINITION

A systematic, critical and reproducible examination of the published scholarly work relevant to a research problem, carried out to establish what is already known, what is disputed, what is missing, and where the proposed research fits.

WORD 1

Systematic

It follows a stated method — not the accident of which papers you happened to find. The method exists on paper before the searching starts, which is what stops the search from drifting towards the conclusion you already want.

Can you write down what you did?

WORD 2

Critical

It evaluates and compares rather than merely listing. Every paper is placed against the others: agreeing, contradicting, superseding. A review that never uses the word “however” has not been critical.

Have you judged, or only reported?

WORD 3

Reproducible

Another researcher following your stated search should arrive at substantially the same set of papers. This is the same standard Unit II demanded of an experiment, applied to the act of reading.

Could someone else land where you landed?

Three words, three tests. A review that fails any one of them is a reading list with paragraphs around it — and a reviewer will say so.

PART 1 · WHY WE REVIEW

Four Things a Literature Review Is Not

Each of these is a real piece of work. None of them is a literature review, and submitting one in its place is the commonest structural failure in a student dissertation.

WHAT STUDENTS SUBMITWHAT IT ACTUALLY ISWHY IT FAILS AS A REVIEW
An annotated bibliography
A list of sources, each with a short description of its contents, in alphabetical or chronological order.
It is organised by paper. A review must be organised by idea, because only ideas can be compared, and only comparison produces a gap.
A summary chain
A sequence of paragraphs each beginning “X et al. proposed …”, running until the papers run out.
It demonstrates reading without demonstrating thinking. Nothing in it is yours, so it cannot lead anywhere.
A search-engine dump
Whatever the first two pages of Google Scholar returned for the obvious query, cited in the order found.
Not systematic and not reproducible: the result depends on the ranking algorithm on the day, which nobody can re-run.
A formality before the “real” work
A chapter written last, to fill the space the template demands between the introduction and the methodology.
Backwards. The review is what tells you the problem is worth attacking and which baselines you must beat. Written afterwards, it can only justify what you already did.

The diagnostic question a reviewer asks: does this chapter end in a claim that the rest of the thesis is obliged to defend? If it ends in a list, the answer is no.

Ten Purposes of a Literature Study

A standard five-mark question. Write six to eight of these as a bold phrase followed by one sentence — never as a paragraph.

1

Establish the state of the art. What is currently the best known result, method or system for this problem?

2

Identify the research gap. The unanswered question. This is the justification for your work, and it links straight back to Unit I.

3

Avoid duplication. Repeating published work without knowing it wastes your time and destroys your novelty.

4

Refine the problem. A vague problem becomes precise once you see how others have formulated it.

5

Choose the methodology. The literature tells you which datasets, metrics and protocols the community will accept.

6

Identify the baselines. In computing you must beat the current best method. The literature is what tells you what that is.

7

Build the vocabulary. Standard definitions, notation and the theoretical framework all come from the literature.

8

Avoid known pitfalls. Others have already reported which approaches fail, and why. Reading saves you the failure.

9

Demonstrate competence. A reviewer judges your command of the field largely by your related-work section.

10

Support interpretation. At the end, you explain your results by agreeing or disagreeing with prior findings.

PART 2 · WHAT COUNTS AS LITERATURE

The Review Happens Three Times, Not Once

Treating it as a single chapter written once is the reason so many reviews are stale by submission.

1

Preliminary

BEFORE THE RESEARCH

To find the problem and justify it. This is the review that decides whether the project is worth starting — and it is the only one that can still save you from a topic that was settled in 2019.

2

Ongoing

DURING THE RESEARCH

To track newly published work and to solve methodological difficulties as they arise. Set database alerts on your search strings on the day you finalise them.

3

Integrative

AFTER THE RESEARCH

To interpret and position what you found. Your discussion section is a literature review: it explains your result by agreeing or disagreeing with specific prior findings.

→

→

THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE

Re-run every search before you submit, and record the date you ran it. In a fast-moving area, a review finalised eighteen months before submission will have missed the paper that a reviewer considers the obvious competitor — and “we were unaware of it” is not an answer that survives.

PART 2 · WHAT COUNTS AS LITERATURE

Worked Example: Three Ways a Project Dies Before a Line of Code

Proposal: “A new hybrid CNN–LSTM model for network intrusion detection.”

A LITERATURE FAILURE

The architecture was published in 2019 and the student did not know.

There is no novelty to claim. The work may be executed perfectly and still be unpublishable, because the contribution has already been made by someone else. One afternoon in DBLP would have caught it.

WHAT THE THREE HAVE IN COMMON

Every one of them is fatal, every one of them is invisible from inside the project, and every one of them is discovered by someone else.

Any one is enough to have the paper rejected, the degree withheld, or the publication retracted. None of the three is detected by working harder on the model.

All three are caught by the same habit: read the primary literature first, and write down where every sentence and every dataset came from.

AN INTEGRITY FAILURE

The related-work section is assembled from a survey paper.

This is plagiarism whether or not the survey is cited, because the words and the judgements are the survey author's. It also means the student has not read the primary work and has inherited that author's errors.

AN ETHICS FAILURE

The dataset is real campus traffic, collected without consent.

Network traces contain the browsing behaviour of identifiable people. Collected without approval or consent, the data cannot lawfully be used, and no result derived from it can be published.

How a Result Travels Through the Literature

Knowing the pipeline tells you where to look for a given kind of information — and how much of it has been checked.

Preprint

ARXIV, WITHIN DAYS

Unrefereed. Fast, current, and entirely the authors' claim.

Workshop

MONTHS

Lightly refereed. Early ideas, negative results, position papers.

Conference

6–12 MONTHS

Peer-reviewed and, in top CS venues, highly selective. The main record.

Journal

1–3 YEARS

Extended, more thoroughly refereed. Archival, and often a superset of the conference paper.

Survey

3–7 YEARS

Secondary. Organises a settled area. Useful as a map, never as a citation for a fact.

Textbook

5–15 YEARS

Tertiary in effect. What the field agrees on once the argument is over.

Four Types of Literature, by Source

The classification is not bookkeeping. It determines what a source may be cited for, and what it may not.

TYPEMEANINGEXAMPLES IN COMPUTINGCITE IT FOR
Primary
Original reports of research, written by the people who did it.
Conference papers, journal articles, theses, patents, technical reports, RFCs, standards, source code, datasets.
Facts, methods, results. This is what a review is made of.
Secondary
Work that summarises, organises or interprets primary sources.
Surveys, systematic reviews, textbooks, review articles, encyclopaedic entries.
Orientation and taxonomy — never for a specific result.
Tertiary
Work that indexes or points to the other two.
Bibliographic databases (DBLP, Scopus), indexes, directories, tertiary studies — reviews of reviews.
Finding things. Almost never cited in the text itself.
Grey literature
Material not formally peer-reviewed, but often important and often first.
arXiv preprints, white papers, industrial lab blog posts, documentation, StackOverflow, GitHub issues.
Practice, tooling and very recent claims — always flagged as unrefereed.

In software engineering especially, industrial practice leads publication by years. Excluding grey literature entirely is itself a source of bias — which is why systematic grey-literature reviews are now a recognised study type.

PART 2 · WHAT COUNTS AS LITERATURE

The Secondary-Source Trap

Building a review out of surveys is the fastest way to produce a chapter that looks complete and is worthless.

THE RULE

Cite what you have read.
If a survey points you to a paper, read that paper and cite it directly.

THE LEGITIMATE USES OF A SURVEY

As a map

Read it first to learn the vocabulary, the sub-areas and the names of the groups working in each. This is the single best use of an afternoon at the start of a project.

As a bibliography

Mine its reference list. Every paper it cites is a candidate for your backward-snowballing pass.

As a citable claim about the field

“Surveys of the area identify three families of approach [8]” is a legitimate citation — it is a claim about the literature, which is what a survey is an authority on.

As a check on your own coverage

When your search is finished, compare your included set against the survey's. Papers it has and you do not are a signal that your search string is too narrow.

  1. 1
    You inherit the survey author's errors.

Every survey compresses, and compression loses conditions. A result stated with three caveats in the original will appear in the survey with one, and in your review with none.

  1. 2
    You inherit the survey author's biases.

A survey is a narrative review with a wider scope. Its selection reflects its author's judgement — and now, invisibly, yours.

  1. 3
    You risk plagiarising the phrasing.

If you never saw the primary paper, the only words available to you are the survey's. Mosaic plagiarism follows almost automatically.

  1. 4
    You cannot find the gap.

The survey already stated the gap as its author saw it, two years ago. A gap you copied is not a justification for your work; it is a justification for theirs.

PART 2 · WHAT COUNTS AS LITERATURE

Grey Literature: When It Counts and When It Does Not

Computer science has a larger, faster and more consequential grey layer than almost any other discipline. Pretending otherwise produces a review that is out of date on the day it is written.

USE IT

The work is genuinely recent and no refereed version exists yet — common in machine learning, where the arXiv version leads the conference version by a year.

The claim is about practice: what tools industry uses, what failures operators actually see, how a system is deployed.

The source is the authoritative one: an RFC, a W3C recommendation, the maintainers' own documentation, a released dataset card.

You are studying the grey literature itself, as a systematic grey-literature review does in empirical software engineering.

DO NOT USE IT

As evidence for a quantitative result that a refereed paper also reports — cite the refereed version, which has been checked.

As the sole support for a central claim of your own work. A reviewer will ask who refereed it, and the answer will be nobody.

When the source is a vendor describing its own product, unless you are explicitly discussing vendor claims as claims.

When a refereed version exists and differs. The published version is the record; the preprint may contain errors the referees removed.

Whatever you decide, state the decision in the protocol and apply it to every candidate. An inclusion rule invented halfway through screening is not a rule; it is a preference.

PART 3 · FINDING AND JUDGING

Where to Search: Six Families of Source

No single database covers computer science. A search run in one place is a search with a known and unmeasured hole in it.

Publisher databases

Full text, held by the people who published it.

IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect, Wiley Online Library.

Indexing databases

Metadata and citations across publishers.

Scopus, Web of Science, DBLP, Semantic Scholar, Google Scholar.

Preprint servers

Unrefereed, and months to years ahead.

arXiv (cs.LG, cs.CV, cs.CR, cs.SE, …), and bioRxiv for interdisciplinary work.

Standards and specifications

The normative text, not a description of it.

IETF RFCs, ISO/IEC, IEEE standards, W3C recommendations.

Artifacts

The code, data and models themselves.

GitHub, Zenodo, Papers with Code, Kaggle, the UCI Machine Learning Repository.

Institutional repositories

Theses and locally held work.

Shodhganga (Indian ETD repository), NDLI, university digital libraries.

Minimum defensible practice for a CS review: at least two publisher databases, at least one index that crosses publishers, DBLP for venue completeness, and arXiv for currency. Record which, and the date.

Three Indexes, Three Different Answers

Students treat these as interchangeable. They are not: each makes a different trade between coverage and curation, and the trade shows up directly in your screening workload.

INDEXWHAT IT COVERSSTRENGTHWHAT IT COSTS YOU
DBLP
Computer-science bibliography: venues and author records, curated by hand, essentially complete for CS.
Venue completeness and clean author disambiguation. The fastest way to see everything a group has published.
No abstracts, no full-text search, no citation counts. It finds venues and people, not topics.
Scopus / Web of Science
Curated, indexed metadata across publishers, with citation links and subject classification.
Reproducible Boolean search with exportable results. This is what an SLR protocol is normally written against.
Subscription access, and incomplete coverage of conferences and of newer or regional venues.
Google Scholar
Everything it can crawl, including preprints, theses, slides, and predatory output.
Recall, full-text matching, and the “Cited by” link that makes forward snowballing trivial.
No stable result set, no export of a full search, and a great deal of noise. It is not a protocol-grade source.
Semantic Scholar
Crawled and enriched corpus with extracted citations, contexts and influence measures.
Citation context — it shows you the sentence in which a paper was cited, which is a genuine reading aid.
Coverage and metadata quality vary by field; the derived measures are proprietary and change.

Use Scopus or Web of Science for the protocol search you will report, DBLP to verify venue coverage, and Google Scholar for snowballing. Reporting a systematic review whose only source was Google Scholar is a reviewer's first objection.

In Computer Science, Conferences Carry the Weight

A DISCIPLINE-SPECIFIC FACT WORTH STATING IN AN ANSWER

Unlike most disciplines, in computing the top conferences are more selective
and more current than most journals. A CS review citing only journals is incomplete.

1

HOW TO JUDGE A VENUE

Acceptance rate

Published by most top conferences. Rates in the 15–25% range indicate genuine selection; a rate above 60% usually indicates none.

CORE ranking

A*, A, B, C for conferences, maintained by the Computing Research and Education Association of Australasia. Widely used and freely checkable.

Indexing

Is the venue indexed in Scopus or Web of Science? Is the publisher one of IEEE, ACM, Springer, Elsevier, USENIX?

Programme committee

Look at the names. If you recognise none of them and none are at institutions you recognise, that is information.

Machine learning

NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI

Vision and graphics

CVPR, ICCV, ECCV, SIGGRAPH

Systems and networking

OSDI, SOSP, NSDI, SIGCOMM

Software engineering

ICSE, FSE, ASE, ISSTA

Security and privacy

IEEE S&P, CCS, USENIX Security, NDSS

Databases and theory

VLDB, SIGMOD, STOC, FOCS

PART 4 · CREDIBILITY

Seven Tests for the Credibility of a Source

Not every published item deserves equal weight. Apply these in order; the first two dispose of most of the problem.

1

Peer review. Was it refereed, and by whom? A single-blind conference programme committee, a journal's referees, and nobody at all are three very different levels of scrutiny.

2

Venue quality. Is the journal indexed in Scopus or Web of Science? What is the conference's acceptance rate or CORE ranking? An unindexed venue with no stated rate is a warning.

3

Authority. Are the authors and the institution known in this area? This is weak evidence on its own and strong evidence in combination with the rest.

4

Currency. In deep learning or security a five-year-old result may be obsolete; in complexity theory it may be definitive. Currency is judged relative to the sub-field, not by the calendar.

5

Citations. A highly cited paper is usually important — but citation count grows with age, so compare only within the same year, and check whether the citations are agreeing or refuting.

6

Evidence. Are the claims supported by data, proofs or released code? Is the evaluation reproducible? This is Unit II's question, asked of somebody else's paper.

7

Bias and funding. Who paid for the study, and who benefits from the result? A vendor benchmark of the vendor's own product is evidence of something, but not of what it claims.

PART 4 · CREDIBILITY

Worked Example: Appraising Two Sources in Ten Minutes

Situation: two papers report a transformer intrusion detector at above 99% accuracy on CIC-IDS2017. Which one do you cite?

SOURCE A

venue : ICSE-adjacent workshop, refereed
indexed : Scopus, IEEE
authors : group with 6 prior papers in the area
year : 2024
citations : 11
code / data : GitHub link, public dataset, seeds given
evaluation : 5 runs, mean and s.d., latency
funding : national research council grant

VERDICT

Cite, and treat as a baseline you must beat. The released artifacts mean the claim is checkable, and the reported variance means it is a measurement rather than a best run.

SOURCE B

venue : journal, unheard of, APC on submission
indexed : “indexed in Google Scholar”
authors : no other publications in the area
year : 2024
citations : 3, all self-citations
code / data : none, dataset “available on request”
evaluation : one run, accuracy only, no baseline tuned
funding : not declared

VERDICT

Do not cite as evidence. Four independent signals of a predatory venue, and an evaluation that reports a single number with no variance, no baseline and no artifact. Nothing in it can be checked.

Neither judgement required reading the method. Provenance, artifacts and reported variance are visible in under ten minutes, and they filter most of what should never have entered your candidate set.

PART 4 · CREDIBILITY

Predatory Publishing

Publishers that charge a fee and print almost anything, with no genuine peer review. They are a problem for you twice over: as a place not to submit, and as a source not to cite.

WARNING SIGNS

Unsolicited email invitations, often flattering and often addressed to the wrong field.

Publication promised within days or weeks of submission.

A very wide and unrelated scope — “Computing, Management and Biosciences”.

Fake or invented metrics: “Global Impact Factor”, “Cosmos IF”, unverifiable numbers.

No identifiable editorial board, or listed editors who did not consent.

No indexing in Scopus, Web of Science, DOAJ or UGC-CARE.

Article processing charges disclosed only after acceptance.

1

WHAT TO CHECK, BEFORE SUBMITTING OR CITING

Think. Check. Submit.

A short, free checklist covering the questions a legitimate publisher can always answer.

DOAJ

The Directory of Open Access Journals: inclusion requires demonstrated editorial process.

UGC-CARE

The reference list maintained for Indian higher education. Publication outside it may not be counted.

Scopus / WoS source lists

Both publish their title lists. If the journal claims indexing, verify it against the list itself.

Citing predatory-journal papers weakens your review, because you are asking your reader to accept a claim that no referee ever examined. Publishing in one is worse: under most institutional rules it does not count at all.

PART 4 · CREDIBILITY

The Metrics, and What They Do Not Mean

Every one of these is a count with a formula attached. Each is useful for something narrow and misused for something broad.

METRICWHAT IT COUNTSLEGITIMATE USEHOW IT IS MISUSED
Citation count
Times this paper has been cited, by whatever the index crawls.
Spotting the seminal papers of an area, compared within the same publication year.
As a proxy for quality. Retracted papers keep accruing citations, and refutations cite the thing they refute.
h-index
The largest h such that the author has h papers with at least h citations each.
A crude, single-number summary of a sustained record.
Comparing across fields and career stages, where it measures mostly age and sub-field size.
Impact factor / SJR / CiteScore
Mean or weighted citations per paper for a journal over a window.
A property of the journal, useful when choosing where to submit.
As a property of a paper in it. The distribution is extremely skewed: most papers in a high-IF journal are cited far below its IF.
Altmetrics
Mentions in news, policy documents, social media and reference managers.
Evidence of attention and reach, which is a real and distinct thing.
As evidence of correctness. Attention and validity are uncorrelated, and sometimes worse than uncorrelated.

The San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA), signed by most major publishers and funders, exists specifically to stop journal-level metrics being used to judge individual papers and individual people.

PART 4 · CREDIBILITY

A Citation Has a Shelf Life

The published record is corrected continuously, and the correction does not reach the copy of the PDF on your disk.

WHY THIS IS A CREDIBILITY QUESTION

The scientific record is self-correcting only because someone reads the correction.

A retracted result that is still cited is still, operationally, part of the field's knowledge. Every author who cites it without checking extends its life, and the error propagates into work that never touched the original data.

This is also why Lecture 5 treats concealing a known error as misconduct rather than embarrassment: the cost falls on everyone who cites you afterwards.

  1. 1
    Papers get retracted, and keep being cited.

A retracted paper remains downloadable, remains indexed, and continues to accumulate citations for years afterwards — the great majority of them from authors who did not know. Your reference list is where that chain stops or continues.

  1. 2
    Corrections and errata are not retractions.

Many papers carry a published correction that changes a number, a figure or a claim. If you are citing the specific quantity that was corrected, you must cite the corrected value.

  1. 3
    Preprints are revised.

An arXiv entry has versions. Cite the version you read, with its identifier, and check whether a later version or a refereed publication supersedes it before you submit.

  1. 4
    Check before the final draft.

Retraction Watch maintains a public database, Crossref carries retraction metadata, and most publishers stamp the PDF. Re-checking your reference list costs an hour and prevents an indefensible citation.

PART 5 · PRACTICE

A Checklist for the Start of Any Review

✓

Write the review questions before you search. Two or three of them, in answerable form. Everything downstream — the search string, the criteria, the extraction form — derives from these.

✓

Search at least four places. Two publisher databases, one cross-publisher index, DBLP for venue completeness. Record the exact string and the date for each.

✓

Read primary sources. Use surveys as maps and bibliographies. Never cite a result you have only seen quoted in someone else's paper.

✓

Decide your grey-literature rule in advance. Include it or exclude it, but write the rule down and apply it to every candidate, not to the ones you happen to like.

✓

Check the venue before you read the paper. Indexing, acceptance rate or CORE rank, and editorial board. Ten seconds of checking saves an hour of reading something you cannot cite.

✓

Re-run everything before submission. Then check your reference list against Retraction Watch and against Crossref retraction metadata.

PART 5 · PRACTICE

Discussion

In pairs, ten minutes. Bring one answer back for class critique.

  1. 1
    Take the topic of your own project. Name the two conferences and one journal where its central result would most plausibly appear — and say how you determined that, without guessing.
  2. 2
    Find a survey in your area published three or more years ago. Pick one claim it makes about the state of the art. Is that claim still true, and what would you have to read to know?
  3. 3
    You find a 2024 arXiv preprint that solves exactly your problem, with no refereed version. What do you do with it — cite it, ignore it, or change your project? Defend your answer.
  4. 4
    A paper in your candidate set reports 99.2% accuracy, releases no code, and appears in a journal you have never heard of. Work through the seven credibility tests aloud and reach a decision.

RECAP · UNIT III · LECTURE 1

Key Takeaways

  1. 1
    Research is cumulative, and that creates two obligations: to know what has already been done, and to be honest about what you took and what you found. Plagiarism is the failure that sits at the junction of the two.
  2. 2
    A literature review is systematic, critical and reproducible. It is not an annotated bibliography, not a summary chain, not a search-engine dump, and not a chapter written last to fill a template.
  3. 3
    It happens three times — before the research to justify it, during to keep it current, and after to interpret the findings. Re-run every search before submission and record the date.
  4. 4
    Sources are primary, secondary, tertiary and grey. Cite what you have read: surveys are maps and bibliographies, not evidence for a specific result.
  5. 5
    No single database covers computer science, and in computing the top conferences are more selective and more current than most journals. Search several places, and report which.
  6. 6
    Credibility is judged on peer review, venue, authority, currency, citations, evidence and funding. The metrics count things; none of them measures whether a paper is right — and a citation has a shelf life.
