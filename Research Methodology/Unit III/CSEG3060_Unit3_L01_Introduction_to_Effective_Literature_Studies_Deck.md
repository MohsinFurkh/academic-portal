## Slide 3 — What a Literature Study Is — and What It Is Not

**Slide Content**
- **Working definition:** the *methodical identification, selection, evaluation and synthesis* of existing scholarly works directly relevant to a research question, conducted to establish the state of knowledge and to justify the need for new research.
- **Three load-bearing adjectives:** **methodical** — without method it degenerates into a casual bibliography; **critical** — without appraisal it merely paraphrases sources; **synthetic** — without synthesis it fails to advance understanding.
- **It is an organised argument**, not a summary of what other people wrote: it synthesises prior knowledge, exposes conceptual and empirical gaps, and positions the proposed work inside an ongoing scholarly conversation.
- **Terminology:** *literature study*, *literature review* and *scholarly review of literature* are used interchangeably in this course; the colloquial "literature survey" often means something far weaker and should not be conflated with it.
- **Contrast to reject:** casual reading accumulates familiarity; an annotated bibliography lists and describes; a literature study *compares, adjudicates and concludes*.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
This slide is the conceptual gate for the whole unit — if students leave believing a review is a summary, every subsequent requirement will look like arbitrary bureaucracy. Write the three adjectives on the board and return to them repeatedly, because each one names a distinct and diagnosable failure mode in submitted work. Warn that examiners frequently open with the definition and with the distinction from general reading, so precision in wording here earns marks directly.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Three-panel "failure mode" diagram: *not methodical → casual bibliography*, *not critical → paraphrase*, *not synthesised → no advance in understanding*, each panel with the corrective requirement beneath it. Place the working definition in a bordered key-highlight box across the top.

## Slide 4 — The Evidence Landscape in Computer Science

**Slide Content**
- **Peer-reviewed journals** — *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering*, *ACM Computing Surveys*, *Journal of Machine Learning Research*.
- **Conference proceedings** — NeurIPS, ICSE, SIGCOMM, CVPR; in computer science these carry archival weight equal to or greater than journals, unlike most other disciplines.
- **Books, monographs and dissertations** — doctoral and master's theses are undervalued by students yet contain the most exhaustive literature reviews available on a narrow topic.
- **Industrial and laboratory technical reports** — Microsoft Research, Google DeepMind and similar labs publish results of real consequence outside conventional venues.
- **Patents, standards and grey literature** — ISO, IEEE, NIST, W3C specifications; preprints on arXiv, white papers and working papers that precede peer review but often define the state of the art first.
- **Consequence:** a CS literature study confined to journal articles alone will be systematically incomplete.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Emphasise the disciplinary peculiarity that top-tier conferences are the primary archival venue in computing, because students trained to privilege journals frequently omit the field's most important papers. Encourage them to mine the related-work sections of recent dissertations as an efficient bootstrap for their own reviews. Flag that grey literature is a genuine methodological tension — indispensable for currency, but unrefereed — and that Lecture 2 will treat how to include it defensibly.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Radial diagram with *Research Question* at the centre and six labelled spokes for the source categories, each carrying one concrete CS example. Add a key-highlight chip: "In CS, conferences are archival — not preliminary."

## Slide 5 — Objectives I: Establishing the State of the Art and Identifying Research Gaps

**Slide Content**
- **Objective 1 — Establish the state of the art:** map existing theories, algorithms, architectures, datasets, benchmarks and empirical findings. Without this map, no researcher can *legitimately* claim to address an open problem.
- **Objective 2 — Identify research gaps:** the literature study is the principal mechanism by which gaps are surfaced, and **the articulation of a gap is itself a contribution to scholarship**.
- **Five types of gap:** **knowledge gap** (unanswered empirical or conceptual question) · **methodological gap** (no adequate method, tool or metric) · **theoretical gap** (no framework explaining a class of observations) · **population/context gap** (an under-studied dataset, demographic, application domain or region) · **replication gap** (prior findings never independently validated).
- **Diagnostic discipline:** a claimed gap must be evidenced by what the literature *does not* contain, not asserted because the student has not read enough.
- **CS illustration:** "no benchmark exists for X" is a methodological gap; "this architecture has never been evaluated on non-English corpora" is a context gap.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Press the point that "nobody has done this" is a claim about the literature and therefore requires evidence, which is exactly what a documented search provides. Use the gap taxonomy as a generative tool rather than a memorisation list — ask students to name a candidate gap in their own project area and classify it, which usually exposes whether they have actually read the recent work. Note that replication gaps are acutely common in machine learning, where reported results are often never independently reproduced.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Five-card row for the gap taxonomy, each card with a definition and a one-line CS example, arranged beneath a "known / unknown" territory map showing the state of the art as mapped ground and gaps as unmapped regions.

## Slide 6 — Objectives II: Efficiency, Refinement and Methodological Selection

**Slide Content**
- **Objective 3 — Avoid duplication of effort:** a disciplined review prevents the costly repetition of already-published studies — acute in computing, where architectures and benchmarks proliferate faster than any individual can informally track.
- **Objective 4 — Refine the research problem and hypotheses:** the initial formulation of a problem is rarely the final one; exposure to existing work narrows, broadens, re-frames or *operationalises it in measurable terms*, and hypotheses are similarly sharpened against available evidence.
- **Objective 5 — Select appropriate methods:** the literature is a repository of validated experimental designs, evaluation metrics, statistical tests, simulation frameworks and tooling.
- **Borrow deliberately:** examining how predecessors investigated similar problems lets a researcher adopt fit-for-purpose methods and pre-empt known methodological pitfalls.
- **Practical implication:** reading changes the research question — a student whose question is identical before and after the review has almost certainly not read critically.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Dwell on Objective 4, because students treat the research question as fixed once approved and then resist revising it; frame question refinement as evidence of intellectual progress rather than of initial error. On Objective 5, point out that adopting the community's standard datasets, metrics and baselines is what makes a result *comparable*, and that idiosyncratic evaluation is a common reason student work cannot be published. Offer a concrete example: discovering that the field reports macro-F1 rather than accuracy should change their evaluation plan immediately.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Before-and-after comparison panel showing a vague initial question ("Can AI detect disease?") transformed into an operationalised one ("Does a 3D U-Net trained on MIMIC-CXR improve segmentation Dice over 2D baselines?"), with a "methods borrowed from the literature" sidebar listing design, metric, baseline and tooling.

## Slide 7 — Objectives III: Frameworks, Credibility and Ethical Practice

**Slide Content**
- **Objective 6 — Build theoretical and conceptual frameworks:** concepts, taxonomies, models and theoretical lenses drawn from the literature supply the scaffolding that makes new observations interpretable rather than anecdotal.
- **Objective 7 — Establish credibility and scholarly lineage:** engaging with foundational works demonstrates scholarly citizenship; reviewers and examiners *expect* this lineage as a signal that the work was not conducted in intellectual isolation.
- **Objective 8 — Support ethical practice:** wide reading familiarises the researcher with the norms of attribution, citation and acknowledgement, and a researcher who has read widely is materially less likely to commit plagiarism, fabrication or inappropriate self-citation.
- **Consolidated summary (Table 1):**

  | # | Objective | Core function |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | Establish the state of the art | Map existing knowledge |
  | 2 | Identify research gaps | Justify novelty |
  | 3 | Avoid duplication | Ensure efficiency and originality |
  | 4 | Refine problem and hypotheses | Improve precision |
  | 5 | Select methods | Borrow and adapt validated tools |
  | 6 | Build conceptual frameworks | Provide theoretical grounding |
  | 7 | Establish scholarly credibility | Demonstrate engagement |
  | 8 | Support ethical practice | Reinforce norms of attribution |

- **Examination note:** be prepared to state all eight and to develop *any one* at length with a computer science example.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Treat this table as the highest-yield artefact of the lecture and say so explicitly, since objective enumeration is the most predictable examination item in this unit. On Objective 8, make the causal mechanism concrete: plagiarism is frequently a symptom of thin reading — a student who cannot paraphrase a concept in their own words has not understood it, and copies instead. Close the objectives block by asking which objective students think their own draft review currently fails.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**

## Slide 8 — Scope I: Source and Temporal Boundaries

**Slide Content**
- **Scope defined:** the deliberately declared boundaries within which literature is gathered, evaluated and synthesised — set to prevent *both* superficial coverage and unmanageable breadth.
- **Source scope — four tiers plus standards:** **primary** (original research articles, full conference papers, patents) · **secondary** (surveys, reviews, meta-analyses, textbooks) · **tertiary** (ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web of Science, DBLP, Google Scholar) · **grey** (arXiv, SSRN, technical reports, dissertations) · **standards** (ISO, IEEE, ACM, NIST, W3C).
- **Common error:** relying exclusively on secondary sources — textbooks and surveys are a legitimate *entry point* but must be supplemented with primary sources for direct engagement with original evidence.
- **Temporal scope — three horizons:** **foundational** works that introduced enduring concepts (Turing 1950; Dijkstra 1959) · **contemporary** work from the last 3–5 years carrying current debates, datasets and benchmarks · **emerging** preprints signalling future direction.
- **Working heuristic:** roughly **60–70% of citations from the last five years**, while deliberately retaining the classical references that anchor the work in its intellectual lineage.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Insist that scope is a *declared* decision recorded in the methods section, not an accident of which papers happened to be found — undeclared scope is the root of most unfair criticism a review receives. Illustrate the secondary-source trap: citing a survey's characterisation of a paper rather than the paper itself propagates any error the survey made, and examiners can detect it. Present the 60–70% figure as a planning heuristic rather than a rule, and note that a fast-moving subfield may justify a higher proportion while a theory-heavy one justifies a lower one.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Left half: a five-tier source pyramid (primary at the base, standards as a side column). Right half: a horizontal timeline with three shaded bands — Foundational / Contemporary / Emerging — annotated with the 60–70% heuristic as a proportion bar.

## Slide 9 — Scope II: Disciplinary, Methodological and Geographic Boundaries

**Slide Content**
- **Disciplinary and interdisciplinary scope:** modern computing is irreducibly interdisciplinary. A review of *deep learning for medical image segmentation* must traverse core CS (ML, vision, software engineering), applied mathematics (optimisation, probability, linear algebra), the domain discipline (radiology, biomedical imaging, clinical workflow) and ethics/policy (data privacy, regulation).
- **Explicit inclusion and exclusion:** because interdisciplinary literatures are effectively unbounded, the researcher must state which neighbouring fields are in scope and which are deliberately excluded, with reasons.
- **Methodological scope:** the review should represent the range of methods used in the area — theoretical analysis, simulation, controlled experiment, case study, design science — so that the chosen methodology of the proposed work is justified *by contrast or extension*, not by default.
- **Geographical and linguistic scope:** guard against **geographical bias** (over-reliance on Anglo-American literature) and **linguistic bias** (excluding significant non-English work); at minimum, reflect explicitly on whether such bias is present.
- **Equity dimension:** these biases are not merely technical omissions — they shape whose problems are treated as important and whose datasets are treated as representative.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Make the interdisciplinary demand concrete by asking what a purely algorithmic review of clinical segmentation would miss — typically the annotation protocol, the clinical decision context and the regulatory constraint, each of which can invalidate an otherwise excellent model. On methodological scope, explain that representing the field's methodological diversity is what allows a student to argue their own design choice rather than merely assert it. Treat the bias discussion as an ethics touchpoint that anticipates Slide 11, since selective coverage has consequences beyond completeness.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Concentric-rings diagram for disciplinary scope (core CS → mathematics → domain discipline → ethics and policy) with a dashed inclusion boundary the instructor can point to. Beside it, a compact two-item warning box for geographical and linguistic bias.

## Slide 10 — Significance at Four Levels: Project, Researcher, Discipline, Society

**Slide Content**
- **Project level:** the foundation on which every later stage rests — without it the design is poorly motivated, the contribution is unclear and the evaluation criteria are unjustified; it is also the source of the references appearing throughout the eventual thesis or paper.
- **Researcher level:** develops transferable competencies — critical and analytical reasoning, synthesis of disparate findings, discipline-specific vocabulary and conceptual literacy, and the capacity to position one's own work in an ongoing conversation.
- **Disciplinary level:** aggregated across the community, reviews advance collective knowledge — systematic reviews and mapping studies identify trends, controversies and frontiers, and give newcomers a defensible entry point.
- **Societal level:** as computing increasingly governs algorithmic decision-making, surveillance, healthcare, transport and communication, the literature study is a principal means by which researchers become aware of the social, legal and ethical consequences of their work.
- **Examinable distinction:** **scope** = coverage, *what is included*; **significance** = importance, *why it matters*. These are routinely tested against each other and must not be conflated.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Put the scope-versus-significance distinction on the board and test it live with a one-sentence answer from two volunteers, because this is among the most reliably confused pairs in the unit. Develop the societal level with a concrete instance — a review of facial recognition that never engages the literature on demographic error-rate disparity has met a technical standard while failing a societal one. Stress to final-year students that the researcher-level benefits are the ones that persist after the project is submitted.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Four-tier ascending pyramid or staircase (Project → Researcher → Discipline → Society), each tier carrying one line of payoff. Dock a bordered key-highlight box in the corner reading "Scope = coverage · Significance = importance".

## Slide 11 — The Literature Study as the Ethical Foundation of Research

**Slide Content**
- **Unit thesis:** ethical research practice **begins long before data collection** — the unit is titled *Ethical Conduct and Literature Studies* because the two are causally linked, not merely adjacent.
- **Four ethical capabilities conferred by thorough reading:** correct attribution of ideas and avoidance of plagiarism · recognition of prior contributions and respect for intellectual property · awareness of ethical controversies already debated in the literature · avoidance of the redundant — and ethically questionable — repetition of completed studies.
- **Mechanism, not exhortation:** plagiarism is frequently a *symptom* of insufficient reading, since a researcher who cannot restate a concept in their own words has not yet understood it.
- **Professional codes** — the **ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018)** and the **IEEE Code of Ethics** both place obligations of honest attribution and credit on computing professionals.
- **Forward link:** *addressing plagiarism* and *ethical considerations in research*, developed in later lectures of this unit, are anchored directly in the literature-study practices introduced today.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
This is the slide that justifies the unit's structure, so deliver it as argument rather than as a list of warnings. Make the mechanism vivid: paraphrase-plagiarism typically occurs at 2 a.m. when a student must write about a paper they skimmed, which means the remedy is earlier and deeper reading rather than better disguising. Reference the ACM and IEEE codes by name, since students are entering a profession that has codified these obligations and examiners expect the connection to be made explicitly.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Case-box styled slide: central claim as a pull-quote ("Ethical research begins in the library"), four ethical capabilities as a 2×2 grid beneath it, and the ACM/IEEE code references as a footer band. Reuse the visual treatment of the title slide to close the rhetorical loop.

## Slide 12 — Types of Literature Review and Choosing Among Them

**Slide Content**
- **Six principal types (Table 2):**

  | Type | Purpose | Typical use |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Narrative** | Broad overview of a topic | Thesis background chapter, introductory sections |
  | **Systematic** | Exhaustive, protocol-driven search and synthesis | Evidence-based studies, mapping a whole evidence base |
  | **Meta-analysis** | Statistical synthesis of quantitative results | Combining effect sizes across empirical studies |
  | **Scoping** | Maps the breadth and nature of research activity | Emerging or heterogeneous fields |
  | **Integrative** | Combines empirical and theoretical literature | Mature fields needing conceptual synthesis |
  | **Umbrella** | Synthesises multiple systematic reviews | High-level policy or curriculum decisions |

- **Four selection determinants:** the research question, the maturity of the field, the resources available, and the depth of analysis required.
- **Realistic expectation for this cohort:** a B.Tech project or PG dissertation typically embeds a **narrative or integrative** review in a background chapter — but elements of systematic method (predefined search strings, explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria) are **increasingly expected** and should be adopted.
- **Forward link:** Lecture 2 treats systematic, narrative, scoping and meta-analytical methods in technical depth, including PRISMA reporting and the statistics of pooling.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Position this slide as a preview that gives students the map before Lecture 2 supplies the terrain, and tell them the comparison table will be extended to eight discriminating dimensions next session. Be explicit and practical about expectations: nobody is asking for a twelve-month systematic review in a single semester, but a documented search string and stated inclusion criteria are now the minimum standard of transparency. Ask students to classify the review type their own project will use and to justify it against the four determinants.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Full-width six-row comparison table with the two types most relevant to student projects (narrative, integrative) row-highlighted. Add a "Lecture 2 preview" arrow strip along the bottom naming the four methods to be developed next.

## Slide 13 — The Process Model: Seven Iterative Stages and Their Pitfalls

**Slide Content**
- **Stage 1–2 — Define the scope, then search:** formulate review questions and eligibility criteria; identify databases, search terms and search strings. *Pitfalls:* **bibliographic shopping** (collecting sources with no integrating argument) and **closed-system searching** (relying on Google Scholar alone while ignoring specialised indices).
- **Stage 3–4 — Screen and assess quality:** apply inclusion/exclusion criteria, then evaluate methodological rigour and relevance. *Pitfall:* **lack of critical appraisal** — treating every source as equally authoritative.
- **Stage 5–6 — Extract and synthesise:** record bibliographic and conceptual information systematically; integrate findings thematically, methodologically or chronologically. *Pitfalls:* **chronological listing** in place of thematic argument, and **unmanaged references** (no Zotero, Mendeley or EndNote).
- **Stage 7 — Report:** present the synthesis in a structured, transparent manner. *Pitfalls:* **recency bias**, **disciplinary parochialism**, and **plagiarism through paraphrase** — paraphrasing without attribution.
- **The process is iterative, not linear:** screening commonly forces search refinement, and synthesis regularly reveals the need to return to the search stage.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Present the pitfalls attached to the stage where they originate rather than as a detached list, since students recognise their own habits far more readily when the failure is located in a step they are about to perform. Make the reference-manager point operational — ask them to install Zotero this week, because retrofitting citations at submission time is where most accidental attribution failures occur. Underline the iteration message: students often believe returning to the search stage signals earlier failure, when it is in fact evidence of a maturing review.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Circular seven-stage process wheel with feedback arrows from Screen → Search and Synthesise → Search to make iteration visible. Run the eight pitfalls as a numbered red-flag sidebar down the right margin, each aligned to its stage.

## Slide 14 — Case Study: Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Healthcare Analytics

**Slide Content**
- **Scenario:** a B.Tech project proposing *"Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Healthcare Analytics."*
- **Scope defined:** foundational federated learning (McMahan et al., 2017) · differential privacy and secure aggregation · healthcare applications and datasets (MIMIC, UK Biobank) · regulatory frameworks (HIPAA, GDPR) · benchmarks and evaluation metrics.
- **Gap identified:** critical reading of recent surveys reveals that most systems optimise accuracy under privacy constraints while **neglecting communication efficiency, energy consumption and fairness across hospital sites with heterogeneous data distributions**.
- **Contribution articulated:** a communication-efficient aggregation algorithm with formal differential-privacy guarantees, evaluated empirically across heterogeneous clinical sites — a claim now *warranted* by the review rather than asserted.
- **Ethical dimension:** the review must also surface literature on patient consent, secondary use of health data and bias in clinical algorithms, binding the project to the ethical theme of Unit III.
- **Your turn (think-and-apply):** for *"Blockchain-based Trust Management in IoT Networks"*, specify (a) three source categories, (b) two foundational works, (c) one likely research gap, (d) one ethical concern the review should surface.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Run this slide as a worked demonstration of the whole lecture: scope becomes state of the art, state of the art becomes a gap, the gap licenses a contribution, and the ethical literature grounds the project. Note how the gap statement is *specific and falsifiable* — communication efficiency, energy, fairness — which is what distinguishes a genuine gap from the vague "little work exists" that examiners penalise. Set the blockchain/IoT exercise for pair work in the final minutes if time permits, or as take-home preparation for Lecture 2.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Case box occupying the upper two-thirds, laid out as a four-step left-to-right chain: Scope → State of the Art → Gap → Contribution, with Ethics shown as a band underlying all four. Place the think-and-apply exercise in a distinct bordered "Your Turn" panel at the foot.

## Slide 15 — Summary, Discussion Questions & Exam Preparation

**Slide Content**
- **Seven takeaways:** a literature study is **methodical, critical and synthetic**, distinct from casual reading and from an annotated bibliography · its **eight objectives** run from state of the art to ethical practice · its **scope** spans sources, time horizons, disciplines, methodologies and geography, and must be deliberately bounded · it is **significant** at project, researcher, disciplinary and societal levels · **review types** serve different purposes · the **process is iterative** · above all it is the **ethical foundation** of research.
- **Discussion questions:** (i) How does a literature study differ from a general reading assignment? (ii) Why is temporal balance necessary, and what does the 60–70% heuristic protect against? (iii) Which single objective do you think your own draft review currently fails, and why? (iv) Defend or refute: *"The literature study is the ethical foundation of research."*
- **Exam focus — definitions first:** write a precise definition of *literature study* and distinguish it from a colloquial "literature survey"; **objectives as enumeration** — know all eight and develop any one at length with a CS example.
- **Exam focus — the tested distinctions:** *scope* (coverage) versus *significance* (importance); primary versus secondary versus tertiary sources with a CS example of each; the five gap types; at least three review types compared with a scenario-based recommendation.
- **Exam focus — application over recall:** practise on a concrete sub-domain (cybersecurity, NLP, IoT) rather than in the abstract, and always be ready to articulate the ethics link — it is the through-line of the unit.
- **Suggested readings:** Creswell & Creswell (2018) · Fink (2019) · Webster & Watson (2002) · vom Brocke et al. (2009) · ACM Code of Ethics (2018) · IEEE Code of Ethics.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Consolidate by returning to the guiding claim from Slide 1 and asking the class to justify it in one sentence using the ethical capabilities from Slide 11. Recommend Webster & Watson (2002) as the single most useful short reading, since it argues specifically for concept-centric rather than author-centric synthesis — precisely the habit that eliminates chronological listing. Close by previewing Lecture 2 on systematic, narrative, scoping and meta-analytical methods, and ask students to bring a draft scope statement for their own project topic.
