What plagiarism is

Presenting someone else's words, ideas, figures, code or results as your own, without proper credit.

Intent doesn't erase it

Accidental plagiarism is still plagiarism.

Ideas count, not just words

Rewording an idea still needs a citation.

The fix is simple

Credit every source, every time.

A · Types of plagiarism

Four types, as they happen in your review

Direct

Word-for-word copying without quotation marks and citation.

Pasting nnU-Net's abstract into your background section.

Mosaic (patchwriting)

Borrowed phrases stitched together, or synonyms swapped into the original structure.

Changing “precise” to “accurate” in a U-Net sentence and calling it yours.

Self-plagiarism

Reusing your own published text, figures or results without disclosure.

Sending your conference paper's results to a journal as if new.

Accidental

Unintended, usually from careless notes or a lost citation.

A copied sentence in your notes that you later believe you wrote.

A · A closer look

“But it was my own paper!” Self-plagiarism

Duplicate publication

The same experiments published twice as if new. Readers and meta-analyses count them twice.

Salami slicing

One study cut into several thin papers, e.g., whole tumour in one, tumour core in another.

Recycled text

Copying large parts of your own earlier paper without citing it.

The legitimate route: conference → journal extension

Cite the conference version, add substantial new work (more data, new experiments, deeper analysis), tell the editor, and check the journal's policy on how much must be new.

B · Prevention

Stop accidental plagiarism at the note-taking stage

Tag every note the moment you write it, with its source:

[Q] Exact quote, in quotation marks
[P] My paraphrase of someone's idea
[M] My own idea or conclusion

Notes on nnU-Net

[P] Isensee21: pipeline adapts pre-processing, network and training to each new dataset.

[P] Isensee21: evaluated on many public segmentation challenges.

[M] Assumes every training case has the same input sequences. My gap?

Keep sources in a reference manager (Zotero or Mendeley) from day one, so no citation is ever lost.

C · Paraphrasing

Five steps to a genuine paraphrase

1

Understand

Read until you could explain it

2

Close it

Hide the source. This is the key step.

3

Explain

Write it as if telling a friend

4

Compare

Different structure and wording?

5

Cite

A paraphrase still needs a citation

Quote only when exact wording matters: a definition, a claim you dispute. Rare in CS papers.

Paraphrase one idea in detail. Summarise a whole paper in a sentence or two.

C · Paraphrasing in practice

Synonym swapping is not paraphrasing

Source (sample sentence written for this lesson)

“Skip connections pass high-resolution feature maps from the encoder directly to the decoder, allowing the network to recover fine spatial detail lost during downsampling.”

✗ Mosaic

“Skip links send high-resolution feature maps from the encoder straight to the decoder, letting the network regain fine spatial detail lost during downsampling.”

Same skeleton, five words swapped, no citation.

✓ Paraphrase

“Because downsampling blurs boundaries, U-Net copies sharper early features across to the decoder, which helps it trace tumour edges accurately [1].”

New structure, applied to her problem, cited.

D · Similarity checks

Reading a similarity report sensibly

What it is

Tools such as Turnitin and iThenticate show text that matches existing sources, as a percentage.

High ≠ guilty

Reference lists, properly quoted text and standard phrases like “Dice similarity coefficient” all match.

Low ≠ innocent

Copied ideas, figures, code and results may not appear in a text match at all.

E · Citation formats

IEEE style: numbered in order of use

In the text

“Encoder–decoder networks with skip connections [1] were later made self-configuring [2].”

In the reference list

[1] O. Ronneberger, P. Fischer, and T. Brox, “U-Net: Convolutional networks for biomedical image segmentation,” in Medical Image Computing and Computer-Assisted Intervention – MICCAI 2015, Cham, Switzerland: Springer, 2015, pp. 234–241.
[2] F. Isensee, P. F. Jaeger, S. A. A. Kohl, J. Petersen, and K. H. Maier-Hein, “nnU-Net: A self-configuring method for deep learning-based biomedical image segmentation,” Nature Methods, vol. 18, no. 2, pp. 203–211, 2021.
Initials, then surname · title in quotes · venue in italics · pages and year · numbers never re-used

The same paper in ACM style

ACM reference format

Olaf Ronneberger, Philipp Fischer, and Thomas Brox. 2015. U-Net: Convolutional Networks for Biomedical Image Segmentation. In Medical Image Computing and Computer-Assisted Intervention – MICCAI 2015. Springer, Cham, 234–241.
FeatureIEEEACM
Author namesInitials: O. RonnebergerFull names: Olaf Ronneberger
YearNear the endRight after the authors
TitleIn quotation marksTitle case, no quotation marks
In-text marker[1][1] or (Ronneberger et al. 2015), by template
Never format by hand. Store BibTeX in Zotero or Mendeley and let the IEEE or ACM LaTeX template do it.

F · Citation ethics

Citing honestly is more than citing at all

Cite what you read

Read U-Net only via a survey? Cite the survey, or go and read U-Net.

Cite data and code

BraTS, nnU-Net's repository, pretrained weights: all get credit.

Cite the final version

Prefer the peer-reviewed version over the arXiv preprint.

No padding

Don't add irrelevant citations to look well-read.

No coercion

Don't demand, or give in to demands for, citations to unrelated work.

No misrepresenting

Don't cite a paper for a claim it does not make.

Key takeaways

Four habits of honest writing

1

Tag every note as quote, paraphrase or your own idea, with its source.

2

Paraphrase with the source closed, then cite it anyway.

3

Credit figures, code, results and datasets, not just sentences.

4

Let a reference manager and the IEEE or ACM template format references.
