  2. Describe the composition, mandate and review procedure of Institutional Ethics Committees.
  3. Apply the UGC and UPES plagiarism regulations to concrete academic situations.

2. The Four-Tier Regulatory Architecture

In computer science, where algorithms increasingly mediate decisions affecting human welfare, finance, healthcare and civil liberties, the gap between ethical aspiration and regulatory enforcement is the practical problem. Four tiers close it, and they are best distinguished by how each is enforced.

TierInstrumentsEnforcement mechanism
1 — International codesNuremberg (1947), Declaration of Helsinki (1964, rev. 2013), Belmont Report (1979)Moral authority; no direct enforcement
2 — Professional society codesACM Code of Ethics (2018), IEEE Code of EthicsEnforceable through membership and publication venues
3 — National law and guidanceICMR (2017), UGC (2018), DPDP Act (2023), IT Act (2000, am. 2008), CDSCO, MeitYLegally binding; penalties attach
4 — Institutional bodiesIECs / IRBs / RECs, Research Integrity Offices, Departmental Research CommitteesOperational gatekeepers — approve, refuse, monitor

The tiers interlock. A UPES-IEC decision applies Belmont principles through ICMR procedure, so an institutional refusal ultimately rests on a 1979 document. Tier 4 is the only layer you will personally interact with as a researcher.

ConceptBrief definition
Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC)A multidisciplinary body constituted to review, approve and monitor research involving human participants or sensitive data
Institutional Review Board (IRB)The US equivalent of an IEC; used interchangeably in international literature
Similarity indexA quantitative measure produced by plagiarism-detection software, expressed as a percentage of matched text
Research misconductFabrication, falsification or plagiarism in proposing, performing or reviewing research, or in reporting results (ORI definition)

3. Tier 1: International Frameworks

3.1 The Nuremberg Code (1947)

Originating from the trials of Nazi physicians, the Nuremberg Code established ten principles for ethical human-subjects research, the foremost being voluntary informed consent. Although written for biomedical research, the consent principle undergirds modern data-protection regulation, including its application to user studies in computer science.

3.2 The Declaration of Helsinki (1964, rev. 2013)

Issued by the World Medical Association, this declaration extends the Nuremberg principles and distinguishes therapeutic from non-therapeutic research. It is the most widely adopted international standard for research involving human participants and is referenced by Indian institutional policies.

3.3 The Belmont Report (1979)

The US National Commission articulated three principles that now inform most IEC review criteria: respect for persons, beneficence and justice.

Why Belmont is the examinable one

IEC review forms are effectively operationalised Belmont: consent procedures test respect for persons, risk–benefit analysis tests beneficence, and recruitment criteria test justice. The three principles are a near-certain multiple-choice item.

All three documents were written for medical research, yet each applies directly to a CS usability study, a crowdsourced annotation task or an adversarial-testing protocol involving people. The object of protection is the participant, not the discipline.

4. Tier 2: Professional Society Codes

The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018) outlines general ethical responsibilities for computing professionals:

  • Contribute to society and human well-being.
  • Avoid harm.
  • Be honest and trustworthy.
  • Be fair and take action not to discriminate.
  • Respect privacy.
  • Honour confidentiality.

The IEEE Code of Ethics commits members to treat all persons fairly and with respect, to refrain from harassment and discrimination, to avoid injuring others, and explicitly requires responsible disclosure of factors that might endanger the public.

Positive duties, not just prohibitions

Both codes impose positive duties — contribute, act against discrimination, disclose danger — and not merely prohibitions. Students read all ethics as a list of prohibitions and consequently believe that doing nothing is always safe. The ACM code says otherwise.

On enforceability: these are professional codes, sanctioned through membership status, publication-venue policy and reputational consequence rather than by law. A code violation rarely reaches a court but can end a publication record. The ACM's “avoid harm” and anti-discrimination duties are the instruments under which algorithmic bias and unsafe deployment are argued as ethics violations rather than merely engineering defects.

5. Tier 3 and 4: Indian Frameworks and Ethics Committees

5.1 The Indian Regulatory Landscape

  • ICMR — National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017). India's most comprehensive ethical standard and the source of IEC composition and procedure rules. ICMR has additionally issued dedicated ethical guidance on artificial intelligence in biomedical research and healthcare, addressing algorithmic transparency, dataset representativeness and bias mitigation — the provisions most directly relevant to computing.
  • UGC. The (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism) Regulations, 2018 define plagiarism levels and penalties for higher-education institutions; subsequent draft revisions address similarity thresholds and generative-AI content.
  • MeitY. Issues policy on emerging technology, including the National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (2018) and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — the operative Indian data-protection statute, which applies to your research data handling, not only to companies.
  • CDSCO. Oversees clinical trials and software as a medical device (SaMD) — increasingly relevant where CS departments build AI/ML diagnostic tools. Students building a diagnostic classifier rarely realise they may be building a regulated medical device.
  • Information Technology Act, 2000 (and 2008 amendments). The legal foundation for prosecuting unauthorised data access, digital fraud and cybercrime — the statute under which a research data-handling failure becomes a criminal rather than an academic matter.
Name the right instrument

Naming the correct Indian instrument in an answer is a marking criterion. Confusing UGC (academic integrity) with DPDP (data protection) is a common and costly error.

5.2 Purpose and Composition of an IEC

Definition — Institutional Ethics Committee

An IEC — also called an Institutional Review Board or Research Ethics Board — is a formally constituted body that reviews research proposals to ensure conformity with ethical standards before the research begins.

Four purposes: protection of the rights and welfare of participants; assurance that risks are minimised and reasonable in relation to anticipated outcomes; verification that informed-consent procedures are adequate; and oversight of ongoing research through continuing review.

Per ICMR 2017 guidelines, an IEC must be multidisciplinary and gender-balanced, with a minimum of five and a maximum of twelve members:

  • Chairperson — external to the institution.
  • One or two basic medical scientists.
  • One or two clinicians.
  • One legal expert or retired judge.
  • One social scientist or philosopher.
  • One layperson representing community interests.
  • Member Secretary, who conducts day-to-day administration.

For computer science research, an IEC should additionally include a data protection officer or information-security expert when proposals involve large-scale personal-data processing — without whom the committee cannot competently assess re-identification risk.

Why multidisciplinary composition matters

This is a stated examination item, and a memorised membership list cannot answer it. The external chairperson guards against institutional self-interest; the legal member reads consent documents as enforceable instruments; the layperson tests whether disclosure is actually comprehensible to a participant.

5.3 Levels of Review

Review levelTriggerOutcome
Exempt reviewMinimal risk; secondary use of de-identified data; survey of public officialsNotification of exemption
Expedited reviewMinimal risk but not exempt; minor modifications to approved protocolsApproval by Member Secretary and one member
Full board reviewGreater than minimal risk; vulnerable populations; novel AI/ML systems involving personal dataDecision at a convened quorum meeting
Exemption is a determination, not an assumption

Exemption is a determination the committee makes, not one the investigator may assume. Self-exempting a protocol is itself a procedural violation. Note also that novel AI/ML systems touching personal data land in full-board review — the trigger students most need to recognise in their own project plans.

5.4 The Review Process

  1. Submission. The principal investigator submits a protocol including the research proposal, consent forms, data management plan, recruitment materials and risk–benefit analysis.
  2. Administrative screening. The Member Secretary checks for completeness.
  3. Primary reviewer assignment. Two or more members conduct detailed reviews.
  4. Board discussion. At a convened quorum meeting, the investigator may be invited for questions.
  5. Decision. Approval, modifications required, deferral or disapproval. “Modifications required” is the most common outcome and is not a rejection.
  6. Notification. A formal letter communicates the decision, typically within 4–6 weeks.
  7. Continuing review. Annual progress reports; any amendment requires resubmission. A protocol amended mid-study without resubmission is operating without approval.

Four to six weeks plus a possible modification cycle means ethics review must be initiated at proposal stage, not when data collection is about to begin. The most common practical failure is a student discovering in week ten that a study needed approval they never sought.

5.5 The UPES-IEC

UPES maintains an Institutional Ethics Committee that reviews research proposals involving:

  • Human participants — surveys, interviews, behavioural experiments, usability studies.
  • Personally identifiable information collected from data subjects.
  • Animal subjects — less common in CS but required for some HCI work.
  • Environmental samples of biological origin.
  • Dual-use research of concern (DURC).

Submissions are routed through the online Research Management System (RMS) portal, and the committee meets quarterly with provision for emergency review of time-sensitive studies. Plan around the quarterly cycle.

Does your CS project need review?

A usability study, a survey of developers, a crowdsourced annotation task, or an adversarial-testing protocol with human participants all require review. A purely computational study on a public, properly licensed, de-identified dataset generally does not.

DURC is listed deliberately: research with legitimate civilian use that is also weaponisable — vulnerability-discovery tooling, disinformation-capable generative models, surveillance automation — is within the committee's remit even with no human participants at all.

6. Plagiarism Policy

6.1 The UGC Definition

UGC Regulations (2018)

“The practice of taking someone else's work or ideas and passing them off as one's own.”

The definition encompasses verbatim copying without attribution; close paraphrasing without acknowledgement; uncited use of methodologies, datasets or algorithms; self-plagiarism; and collusion (coordinated submission of identical work).

Note the third clause: it places CS artefacts squarely inside the regulation. A borrowed pipeline or dataset is regulated content, not just borrowed prose.

6.2 Similarity Index Thresholds

Similarity indexCategoryPenalty (minimum)
Up to 10% (excluding quotes, bibliography, small matches)MinorWarning; no penalty if properly cited
10–40%ModerateDenial of degree for students; withdrawal of paper for faculty
Above 40%MajorExpulsion; blacklisting of authors

Two structural points to retain: the exclusions mean that the effective index is what the regulation addresses; and thresholds are floors for penalty, so an institution may impose stricter limits than the UGC minimum but not laxer ones.

6.3 Proposed Revisions

  • Exclusion of generative-AI output from similarity detection.
  • Mandatory declaration of AI-assisted content generation.
  • Stricter thresholds for doctoral theses.
  • Specific treatment of code plagiarism using tools such as MOSS or JPlag.

6.4 The UPES Policy in Operation

  1. Software in use. UPES licenses Turnitin for textual submissions and Ouriginal for cross-checking. Code submissions may be checked using MOSS at the discretion of the supervising faculty.
  2. Acceptance thresholds (excluding bibliography and quoted material): M.Tech and PhD dissertations, similarity index at or below 10%; B.Tech project reports, at or below 20%; conference and journal publications from UPES, at or below 15% excluding self-citation.
  3. Submission workflow. Submit via the LMS integrated with Turnitin → receive the similarity report within 24 hours → supervisor reviews marked regions → revise and resubmit if the threshold is exceeded → final submission to the Departmental Research Committee.
  4. Consequences. First offence: written warning and mandatory resubmission. Second offence: project grade of “F” and six-month debarment from re-registration. Third offence, or major plagiarism above 40%: expulsion, and revocation of degree if discovered post-graduation.
  5. Generative AI. Use for grammar and code-suggestion purposes is permitted but requires explicit declaration in the Acknowledgement section. Submitting AI-generated text as one's own original writing constitutes academic misconduct.
Worked case — applying the policy

Aditi submits her final-year project report for Turnitin screening. The report yields a 22% similarity index, with 14% attributable to a methodology section that closely paraphrases a published survey without quotation marks.

  1. Review. Her supervisor notes the flagged regions.
  2. Threshold check. For B.Tech reports the threshold is 20%; 22% exceeds it.
  3. Remedial action. Aditi rewrites the methodology section, citing the original survey and using quotation marks for technical definitions.
  4. Resubmission. The new report shows 9%.
  5. Clearance. The Departmental Research Committee accepts the revised report.

Had she not revised, the project would have received an “F” grade, and her supervisor would have been required to report the incident to the Departmental Disciplinary Committee. The instructive detail: the 14% was fixable by citation and quotation, so the offence was one of attribution rather than of writing ability.

7. Ethical Dilemmas in Computer Science

  • Informed consent in online studies. Participants may not read consent forms; deception is sometimes methodologically necessary (studying susceptibility to phishing); and withdrawal of data after collection is technically difficult once results are anonymised and aggregated.
  • Data privacy and anonymisation. Datasets from social media, mobile phones and IoT devices often remain re-identifiable. Simple anonymisation is rarely sufficient; differential privacy, k-anonymity and federated learning have emerged as responses.
  • Algorithmic bias and fairness. Algorithms trained on biased data perpetuate and amplify social inequities — hiring algorithms that discriminate against women (the Amazon recruiting tool case, 2018), facial recognition with higher error rates for women and people of colour, predictive policing that disproportionately targets minority neighbourhoods. ACM: non-discrimination.
  • Dual-use research of concern. Research with legitimate civilian applications that is also weaponisable: large language models that generate disinformation, vulnerability-discovery tools that facilitate cyberattacks, AI systems that automate surveillance. IEEE: public-endangerment disclosure.
  • Authorship and credit disputes. Omitted contributors, honorary authorship, and competing claims of priority for the same idea.
  • Whistleblowing and disclosure. Loyalty to peers against responsibility to the public. Many institutions, including UPES, have whistleblower protection policies aligned with the Public Interest Disclosure and Protection of Informers Act, 2010.
  • Reproducibility and data fabrication. Publication pressure drives selective reporting, p-hacking and, at the extreme, outright fabrication.
Ethics is not simply prohibition

Some valid research questions cannot be studied with full prior disclosure — which is precisely why a committee exists: to weigh justification, debriefing and harm rather than to apply a blanket rule.

8. Case Studies

8.1 The Facebook Emotional Contagion Study (2014)

Researchers at Cornell University, in collaboration with Facebook, manipulated the news feeds of approximately 689,000 users to study emotional contagion. Users were not asked for consent, on the basis that the study was conducted under the platform's terms of service.

Ethical issues: lack of informed consent; potential psychological harm; insufficient IRB oversight. Outcome: public outcry and regulatory scrutiny; the US Department of Health and Human Services clarified that certain social-media research does qualify as human-subjects research requiring IRB review; the lead researcher issued a public apology.

Lesson: even when research uses existing data, manipulation of the user environment triggers ethical obligations. CS researchers must engage an ethics committee for any study that alters user experience.

8.2 The Cambridge Analytica Scandal (2018)

Personal data of approximately 87 million Facebook users was harvested through a personality-prediction app and used for political micro-targeting without consent.

Ethical issues: violation of informed consent; misuse of data beyond stated purposes; mass-scale privacy violation. Outcome: investigations in the US, UK and EU; congressional hearings; a $5 billion FTC penalty; and impetus for GDPR enforcement and for India's DPDP Act, 2023.

Lesson: researchers must verify the chain of consent for any secondary data, particularly when data crosses jurisdictional lines.

8.3 Theranos (2003–2018)

Theranos claimed to have developed blood-testing technology requiring only a finger-prick sample. Internal whistleblowers alleged that the technology was non-functional and that results were being run on conventional machines or fabricated. Outcome: criminal prosecution of the founders, SEC and FDA investigations, dissolution of the company.

Lesson: data fabrication in computer science research — for instance, reporting model accuracy not supported by experiments — is research misconduct of the same category, with severe consequences.

8.4 Henrietta Lacks and the HeLa Cells

In 1951, Henrietta Lacks's cancer cells were taken without her knowledge or consent and became the basis for decades of biomedical breakthroughs. The HeLa genome was published in 2013 without consulting the Lacks family. The NIH subsequently established a HeLa Genome Data Access Working Group including family members to govern future access.

Lesson: data derived from identifiable sources retain ethical obligations even decades later. The case inspired data-access frameworks in computational biology.

8.5 Microsoft “Tay” (2016)

Microsoft released a Twitter chatbot that learned from interactions with users. Within roughly sixteen hours, coordinated adversarial input caused it to produce racist, misogynistic and otherwise offensive output, and it was shut down.

Ethical issues: failure to anticipate adversarial manipulation; inadequate pre-deployment testing; lack of safety guardrails. Lesson: researchers deploying interactive AI must implement safeguards, conduct red-teaming, and engage ethics review where human participants are involved in adversarial testing. ACM's “avoid harm” applies at deployment, not only at design.

8.6 A Domestic Plagiarism Case (2016)

A doctoral scholar at an Indian IIT was found to have plagiarised approximately 60 per cent of a thesis. The plagiarism was detected through Turnitin and confirmed by manual review. Under the UGC Regulations, the scholar's registration was cancelled, and the supervising committee faced scrutiny for inadequate oversight.

Lesson: supervisors bear responsibility for ensuring the originality of student work, and institutions must conduct periodic checks throughout the research lifecycle, not only at submission.

The connecting thread

In five of these six cases the harm was foreseeable, and the missing element was an independent review that nobody sought.

9. Summary

  • Research ethics is enforced through a multi-tiered regulatory architecture spanning international codes (Nuremberg, Helsinki, Belmont), professional society codes (ACM, IEEE), national frameworks (ICMR, UGC, DPDP Act) and institutional bodies (IECs, IRBs).
  • Institutional Ethics Committees are the operational pivot; their composition, procedures and authority are governed by ICMR and equivalent standards, and UPES operates the UPES-IEC with specific provisions for CS research.
  • Plagiarism policies at UPES and under UGC use similarity thresholds and tiered consequences, with generative AI the live area of revision.
  • Ethical dilemmas in computer science include online consent, algorithmic bias, dual-use concerns, authorship disputes and reproducibility.
  • Case studies illustrate the real-world consequences of violations and underscore the necessity of institutional review.

Across eight lectures this unit moved from why literature matters, through how to analyse it, to the obligations that make the resulting knowledge trustworthy. This lecture supplies the machinery by which those obligations are enforced.

10. Exam Preparation

Seven preparation points
  1. Memorise the specific thresholds. UGC and UPES thresholds are frequently asked; know the exact percentages and corresponding penalty levels.
  2. Differentiate review levels. Understand when full-board, expedited and exempt review apply.
  3. Be familiar with named documents: Nuremberg Code, Declaration of Helsinki, Belmont Report, ICMR 2017, UGC 2018, DPDP Act 2023.
  4. Practise case analysis. Examiners present a hypothetical and ask you to identify the issue and recommend a remedy.
  5. Know the composition of an IEC and be ready to explain why multidisciplinary composition matters.
  6. Link each dilemma to a specific code. The Tay case implicates ACM's “avoid harm”; Cambridge Analytica implicates DPDP/GDPR; the thesis case implicates UGC 2018.
  7. Stay current. Generative AI and DPDP Act enforcement are likely current-affairs items.
Four-move case analysis
  1. Identify the ethical issue.
  2. Name the governing code or regulation.
  3. State what review or disclosure was owed.
  4. Recommend the remedy.

Answers that identify the issue correctly but cite no instrument lose most of the available marks.

  2. Discuss the UGC Plagiarism Regulations (2018) and the proposed revisions. How does UPES operationalise these in its academic workflow? 10 marks

  4. Compare and contrast the ACM Code of Ethics (2018) with the IEEE Code of Ethics. To what extent are they enforceable in practice? 10 marks
  5. Examine the Cambridge Analytica case in the context of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. What liabilities would researchers face if a similar incident occurred today at an Indian institution? 10 marks
