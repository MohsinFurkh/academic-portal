PART 1 · ATTRIBUTION

Why We Cite

Five reasons, and only one of them is about avoiding trouble. A citation is a working part of the argument, not a legal disclaimer attached to it.

1

Credit

To give credit to the originator of an idea. This is an ethical duty, owed to a person who did work you are now benefiting from.

THE CONSEQUENCE STUDENTS MISS

Citing carefully does not dilute your contribution. It is the only thing that makes your contribution visible at all.

In a page with no citations, every sentence is equally unsupported and equally unremarkable. In a page where prior work is cited precisely, the two uncited sentences stating your result stand out — and the reader can see exactly how far past the state of the art you went.

Under-citing does not make you look original. It makes you look unread.

2

Verification

To let the reader check your claims. A scientific duty: an uncheckable claim is not evidence, whatever else it may be.

3

Location

To place your work in the field — which conversation you are joining, and which position in it you are taking.

4

Separation

To separate your contribution from everyone else's. Citation is what makes your novelty visible; without it, a reader cannot tell which sentence is yours.

5

Avoiding plagiarism

The negative reason, and the one students think of first. It follows from the other four rather than standing on its own.

PART 2 · PLAGIARISM

Plagiarism: The Definition, and Three Things That Follow

DEFINITION

The practice of presenting another person's words, ideas, data, code, designs or other creative work as one's own, without adequate acknowledgement of the original source.

CONSEQUENCE 1

It covers ideas, not only words.

Rewriting someone's idea in your own sentences, without citing them, is still plagiarism.

This is the form students find hardest to accept, because the words genuinely are theirs. But the contribution being claimed is not the phrasing — it is the idea, and that came from somewhere.

CONSEQUENCE 2

Intent is not required.

Careless note-taking produces the same offence as deliberate copying.

Universities penalise both. Intent may affect the severity of the penalty, and it never affects whether the offence occurred. “I forgot where it came from” describes how it happened, not whether it did.

CONSEQUENCE 3

It is not copyright infringement.

Copyright is a legal right of an owner; plagiarism is an ethical breach against the academic community.

The two overlap but neither implies the other. Copying a public-domain work without credit breaks no copyright and is still plagiarism. Quoting a paragraph with full attribution is not plagiarism and may still need permission.

PART 2 · PLAGIARISM

Eleven Types — I: The Text-Based Forms

Examiners reward the classification. Name the type, define it in one clause, and give a computing example.

TYPEWHAT IT ISNOTE
1 · Direct / verbatim
Copying text word for word, without quotation marks or citation.
The clearest case, and the one similarity software catches immediately.
2 · Mosaic / patchwork
Weaving phrases from several sources into your own sentences, changing a few words.
The most common student form, and still easily detected — matching works on phrases, not documents.
3 · Paraphrasing plagiarism
Restating a source's content correctly in your own words, with no citation.
The words are yours; the idea is not. Software may miss it entirely; a reader who knows the field will not.
4 · Self-plagiarism
Re-using your own previously submitted or published work as if it were new, without disclosure.
Includes duplicate publication of one paper in two venues. Disclosure and citation make re-use legitimate.
5 · Salami slicing
Splitting one study into several minimally different papers to inflate a publication count.
“Least publishable units”. It fragments the evidence and wastes reviewers' time.
6 · Source-based
Citing a source that does not exist, or one you did not read; or misrepresenting what a source says.
Also covers citing a secondary source as though it were the primary one — Lecture 1's trap.

Types 2 and 3 account for most real cases. Both begin in the same place: a source open on screen while you write, and a note file that did not record which sentences were copied.

PART 2 · PLAGIARISM

Eleven Types — II: Beyond Text

These five matter disproportionately in computer science, where much of what is reused is not prose at all.

TYPEWHAT IT ISNOTE
7 · Data plagiarism
Presenting another group's experimental results, tables or figures as your own.
Shades into falsification, which Lecture 5 treats as misconduct rather than as a citation failure.
8 · Image and figure
Reproducing diagrams, screenshots or architectural figures without permission and attribution.
Redrawing a figure does not remove the obligation. “Adapted from [14]” in the caption does.
9 · Code plagiarism
Copying source code from a repository, a classmate, a textbook or a StackOverflow answer.
Carries a legal obligation as well as an academic one. Treated in detail on the next slide.
10 · Accidental
Genuine citation failure from careless notes, a lost quotation mark, or a mangled paraphrase.
Still an offence. This is why prevention is a note-taking habit and not a moral resolution.
11 · Contract cheating
Submitting work written by another person or an agency; ghostwriting.
The most severe form, because it also involves deception about authorship itself. Increasingly prosecuted.

A useful ordering for an answer: text-based forms (1–6), non-text forms (7–9), then the two that concern the process rather than the product — accidental plagiarism, which is a failure of method, and contract cheating, which is a failure of authorship.

PART 2 · PLAGIARISM

Code Plagiarism: The Computer-Science Case

Adding this to the standard list is the fastest way to turn a generic answer into a computing one.

1

Structural detectors are not fooled by renaming.

MOSS (Measure of Software Similarity), JPlag and Codequiry compare token streams and abstract syntax trees, not characters. Renaming variables, reordering functions, changing comments and reformatting all leave the structure intact — and the structure is what is matched.

WHAT AN ATTRIBUTION LOOKS LIKE

/*
* Ring-buffer implementation
* adapted from libfoo v2.3,
* (c) 2019 A. Author,
* Apache-2.0. Modified to
* support variable-size
* records.
*/

Six lines. They record what was taken, from where, under what licence, and what you changed — which is also exactly the information your future self will need when the library is updated.

Do the same in the report: a footnote or an appendix listing reused components.

2

Reuse is normal; undisclosed reuse is not.

Real software is built from libraries and from other people's solutions. The offence is presenting reused code as your own work. A header comment naming the source and the licence converts the same act into ordinary practice.

3

Licences add a legal obligation on top of the academic one.

GPL, Apache-2.0, MIT and BSD each carry attribution requirements, and some carry conditions on how your own code may then be distributed. Citing the source satisfies the academic duty; it does not by itself satisfy the licence.

4

StackOverflow is a source.

Code from an answer is under CC BY-SA and requires attribution. “Everyone copies from StackOverflow” is true and irrelevant: everyone is also required to say so.

PART 2 · PLAGIARISM

Why It Happens, and What It Costs

The causes are mostly mundane. The consequences are not.

WHY IT HAPPENS

Poor time management and deadline pressure.

Not knowing the rules — especially what counts as an adequate paraphrase.

Weak academic-writing skill, particularly in a second language, which pushes a writer towards the source's phrasing.

“Publish or perish” pressure, and quantity-based evaluation of researchers.

Careless note-taking: text copied into notes, and later forgotten to be copied.

Easy availability of digital text and of generative tools.

A belief that detection is unlikely.

Student

Zero marks, resubmission, suspension, cancellation of registration — or of an already-awarded degree.

Researcher

Rejection, retraction of the published paper, loss of employment, debarment from funding.

Institution

Loss of reputation and of accreditation standing.

Science

A corrupted record: false results continue to be cited for years after retraction.

Note the asymmetry: the causes are small and the consequences are not recoverable.

PART 3 · ADDRESSING PLAGIARISM

Prevention: Ten Habits, from the First Day

Nine of these cost nothing. The first one prevents more cases than the other nine combined.

1

Keep disciplined notes. Mark every copied sentence with quotation marks and the full source at the moment you copy it.

2

Use a reference manager. Zotero, Mendeley or JabRef, from the beginning, so that a citation is never lost.

3

Read, close the source, then write. If the source is on screen while you write, your sentences will echo it. This is mechanical, not moral.

4

Quote sparingly and correctly. Quotation marks or an indented block, plus a citation with the page number.

5

Paraphrase properly. Your own words and your own sentence structure — see the next two slides.

6

Cite while writing, not afterwards. Citations added at the end are the ones that go missing, or attach to the wrong sentence.

7

Attribute figures and code. In the caption or a header comment, and respect the licence as well as the citation duty.

8

Check your own similarity report. Before submission — and read it source by source, rather than looking only at the percentage.

9

Declare re-use of your own work. A sentence in the introduction, and a citation to the earlier paper. That is all self-plagiarism requires.

10

Disclose AI assistance. As your institution or venue requires. Undisclosed use is treated as misrepresentation.

PART 3 · ADDRESSING PLAGIARISM

Quoting, Paraphrasing and Summarising

Three different operations with three different requirements. All three require a citation; only one of them permits the source's words.

TECHNIQUEWHAT IT ISREQUIREMENTS
Quotation
The exact words of the source.
Quotation marks, or an indented block for a long quote; a citation with the page number. Use rarely in engineering writing — reserve it for definitions and for a phrase that would lose something if reworded.
Paraphrase
The source's idea restated in your own words and your own sentence structure, at similar length.
Citation required. Must not preserve the original phrasing or the original structure. Swapping synonyms into the source's sentence is not a paraphrase — it is mosaic plagiarism with extra steps.
Summary
The source's idea compressed to its essentials.
THE TEST THAT SETTLES MOST CASES
Citation required. Substantially shorter than the original, and in your own words throughout. Summarising several sources into one sentence is the basic move of synthesis.

Cover the source. Write the sentence from memory. Then compare.

If more than about three consecutive words coincide by accident, either rewrite it or quote it. The test works because it removes the source from your working memory — which is the actual mechanism by which unintentional copying happens.

Worked Example: A Paraphrase That Fails, and One That Works

ORIGINAL SOURCE

“Cache-oblivious algorithms achieve asymptotically optimal use of the memory hierarchy without any knowledge of cache parameters, which makes them portable across machines with different cache sizes.”

UNACCEPTABLE — mosaic plagiarism

Cache-oblivious algorithms obtain asymptotically optimal usage of the memory hierarchy without any awareness of cache parameters, which renders them portable across machines with differing cache sizes.

Synonyms swapped, structure identical. Even with a citation this is too close to the original — the sentence is still the source's, wearing a different coat.

ACCEPTABLE — paraphrase

Because their design does not depend on any specific cache size or block length, cache-oblivious algorithms run efficiently on very different machines while still meeting the theoretical optimum for memory-hierarchy usage [14].

The information is the same; the sentence is not. It begins in a different place, subordinates a different clause, and carries a citation.

ACCEPTABLE — quotation

Frigo et al. describe such algorithms as achieving “asymptotically optimal use of the memory hierarchy” [14, p. 285] without knowledge of cache parameters.

The distinctive phrase is quoted, attributed and page-referenced; the rest is the writer's own. This is the correct move when the original wording is itself worth keeping.

Note what changed between the first and the second: not the vocabulary, but the sentence's architecture. A paraphrase is a re-thought sentence, not a re-worded one.

AI Paraphrasing Tools — I: The Integrity Concerns

Running a copied passage through a rewriter, a spinner or a general-purpose language model, and submitting the output. This is now asked directly in examinations, so it is worth being precise about why it fails.

1

The source of the ideas is unchanged.

Rewording does not create authorship. If the content, the structure and the argument came from another paper and are not cited, the result is paraphrasing plagiarism — regardless of how the rewording was produced.

2

Evading detection is an aggravating factor, not a defence.

Deliberately rewriting text so that similarity software will not flag it demonstrates intent to conceal. Institutions treat this more severely than the original copying, because it converts carelessness into deception.

3

The submitted text is not the student's work.

Where the tool generated the prose, the submission misrepresents authorship even if every source is correctly cited. That is a separate offence from plagiarism, and it is not cured by citation.

4

Undisclosed use breaches most current policies.

Universities, journals and funding agencies now require disclosure of AI assistance. Most publishers also state explicitly that an AI tool cannot be an author, because it cannot take responsibility for the work.

5

Responsibility is not transferable.

“The tool wrote it” is not a defence. The author is accountable for every sentence submitted, including the ones they did not read closely.

AI Paraphrasing Tools — II: The Methodological Concerns

These matter as much as the ethical ones, and they are what the question “briefly describe the methodological concerns” is asking for.

1

Loss of technical precision. Paraphrasers substitute synonyms without understanding the domain. Significant (statistically) becomes important; complexity becomes difficulty; deterministic becomes predictable. The sentence still reads well and now says something false.

2

Distortion of hedging and scope. Qualifiers carry the scientific meaning. A tool that turns “accuracy improved by 3% on read-heavy workloads” into “accuracy improved substantially” has converted a defensible claim into an unsupported one.

3

Fabricated or mismatched citations. Generated text frequently attaches references that do not exist, or attributes a claim to the wrong source. The citation is correctly formatted, which is precisely what makes it hard to catch.

4

Inherited errors. If the source was wrong, the rewritten version is wrong — and now the error appears in your voice, without the citation that would let a reader trace it back.

5

No verification of the underlying claim. Rewriting is not reading. The author has not checked the method, the data or the validity of what is being asserted, so the review has no critical content at all.

6

Loss of a consistent authorial argument. A literature review must build your argument towards your gap. Spun paragraphs are stitched summaries; they cannot do this, which is why such reviews end without a usable gap statement.

7

Reproducibility and accountability. If a reviewer asks why a particular claim was made, the author must answer from the source. Generated text leaves no such trail.

Legitimate Versus Illegitimate Use

The distinction examiners look for is between using a tool on your own understanding and using it instead of understanding.

GENERALLY ACCEPTABLE, SUBJECT TO DISCLOSURE

Checking grammar and spelling.

Improving the fluency of a sentence that you wrote.

Suggesting search terms and synonyms for your Boolean string.

Explaining a difficult paper to you before you read it yourself.

Drafting boilerplate you would otherwise copy from a template.

NOT ACCEPTABLE

Generating the related-work section.

Rewriting a source's paragraphs so that they pass a similarity check.

Producing text whose content you have not verified.

Presenting generated prose as your own writing.

Citing references the tool supplied, without checking that they exist.

THE SAFE TEST

Could you defend every sentence, and its citation, to an examiner who asked where it came from?

If the answer is no for any sentence, that sentence is not yet yours — whatever produced it.

PART 4 · DETECTION AND REGULATION

Detection and the Similarity Index

Turnitin, iThenticate, Ouriginal, DrillBit and PlagScan compare a submission against web pages, published articles and previously submitted student work, and report a similarity index — the percentage of text matching other sources.

A HIGH PERCENTAGE MAY BE INNOCENT

Correctly quoted and attributed material.

The reference list and the table of contents.

Standard technical phrases, equation and algorithm names.

A properly cited methodology description.

A LOW PERCENTAGE MAY HIDE SERIOUS PLAGIARISM

An uncited paraphrase — the words genuinely differ.

A stolen idea, method or experimental design.

Translated text from another language.

Copied source code, which text matching does not see at all.

THE CONCLUSION

The similarity index measures textual overlap, not plagiarism. The report must be read source by source by a human.

Two further points worth knowing. First, these tools also index student submissions, so copying from a senior's thesis is among the easiest cases to detect, not the hardest. Second, text matching sees no source code: MOSS and JPlag exist precisely because a similarity index of 2% is entirely consistent with a wholly copied program.

PART 4 · DETECTION AND REGULATION

Worked Example: Is 18% Similarity Acceptable?

The expected answer is “it depends, and here is what it depends on”. Never a bare yes or no. Structure it in three parts.

1

Under the UGC Regulations, 2018, 18% falls in Level 1 — above 10% and up to 40% — which attracts revision and resubmission. It is above the Level 0 free band, so it cannot be dismissed as automatically acceptable.

The regulatory position.

2

When 18% is acceptable

Properly quoted and attributed material, the reference list and the table of contents — all formally excluded from the check.

Standard technical terms, equation and algorithm names, and common methodological phrases.

The author's own earlier published work, disclosed and cited.

Scattered short matches of a few words each, spread thinly across many sources.

Re-run the check with the exclusions applied. The figure typically falls well below 10%.

3

When 18% is unacceptable

A single large block matching one source — a whole paragraph, or a full page of related work.

Uncited matches, however short.

Matched text in sections that must be original: the abstract, the contribution statement, the results, the discussion.

Matches to a classmate's submission, or to an unpublished thesis.

Unacceptable, even though the percentage is modest. The distribution is what condemns it.

The technical justification to close with: the similarity index measures textual overlap, not plagiarism. Its distribution matters more than its magnitude — 18% spread across forty sources as standard phrasing is innocent; 18% concentrated in one uncited source is plagiarism.

The UGC Regulations, 2018 — The Four Levels

The UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity and Prevention of Plagiarism in Higher Educational Institutions) Regulations, 2018. Writing the title in full immediately signals a prepared answer.

LEVELSIMILARITYACTION FOR A STUDENT
Level 0
Up to 10%
No penalty.
Level 1
Above 10% and up to 40%
Revise and resubmit the script within a stipulated time, up to six months. No marks or credit are awarded for the plagiarised script.
Level 2
Above 40% and up to 60%
Debarred from resubmission for one year.

PART 4 · DETECTION AND REGULATION

The UGC Regulations, 2018 — Exclusions, Scope and Machinery

The four levels are the examinable table. These six provisions are what the regulation actually requires an institution to do.

Exclusions from the check

All quoted work with proper attribution; the bibliography and references; tables of contents; and standard or common phrases and generic terms.

Applicability

Theses, dissertations, and any submitted or published academic document. The regulations cover students, faculty, researchers and staff alike.

Penalties for faculty and staff

Escalating: withdrawal of the manuscript, denial of annual increments, denial of the right to supervise research scholars, and in the most serious cases suspension or dismissal.

Institutional machinery

Every institution must constitute a Departmental Academic Integrity Panel (DAIP) and an Institutional Academic Integrity Panel (IAIP) to investigate allegations.

Instruction and access

Institutions must provide instruction on academic integrity and access to detection software for students and staff.

Undertaking and supervision

Students submit a signed declaration that the work is original and has been checked. The supervisor certifies originality and may be held responsible for negligence.

Two names are worth memorising exactly: the Departmental Academic Integrity Panel and the Institutional Academic Integrity Panel. Together with the four bands and the four exclusions, they are the whole of what a question on the regulation expects.

PART 5 · PRACTICE

A Checklist Before You Submit Anything

✓

Every borrowed idea carries a citation. Not only quoted words. If a sentence would not exist without a particular paper, that paper is cited in it.

✓

Every quotation has marks, a citation and a page number. And there are very few of them, because this is engineering writing.

✓

Every paraphrase passes the cover-and-rewrite test. Close the source, write from memory, compare. More than three consecutive coinciding words means rewrite or quote.

✓

Every reused figure, dataset and code file is attributed. In the caption, in a header comment, or in an appendix — with the licence named where one applies.

✓

Your own earlier work is disclosed and cited. One sentence in the introduction. This is all that separates legitimate re-use from self-plagiarism.

✓

AI assistance is disclosed, and every generated sentence verified. Including every reference the tool supplied: check each one against the actual publisher record before it enters your bibliography.

✓

You have run the similarity check and read the report. Source by source, not as a headline percentage — and you can explain every match above a few words.

PART 5 · PRACTICE

Discussion

In pairs, ten minutes. Bring one answer back for class critique.

  1. 1
    Take a paragraph from a paper in your area. Paraphrase it properly, then paraphrase it badly on purpose. Exchange with your partner and see whether they can tell which is which, and say why.
  2. 2
    A thesis reports 8% similarity, all of it a single uncited paragraph copied from one source. Which UGC level applies, and is the level the right answer to the question “was this plagiarism?”
  3. 3
    You reuse 200 lines of Apache-2.0 code in your project. List everything you must do — academically and legally — and say which of those a citation alone does not cover.
  4. 4
    Your group used a language model to polish the abstract. Draft the disclosure sentence you would put in the report, and decide where in the report it belongs.

RECAP · UNIT III · LECTURE 4

Key Takeaways

  1. 1
    Citation gives credit, enables verification, locates your work, separates your contribution from everyone else's, and avoids plagiarism. Under-citing does not make you look original; it makes you look unread.
  2. 2
    Plagiarism is presenting another's words, ideas, data or code as one's own. It covers ideas as well as words, intent is not required, and it is distinct from copyright infringement.
  3. 3
    Eleven types: verbatim, mosaic, paraphrasing, self-plagiarism, salami slicing, source-based, data, image, code, accidental and contract cheating. In computing, add code plagiarism and name MOSS and JPlag.
  4. 4
    Prevention is a note-taking habit, not a moral resolution. Mark copied text at the moment you copy it, use a reference manager, and read-close-then-write.
  5. 5
    AI paraphrasers fail on both grounds: the ideas are still someone else's and the prose is not yours, while synonym substitution destroys technical precision, distorts hedging, and fabricates citations.
  6. 6
    The similarity index measures textual overlap, not plagiarism — its distribution matters more than its magnitude. The UGC Regulations, 2018 set four bands (0–10, 10–40, 40–60, above 60), exclude quoted material and references, and require DAIP and IAIP panels in every institution.
