1 Fixing grammar and awkward phrasing in a paragraph you wrote.

5 Asking it to suggest related work, then finding and reading those papers yourself.

6 Having it generate synthetic data to fill gaps in your real dataset.

Two of these are prohibited outright by essentially every publisher. One is fine but for a reason that surprises people. The interesting work is at the boundary.

Reveal · one principle, not eight rules

Accountability cannot be delegated

Fine — no disclosure needed

  • 1 · Grammar and phrasing on your own text. Same status as a spell-checker or a proofreader.
  • 2 · Explaining a paper to you. A tutor, not a source. You still read the paper.
  • 5 · Suggesting related work you then find and read. The verification is what makes it fine.

Common thread: the tool assists your understanding or polish. You could defend every sentence without it.

Declare it

  • 4 · Generated code that you tested and understand. Disclose in methods; you remain responsible for every line.
  • 6 · Synthetic data. Legitimate technique, but it must be declared prominently — a reader must never mistake generated data for collected data.
  • 3 · Drafting assistance beyond phrasing, if you then verified and rewrote it. As stated in the exercise, it is not.

Common thread: the tool contributed content. Content requires provenance.

Never

  • 3 · Generating a literature review from titles. You have not read the papers. The model will fabricate what they say, and you will have asserted it under your name.
  • 7 · Pasting confidential material. A paper under review is confidential; uploading it breaches that duty regardless of the tool's data policy.
  • 8 · AI as co-author. Universally prohibited — see slide 5.

One principle generates all three columns: you remain fully accountable for every claim, citation, number and line of code in your work — so any use that puts something in your paper you cannot personally verify is prohibited, and any use that contributes content must be disclosed.

Part A · why the rules are shaped this way

Four things these tools do that break research

Fabricated citations

Plausible authors, plausible title, plausible venue, plausible year — and the paper does not exist. Or it exists and says something else.

Why it is fatal: a fabricated reference in your thesis is indistinguishable, to an examiner, from a deliberately invented one. Check every citation against the actual paper.

Confident wrongness

Fluent, well-structured, authoritative — and incorrect. There is no uncertainty signal in the prose to warn you.

Worst where you are weakest: you cannot catch errors in material you do not already understand, which is exactly where you were most tempted to use it.

Homogenised thinking

These models produce the consensus framing. Research contributes by departing from consensus.

The subtle cost: ask it for your research gap and you get the crowded, obvious one — the trap from Lecture I.3. Novelty is precisely what a model trained on existing work cannot supply.

Confidentiality leakage

Unpublished work, participant data, a paper under review, proprietary code from an internship.

Note: consent obtained for your study does not cover sending participants' data to a third-party service. That is a new processor and a new disclosure.

Notice that three of the four are invisible in the output. That is what makes verification a duty rather than a courtesy.

Part B · the rules you will be held to

The policy landscape — remarkably consistent

Body AI as author? Disclosure required? Notable specifics
ACM No Yes — in acknowledgements or methods Generated text must be disclosed; authors remain responsible for the entire content
IEEE No Yes — declare in the acknowledgements AI-generated text must be identified; the human authors are accountable
Elsevier No Yes — a dedicated statement before the references Permitted only to improve readability and language, not to replace author tasks such as interpreting results or drawing conclusions
Springer Nature No Yes — in the methods or acknowledgements Also restricts AI-generated images in most cases
Major CS conferences No Generally yes Several explicitly prohibit uploading papers under review to AI services — a reviewer confidentiality rule

The universal reason AI cannot be an author. Authorship requires accountability — the ability to approve the final version, to answer for its integrity and to respond to challenges. A tool can do none of these, so it cannot hold the role. This is exactly the criterion from III.3, applied consistently.

These policies change. Check the venue's current instructions before every submission — including for your UPES coursework, where the applicable policy is the one in your assignment brief. When in doubt, disclose. Nobody has ever been penalised for declaring more than required.

Write the disclosure statement for your own project

1 · 4 min — write yours 2 · 3 min — swap; neighbour asks the two questions 7:00

The four elements

  1. Which tool, and which version, if you know it.
  2. For what — be specific: “language editing of §4”, not “assistance”.
  3. What you did to verify the output.
  4. What you did not use it for — this is the sentence that earns trust.

A worked example:
“ChatGPT (GPT-4) was used for language editing of Sections 3 and 5, and to draft an initial version of the data-cleaning script in Appendix B. All generated code was read, tested against manually verified cases, and modified by the authors. No AI tool was used to generate results, to interpret findings, to select or summarise cited works, or to produce any text in Sections 4 and 6. All citations were verified against the original publications. The authors take full responsibility for the content.”

Note how specific the negatives are. A vague disclosure (“AI was used to assist with writing”) tells a reader nothing and reassures nobody.

Step 2 · The two questions

Your neighbour asks exactly two things about your statement:

  1. “Point to something in your project that came from the tool. Now defend it to me as if I doubt it.” If you cannot, that thing should not be in your project.
  2. “Is there anything you used it for that your statement does not mention?” The honest answer is often yes. Add it.

The verification duty, stated plainly. If a generated citation is fabricated, you fabricated it. If generated code has a bug that corrupts your results, your results are wrong. If a generated summary misrepresents a paper, you misrepresented it. The tool is never the author, so it is never the one at fault.

Keep this statement. PBL 1, released at V.1 in October, requires a mandatory AI-use disclosure log — you are starting it now.

Unit III in four sessions — and the thread through them

III.1 · Search

Narrative vs SLR vs mapping study. Databases and what each is bad at. Query building: OR within concepts, AND between. Inclusion/exclusion criteria fixed first. Snowballing. PRISMA counts.

Key idea: the difference between narrative and systematic is reproducibility.

III.2 · Read & analyse

Three-pass method. Critical appraisal using Unit II. Two sentences per paper. Taxonomy → comparison matrix → gap table. Reference managers.

Key idea: “what it does not establish”, accumulated over thirty papers, is your gap.

III.3 · Ethics

Seven forms of plagiarism. Similarity ≠ plagiarism score. Real paraphrasing. FFP and the grey zone. Authorship, COI, consent, data privacy.

Key idea: would a reader change their view if they knew? Then disclose.

III.4 · Generative AI

Fine / declare / never. Four failure modes. Publisher policies. AI is never an author. The disclosure statement.

Key idea: accountability cannot be delegated.

The single thread through all four: research is a public claim, so every step behind it must be one you can show someone — the search string, the screening decision, the source of a sentence, the origin of a number.

Where it goes. Unit II gave you evidence you produced; Unit III gave you evidence others produced and the rules for handling both honestly. Unit IV turns all of it into a document someone will read: technical writing, IMRaD, and the research proposal — Assignment 2.
