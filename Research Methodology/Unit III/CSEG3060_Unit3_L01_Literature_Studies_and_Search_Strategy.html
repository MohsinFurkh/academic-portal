Do first · 4 minutes · be honest, nobody is marking this

How did you actually find the papers in your Assignment 1?

1 · Alone, 90 s — write the literal steps you took 2 · Pairs, 90 s — compare; could either of you repeat the other's? 3 · Class, 60 s 4:00

Write down, in order, exactly what you did. Then answer three questions about it:

  1. Could you write down the steps precisely enough that I could run them and get the same set of papers?
  2. How many papers did you look at and reject? Can you say why you rejected them?
  3. What did your method systematically miss? Not "nothing" — every method misses something. Name it.

The honest answer for most people is: typed some words into Google Scholar, opened the first page, took whatever looked relevant and was free to download, and stopped when there were enough. If that is your answer, write it down. It is the starting point for everybody.

Reveal · why it matters that you cannot write it down

An unwritten search method has three biases built in

Ranking bias

You took what the engine ranked first.

Google Scholar ranks heavily by citation count, which correlates with age. Your "state of the art" is systematically several years old, and the recent work that contradicts it is on page 4.

Access bias

You took what you could download.

Free-to-read is not a property of quality. You have silently excluded a slice of the literature and the exclusion is invisible in your write-up — nobody, including you, knows what is missing.

Stopping bias

You stopped when you had "enough".

Enough for what? With no stated criterion, you stop when the papers start agreeing with you — which is confirmation bias with a search engine attached.

The fix is not "search harder". It is write the procedure down before you run it — then the biases become stated limitations instead of hidden ones.

That is the entire difference between a narrative review and a systematic one. Not effort, not length, not how many papers. Reproducibility. The same idea you applied to experiments in II.4, applied to reading.

Part A · what kind of review are you writing?

Narrative · Systematic Literature Review · Systematic Mapping Study

Narrative review Systematic literature review Systematic mapping study
Question “What is known about X?” “What does the evidence say about a specific question?” “What research exists on X, and where are the gaps?”
Search Expert judgement; not documented Documented protocol, fixed in advance Documented protocol, broader query
Depth per paper Variable Deep — results extracted and quality assessed Shallow — usually title, abstract, keywords only
Typical scale 20–60 papers 20–80 after screening hundreds 100–1,000+ classified
Output A readable synthesis and argument Aggregated evidence, and a verdict A classification map — bubble plots of topic × method × year
Reproducible? No Yes Yes
Best for you when Writing your report's Chapter 2 background The gap is narrow and you want a defensible answer You are still choosing a topic and need the landscape

The honest recommendation for a one-semester project: a mapping study to choose and justify the gap, then a narrative review written with systematic habits — a documented search string and stated criteria — for the report itself. A full SLR is typically a six-month job.

Do not call it systematic unless it is. The word is a technical claim about method, not a compliment about thoroughness. An examiner who sees “systematic review” will ask for your protocol, your inclusion criteria and your PRISMA counts.

Part B · where to search

Six sources. None of them is sufficient alone.

Scopus

Good: broadest curated coverage, excellent Boolean and field search, citation counts, exportable BibTeX/CSV. The default starting point for an SLR.

Bad: subscription required; some CS conferences indexed late or not at all.

Web of Science

Good: highly curated, strong citation analysis, good for journal work.

Bad: weaker CS conference coverage — which is a serious problem, because in computing the best work is often at conferences.

IEEE Xplore

Good: full text of IEEE conferences and journals; essential for systems, networks, hardware, signal processing.

Bad: publisher-specific — it only knows about IEEE. Never a complete search on its own.

ACM Digital Library

Good: the core venues for software engineering, HCI, PL, databases and theory.

Bad: also publisher-specific. Use it with IEEE, not instead of.

DBLP

Good: free, comprehensive CS bibliography, superb for tracing an author's or a venue's complete output. Clean BibTeX.

Bad: metadata only — no abstracts, so you cannot search by topic properly.

Google Scholar

Good: free, enormous reach, full-text indexed, finds preprints and theses, forward citation search.

Bad: not reproducible — results are personalised and change over time; no reliable Boolean; no export of a full result set; indexes non-peer-reviewed material indiscriminately.

The working rule: Scopus or Web of Science for the systematic sweep · IEEE and ACM for CS full text · DBLP to complete an author or venue · Google Scholar for forward citations and to catch what the others missed — never as your only source, and never as your documented one.

Also worth knowing: arXiv for preprints (fast, not peer-reviewed — say so), Semantic Scholar and Connected Papers for citation-graph exploration, and Papers with Code for ML with released artefacts.

Part C · how to search

A search string is built, not typed

Five steps

  1. Break the question into concepts. Usually three: population, intervention, outcome. Ignore the rest.
  2. List synonyms for each concept — including the ones you would never use. “code review” / “peer review” / “inspection” / “walkthrough” are the same concept across four decades.
  3. OR within a concept, AND between concepts. This is the whole grammar.
  4. Add truncation and phrases: test* catches test, testing, tester; "code review" forces the phrase.
  5. Restrict the field — title, abstract, keywords. Full-text search returns papers that mention your term once in the related work.

Worked example. Question: does automated code review reduce defects?

TITLE-ABS-KEY(
  ("code review" OR "peer review" OR inspection OR walkthrough)
  AND (automat* OR "static analysis" OR bot OR tool)
  AND (defect* OR bug* OR fault* OR quality)
)

Calibrating: the result count tells you what to do

> 2,000 hits — too broad. Add a concept, restrict the field, or narrow the population. You are searching a topic, not a question.

50–500 hits — workable. Screenable by title and abstract in a few hours.

< 10 hits — almost always your synonyms, not a virgin field. Check what terms the papers you do have actually use in their keywords.

0 hits — a spelling or syntax error, nine times out of ten.

The calibration test: pick three papers you already know are relevant. Does your query return all three?

If it misses one, read that paper's keywords and add what you find. Repeat until all three are caught. Only then run the real search — and record the string, the database and the date.

Part C · from 400 hits to 30 papers

Screening: decide the rules before you see the papers

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Write them before screening. Each must be decidable from the title and abstract alone, and by someone who is not you.

Include if…
peer-reviewed · 2015 or later · reports empirical evaluation · concerns the population of interest · written in English

Exclude if…
position paper or editorial · no evaluation · duplicate/extended version · under 4 pages · not accessible in full text

The reliability check: have a teammate independently screen 10–20% of the same abstracts and compare. Disagreement above roughly 10% means your criteria are ambiguous, not that your teammate is careless. Report the agreement.

Two stages: title+abstract first (fast, ~1 min each), then full text on the survivors. Record a reason for every full-text exclusion — you will need them for the PRISMA diagram.

Snowballing — what the database search missed

Database search alone typically misses 10–30% of relevant work, because terminology drifts and indexing is imperfect.

  • Backward snowballing — go through the reference lists of your included papers. Finds the foundational work everyone cites.
  • Forward snowballing — find papers that cite your included papers, via Scopus or Google Scholar. Finds the newer work, including the paper that refutes your key reference.

Iterate until saturation — until a round adds nothing new — and report how many rounds that took.

Record the PRISMA counts as you go: identified → duplicates removed → screened → excluded at title/abstract → full text assessed → excluded with reasons → included. Four numbers and a reason list. Reconstructing them afterwards is miserable; capturing them live costs nothing.

Build your string, run it, screen the first ten

1 · 4 min — build the string 2 · 3 min — run and calibrate 3 · 3 min — screen 10 titles 4 · 2 min — swap and check 12:00

Do this, in order

  1. Three concepts from your own research question. Write them as three columns.
  2. Three or more synonyms per concept. Include an old-fashioned one and an over-formal one.
  3. Assemble: OR within columns, AND between them, truncation where the word has endings, quotes on phrases.
  4. Run it on Scopus if you have access, otherwise IEEE Xplore or ACM DL. Restrict to title/abstract/keywords.
  5. Calibrate against the count table on slide 6, and against three papers you already know. Adjust and re-run.
  6. Write two inclusion and two exclusion criteria.
  7. Screen the first ten titles — in or out, and the reason.

No laptop? Pair with someone who has one. Doing this on paper and running it tonight also works — but the calibration loop is the point, and it needs a live result count.

Record these five things in your notebook

1 · The exact string, copy-pasted, not paraphrased

2 · Database and the date you ran it

3 · Number of hits

4 · Your inclusion and exclusion criteria

5 · The 10 screening decisions with reasons

Those five things are the methodology paragraph of your literature chapter. You are not preparing to write it — you are writing it.

Step 4, the swap: hand your string to a neighbour. They run it exactly. If they get a different count, something is undocumented — and that is precisely the failure this whole lecture is about.

Consolidation

Eight things that must survive this hour

  1. The difference between narrative and systematic is reproducibility, not effort.
  2. An undocumented search carries ranking, access and stopping bias — invisibly.
  3. Mapping study to find the gap · SLR to answer a narrow question · narrative for your background chapter.
  4. No database is sufficient alone. Scopus/WoS for the sweep, IEEE+ACM for CS full text, DBLP for authors, Scholar for forward citations.
  1. A query is built: concepts → synonyms → OR within, AND between → truncate → restrict the field.
  2. Calibrate against three papers you already know. If the query misses them, it is broken.
  3. Write inclusion and exclusion criteria before screening, and have someone else screen 10–20%.
  4. Snowball both ways until saturation, and record the PRISMA counts as you go.

If you remember one sentence: write down the string, the database and the date — a search you cannot hand to someone else is an opinion about the literature, not a survey of it.
