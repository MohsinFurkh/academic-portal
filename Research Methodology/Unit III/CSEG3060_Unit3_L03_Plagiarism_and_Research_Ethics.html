Reveal · the answers, and they surprise people

Only one of those was clearly fine

2 · Paraphrase + cite — FINE. This is exactly what scholarship is. Provided the paraphrase is genuinely yours and not the original with synonyms swapped in — see slide 6.

1 · Reusing your own text — SELF-PLAGIARISM. Surprising to most students: you cannot plagiarise yourself, surely? You can. The reader is being led to believe this is new work. Repair: cite your earlier report like any other source.

3 · Stack Overflow code — DEPENDS ON THE LICENCE AND THE ATTRIBUTION. Stack Overflow contributions are CC BY-SA licensed, which requires attribution and has share-alike implications. Uncredited, it is plagiarism; credited, it is normal practice. Comment the source URL and date. Same for any copied snippet.

4 · Dropping the outlier — FALSIFICATION, unless declared. This is the most dangerous item on the list because it feels like tidying. Dropping data that disagrees with you, without a pre-stated rule and without reporting it, is manipulation of results. Repair: state your exclusion rule before collecting, apply it blindly, and report every exclusion and its reason.

5 · Gift authorship — MISCONDUCT. Authorship is a claim of intellectual contribution and carries accountability for the work. Kindness is not a qualifying criterion. It also dilutes the credit of everyone who did work.

6 · Citing unread work — QUESTIONABLE, and it propagates errors. You are vouching for a claim you have not checked; if the survey mis-summarised it, you have now repeated the error under your own name. Read it, or cite the survey as your source: “as reported in [survey]”.

Note the pattern: five of the six are things a decent person does under deadline pressure without ever deciding to cheat.

Part A · plagiarism

Seven forms, and you already know only the first

  • 1 · Verbatim copying — text lifted without quotation marks or citation. The one everybody knows.
  • 2 · Mosaic / patchwriting — stitching phrases from several sources into a paragraph, changing a few words. The commonest form in student work, and usually unintentional.
  • 3 · Paraphrase without citation — the idea is someone else's even when every word is yours. Citation is owed to ideas, not just to words.
  • 4 · Self-plagiarism — reusing your own previously submitted text or results without disclosure.
  • 5 · Source-code plagiarism — copied code, uncredited. Detectable by MOSS and JPlag regardless of renamed variables.
  • 6 · Figure, table and data plagiarism — reproducing a figure without permission and attribution. Students forget figures entirely.
  • 7 · Ghost / contract writing — submitting work written by someone else, paid or otherwise. The most serious.

Why this happens to honest people

  • Note-taking without source tracking. You paste a passage into a notes file in week 2, find it in week 11, and no longer remember it was not yours. This single mechanism accounts for most accidental plagiarism.
  • Writing with the source open beside you — your sentences drift toward theirs.
  • Deadline compression. Everything on this list gets more likely at 2 a.m.
  • Believing that "common knowledge" covers more than it does.

Three habits that prevent nearly all of it: (1) in your notes, put quotation marks and a citation key on anything copied, always, even in private notes. (2) Close the source before writing your version. (3) Write from your notes, not from the paper.

Common knowledge test: would a reader in your field accept it without a source? “TCP guarantees ordered delivery” — yes. “TCP accounts for 85% of traffic on this network” — no, cite it.

Part A · Turnitin, iThenticate, MOSS, JPlag

A similarity score is not a plagiarism score

Four misunderstandings

  • “Under 10% is safe.” False in both directions. A 4% match can be a stolen central paragraph; a 30% match can be entirely references, standard terminology and correctly quoted material.
  • “The tool decides.” It does not. It produces a report; a human judges whether each match is legitimate.
  • “If I paraphrase enough, I am fine.” The tool misses it; a supervisor who knows the literature does not. And paraphrase-without-citation is still plagiarism.
  • “It checks everything.” Coverage is finite — some sources, some languages and much code are not indexed.

What to actually do

  • Run it early, on a draft — not the night before submission, when nothing can be fixed.
  • Read the report match by match. For each: is it a correctly quoted and cited passage, a standard phrase, a reference-list entry, or a real problem?
  • Exclude the bibliography and quotes before interpreting the number.
  • Look for the single biggest continuous match, not the total. One 300-word block matters far more than fifty 6-word matches.
  • For code: MOSS and JPlag compare structure, not text — renaming variables and reordering functions does not defeat them, and attempting it is itself evidence of intent.

The tool is a smoke detector, not a judge.

Part B · doing it correctly

Three versions of the same source. Only one is acceptable.

SOURCE — Zhang et al. (2021):

“Convolutional models trained on curated agricultural imagery exhibit substantial performance degradation when deployed on photographs captured under uncontrolled field conditions, primarily due to variation in illumination and background clutter.”

A · Word-swapping — still plagiarism. “Convolutional networks trained on curated farming images show considerable performance decline when used on pictures taken in uncontrolled field settings, mainly because of variation in lighting and background clutter [12].”

Citation present, and it is still plagiarism. The sentence structure, clause order and argument are entirely Zhang's; only the vocabulary moved. This is patchwriting, and it is what a thesaurus-based “paraphrasing tool” produces.

B · Quotation — correct but overused. As Zhang et al. observe, such models “exhibit substantial performance degradation when deployed … under uncontrolled field conditions” [12].

Perfectly legitimate — but in computing, direct quotation is reserved for definitions, or for wording you intend to dispute. A literature review built from quotations reads as though you did not understand the material well enough to restate it.

C · Genuine paraphrase — correct. “The gap between benchmark and field performance is well documented: Zhang et al. [12] attribute it chiefly to lighting variation and cluttered backgrounds, neither of which appear in curated datasets. This matters for the present work because our target images are captured by farmers rather than researchers.”

Restructured, compressed, and — crucially — integrated into your own argument. It says why the source matters here. That last clause is something no paraphrasing tool can produce, because it depends on your project.

The reliable method: close the source, write what it means from your notes, then reopen it to check you were fair.

Part C · research integrity

FFP — and the much larger grey zone underneath it

The three that end careers

  • Fabrication — inventing data or results that were never collected.
  • Falsification — manipulating data, equipment or processes so results are not accurately represented. Includes selectively dropping runs, altering images, and adjusting an axis to exaggerate a gap.
  • Plagiarism — as above.

These are the internationally recognised definitions of research misconduct. In India the UGC (Promotion of Academic Integrity) Regulations 2018 additionally define graded penalties for plagiarism in academic work.

Questionable research practices — far commoner

  • HARKing — presenting a post-hoc hypothesis as if predicted. (II.2)
  • p-hacking / metric-shopping — trying analyses or metrics until one looks good, and reporting only that.
  • Selective reporting — omitting the benchmarks you lost, the seeds that disagreed, the ablation that failed.
  • Cherry-picked baselines — comparing against what you can beat. (II.4)
  • Salami slicing — splitting one study into several papers to inflate a count.
  • Citation manipulation — padding with self-citations or irrelevant work.

Notice: nearly every item is a Unit II methodology error that has crossed into an integrity problem because it was hidden.

The distinguishing question is not “did I do something unusual?” but “would a reader change their view of my result if they knew I had done this?” If yes, disclose it. Disclosure converts almost every grey-zone practice into a legitimate one.

Part C · the rest of the ethics you owe

Four obligations that apply to your project this semester

Authorship

Requires substantial contribution to conception, design, execution or interpretation, plus approval of the final version, plus accountability for it. All three.

Wrong: gift authorship (a friend who needs it), ghost authorship (someone who wrote it is omitted), and adding a supervisor who never saw the paper.

Do now: agree author order and roles in writing, at the start of the group project — not at submission.

Conflicts of interest

Anything that could reasonably be seen to bias your judgement: funding, employment, equity, a company relationship, personal ties to those you review.

The rule is disclosure, not avoidance. A conflict disclosed is managed; a conflict discovered later invalidates the work.

Applies to you if: you evaluate a tool built by your own lab, or your internship company supplied the data.

Human subjects

Informed consent: participants know the purpose, what is collected, how it is stored, and that they may withdraw at any time without penalty.

Institutional ethics approval where required. Extra care with vulnerable groups, and with your own students or juniors — power imbalance compromises consent.

Applies to you if: your project has a survey, a user study, interviews, or any observation of people.

Data privacy

Collect the minimum. Pseudonymise early. Set a retention period and delete. Never publish a dataset that can re-identify individuals.

Note: “publicly visible” is not “public”, and “public” is not “yours to use”. Check the licence and the terms of service — scraping is frequently a ToS violation.

India's DPDP Act 2023 governs personal data processing and applies to student research too.

Case A · The tidy dataset

A team runs five seeds. Four give 91–93% accuracy; one gives 67%. They cannot find a cause. Under deadline, they report the mean of the four and note “five runs were conducted”. The paper is accepted.

Case B · The helpful supervisor

A student's paper is accepted. Their supervisor asks that a colleague from another institution — who suggested the topic in a corridor conversation but did no work — be added as second author, saying “this is normal, it helps everyone”.

Case C · The convenient corpus

A team scrapes 40,000 public posts from a forum for a sentiment study. The forum's terms prohibit automated collection. No usernames are stored, but posts are quoted verbatim in the paper and are searchable back to their authors.

For each of two cases, write four things. This is the mark scheme.

2 · Who is harmed, and how? (1 mark)

3 · The repair. What should they have done, concretely? (1.5 marks)

4 · The prevention. What earlier decision would have removed the dilemma? (0.5 marks)

Consolidation

Eight things that must survive this hour

  1. Citation is owed to ideas, not just to words. A perfect paraphrase without a citation is still plagiarism.
  2. Self-plagiarism is real. Cite your own earlier work like anyone else's.
  3. Most student plagiarism starts in notes that lost their source. Quote-mark and key everything you copy, always.
  4. A similarity score is not a plagiarism score. Read the matches, not the number.
  1. Paraphrase by closing the source and writing from your notes — then integrate it into your argument.
  2. FFP ends careers; the grey zone — HARKing, metric-shopping, selective reporting — is far commoner and is Unit II errors gone unreported.
  3. Authorship needs contribution + approval + accountability. Agree it in writing, at the start.
  4. “Publicly visible” ≠ “public” ≠ “yours to use”. Check the licence and the terms.

If you remember one sentence: ask whether a reader would change their view of your work if they knew what you did — if yes, disclose it, and disclosure turns almost every grey-zone practice into a legitimate one.
