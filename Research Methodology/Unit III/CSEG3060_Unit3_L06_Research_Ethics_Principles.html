Principle I

Honesty, and its computing-specific failure modes

The truthful representation of methods, procedures, data, results and contributions — the bedrock on which the credibility of scientific knowledge rests.

Five obligations

  • Report results as observed, including negative findings
  • Disclose methodologies faithfully, including limitations
  • Avoid fabrication — inventing data, results or experiments
  • Avoid falsification — manipulating, altering or omitting data
  • Report errors promptly and correct the record

Failure mode 1 · selective reporting

Reporting only the best of many runs artificially inflates claimed gains. This is selective honesty — technically true statements that omit context and mislead.

Failure modes 2 and 3

Claiming an algorithm is “novel” when it is a renamed prior method · reporting on a cleaned subset without disclosing exclusions.

CS research is driven by software, hardware, datasets, benchmarks and trained models — and the reproducibility of every one of them depends on honest reporting.

The FFP framework

Research-integrity policy worldwide centres on Fabrication, Falsification, Plagiarism. Honesty is the positive disposition that prevents all three.

Operational practices

  • Maintain a tamper-evident research log — a version-controlled notebook
  • Preserve raw data with metadata
  • Adopt pre-registration for confirmatory studies
  • Uphold double-blind review norms
Note that reckless sits on the misconduct side: negligence severe enough to disregard obvious checks is not excused by absence of intent to deceive.

Principle II

Objectivity is a regulative ideal, not a property of the subject

The commitment to let evidence — not personal interest, ideology, prior commitment or external pressure — drive design, analysis and interpretation.

The CS-specific illusion

Computing feels objective because it handles formal systems. Yet every layer of an empirical CS study — dataset choice, hyperparameter selection, evaluation metric — is shaped by human judgement. Objectivity requires continuous effort.

1 · Confirmation bias

Emotional investment in a proposed method leads to benchmarks that unconsciously favour it.
Remedy: matched baseline conditions, standard suites, report across the full input distribution.

2 · Conflicts of interest

Industry funding, equity, consulting and personal relationships compromise disinterest.
Remedy: disclosure — necessary but never fully neutralising.

3 · Significance hunting

Tweaking architectures, hyperparameters, seeds or preprocessing until a result “works”.
Remedy: pre-registration, blinding, adversarial collaboration.

Every individual action in the significance-hunting loop is standard practice — which is exactly why the aggregate is so hard to see and so easy to rationalise.

Principle II · continued

You cannot report honestly what you cannot measure

The claim as usually stated

“12% faster.”

Without confidence intervals, standard deviations, hardware variance and sample-size justification, this is a number but not an empirical finding.

Effect size — how large is the difference?

Cohen's d = (μnew − μbaseline) / σpooled

Uncertainty — how well is it pinned down?

x̄ ± t0.025, n−1 · s / √n
Both are required. Effect size without uncertainty overstates confidence; uncertainty without effect size obscures practical relevance. Together they let readers assess the claim on its merits rather than on the author's enthusiasm.

Objectivity is partly a technical competence, not only a disposition — a researcher who cannot quantify uncertainty cannot report it honestly, however good their intentions.

Principle III

Integrity is consistency of character

Honesty

Pertains to specific statements and data. Can be satisfied statement by statement.

Integrity

Pertains to the character and consistency of the researcher across the whole research lifecycle. Cannot be satisfied piecemeal — it is the property of a pattern.

The operative test: a researcher of integrity acts ethically when no one is watching, when peer pressure pushes the other way, and when expedience tempts compromise.

What integrity subsumes

  • Adherence to institutional and professional standards
  • Consistency between public statement and private conduct
  • Willingness to take costly ethical stands — retracting one's own work, reporting misconduct
  • Asking “Is this the right thing to do?” before every significant decision

Operational practices

  • Adopt transparent authorship frameworks — CRediT
  • Establish lab agreements on data ownership and publication
  • Document difficult decisions and their reasoning at the time

Principle III · continued

Authorship, mentorship and whistleblowing

Authorship attribution

CRediT — 14 contributor roles: conceptualisation · methodology · software · validation · formal analysis · investigation · data curation · writing – original draft · writing – review and editing · visualisation · supervision · project administration · funding acquisition · resources

Two named abuses

Gift authorship — including someone who did not meaningfully contribute.
Ghost authorship — omitting a contributor whose work materially shaped the paper. The second is the one that harms students.

Order of authorship

The CS convention of alphabetical ordering sometimes masks the actual contribution distribution. Integrity requires transparent disclosure when the alphabet misrepresents the work.

Mentorship failures

  • Pressuring students to keep the lab's “story” consistent in reviews
  • Accepting authorship in exchange for funding or lab resources
  • Failing to credit students whose code and experiments populate papers

Whistleblowing

Whistleblowers face retaliation, ostracism and stalled careers, yet the practice is essential. Ombudspersons, confidential channels and protective law — U.S. Whistleblower Protection Act, EU Directive 2019/1937 — mitigate the cost.

Principle IV

Five categories of intellectual property in CS

Ideas, expressions, methods, data and code have authors with legitimate claims to attribution — and this principle extends beyond what law strictly requires.

A · Textual IP

Verbatim and idea plagiarism. CS papers are densely argumentative, so the threshold for attribution is lower than in many disciplines: even one lifted sentence is misconduct.

B · Software and code IP

Honour licence terms — GPL, MIT, Apache, BSD differ materially · do not re-implement a protected expressive structure after reading another team's code · cite the libraries you use · deposit your own code where possible.

C · Data IP

Cite dataset creators · respect licences (Creative Commons, dataset-specific terms) · never redistribute restricted data through side channels · respect the consent terms under which human-subjects data were collected.

D · Algorithmic and methodological IP

Unpatented algorithms still have authors. The community convention is eponymous citation: RSA, Dijkstra's algorithm, Adam (Kingma & Ba), Transformer (Vaswani et al.), ResNet (He et al.).

E · Trademark and brand IP

Naming a system after a competitor's trademark, or using proprietary screenshots beyond fair use, risks infringement — and is in any case unprofessional.

Principle IV · continued

Legal is the floor, not the standard

ActLegal statusEthical status
Verbatim copying of text without citationGenerally not illegal in small amounts (fair use)Unethical

Stockpiling research data for yearsGenerally legalMay be unethical if it blocks community progress

The two domains do not coincide

IP law is broader than research ethics in some respects (patent law) and narrower in others — many forms of idea plagiarism are perfectly legal and clearly unethical.

Operational practices

  • Track every idea, method and quotation in a citation manager
  • Run submissions through plagiarism detection before submitting
  • Maintain a licence-awareness checklist for every dependency
  • When paraphrasing, ensure the structure as well as the wording is yours
“It is legal” is never a sufficient answer to an ethical question. Legal compliance is assessed by counsel after the fact; ethical compliance is assessed by the community continuously.

Synthesis

One act, four principles — and a five-test workflow

Fabricating data is dishonest, decouples inference from evidence (objectivity), reflects a failure of character (integrity), and betrays the community's trust.

Plagiarism disrespects IP, is dishonest about authorship, corrupts the integrity of the record, and skews objectivity by inflating apparent support for a claim.

The five-test diagnostic workflow

  1. Honesty test — can I state this plainly, including what is less flattering?
  2. Objectivity test — has the evidence, not my preference, driven this conclusion?
  3. Integrity test — would I be comfortable if this were reported in the campus newspaper?
  4. IP-respect test — have I credited sources, code, data and methods?
  5. Public-reason test — can I justify this in a forum of respected peers?
If all five pass, the decision is almost certainly ethical. If any fails, further reflection — or consultation with an advisor or ethics officer — is warranted. A failed test is a prompt to consult, not a verdict of guilt.

Consolidation

Four takeaways, and three applied scenarios

Honesty

Truthful reporting of methods, data and findings — the antidote to fabrication, falsification and selective reporting.

Objectivity

Evidence rather than interest drives inference — managing conflicts, resisting confirmation bias, methodological rigour.

Integrity

Consistency across the whole lifecycle — authorship, mentorship, and defending truth at a cost.

Respect for IP

Attribution of ideas, text, code, data and methods, with licence compliance.

Scenario 1

A co-author removes your name from the byline because they disagree with your methodological criticism. How do you proceed, and which principles are at stake?

Scenario 2

Your advisor presses you to report benchmark results from a partial dataset because “the full dataset will take too long”. Identify the principles and propose a constructive course of action.

Scenario 3

An elegant open-source implementation does almost what you need. Compare (a) fork and modify with citation, (b) reimplement from the paper alone, (c) use as-is and relicense.

Weakness in one principle weakens the whole system. In a field whose outputs scale rapidly into society, adherence is constitutive of the discipline's claim to scientific status.

Exam preparation

Revision map and answer technique

Revision concept map

  • Honest error vs misconduct — intent, response, documentation, frequency
  • FFP — fabrication, falsification, plagiarism
  • Selective reporting vs negative-results reporting
  • Objectivity vs confirmation bias vs conflict of interest
  • Integrity vs honesty — character against specific statement
  • Authorship — gift, ghost, alphabetical, CRediT
  • IP categories and licence compliance — GPL, MIT, Apache, BSD, CC
  • The five-test diagnostic workflow

Answer technique

Name the principle, then the specific obligation breached, then the remedy. Prefer the omission-detecting reading of a scenario — most examination cases turn on what was left undisclosed.
