Running example

Student ‘X’ wants to “use AI to find brain tumours in MRI”

Classification vs. segmentation

Classification answers one question: is there a tumour? Segmentation goes further. It labels every pixel as tumour or healthy tissue, drawing the tumour's exact boundary.

That boundary matters clinically: measuring tumour volume, planning surgery and tracking response to treatment.

Public benchmark: the BraTS challenge (Menze et al., IEEE TMI 2015) provides MRI scans with expert-labelled tumour sub-regions.

A · Sources

Read the map before the territory

Step 1 · Secondary first

Read one good survey

  • Main method families
  • Benchmark datasets and metrics
  • Open problems named by experts
  • The key papers to read next

Step 2 · Primary next

Go deep on key papers

  • Exact architecture and loss
  • Data, pre-processing, splits
  • Results and their conditions
  • Limitations surveys skip
Common mistake: quoting U-Net's results from a survey without reading U-Net itself. Cite what you have actually read.

B · Boolean queries

Six operators do most of the work

OperatorWhat it doesExample
ANDBoth terms must appear: narrowssegmentation AND MRI
OREither term (synonyms): broadensglioma OR "brain tumor"
NOTRemoves a term: use sparinglyNOT "computed tomography"
" "Exact phrase"deep learning"
*Truncation: any word endingsegment* → segmentation, segmented
( )Groups terms, like brackets in maths(U-Net OR transformer) AND MRI
Rule of thumb: OR inside a concept, AND between concepts.

B · Boolean queries

Query contains one concept per line

("brain tumor" OR "brain tumour" OR glioma)

The problem. US and UK spellings, plus the clinical term.

AND segment*

The task. Matches segmentation, segmenting, segmented.

AND (MRI OR "magnetic resonance")

The modality. Abbreviation and full form.

AND ("deep learning" OR CNN OR U-Net OR transformer)

The method family.

NOT ("computed tomography" OR CT)

Out of scope. Check that no good papers were lost.

Tip: add one line at a time and watch the result count. Each AND should shrink it; each OR should grow it.

B · Boolean queries

Same idea, different syntax in each database

IEEE Xplore

Command Search accepts AND, OR, NOT, quotes and *. You can restrict each term to the title or abstract.

("Abstract":glioma) AND ("Abstract":segment*)

PubMed

Use MeSH, PubMed's controlled vocabulary, so every synonym for a disease is caught automatically.

"Brain Neoplasms"[MeSH] AND segmentation[tiab]

Google Scholar

Brackets and NOT are unreliable. Exclude terms with a minus sign; use allintitle: to search titles only.

glioma segmentation MRI -"computed tomography"

C · Databases

Know where the field publishes

Conferences

MICCAI · ISBI · MIDL · CVPR · NeurIPS

Where most new methods first appear. MICCAI is the flagship; U-Net was published there.

Journals

IEEE TMI · Medical Image Analysis

Extended, carefully reviewed versions of methods and benchmarks.

High-impact science

Nature Methods · Nature Comms

Field-changing tools: nnU-Net (Nature Methods, 2021) and MedSAM (Nature Communications, 2024).

Rule: a peer-reviewed paper outranks a preprint. Found it on arXiv? Check for a published version and cite that.

D · Citation chaining

Taming a paper with thousands of citations

1

Open the seed

On Google Scholar, find U-Net and click Cited by.

2

Search within

Tick “Search within citing articles” under the search box.

3

Narrow it

Type glioma OR BraTS. You now see only papers that build on U-Net and study brain tumours.

Visual tools

Semantic Scholar and Connected Papers draw citation networks, so clusters of related work stand out.

When to stop

At saturation: when new searches keep returning papers you already have.

Try it · 20 minutes

Your turn: based on your selected problem

Pick one topic

  • Polyp segmentation in colonoscopy
  • Retinal vessel segmentation
  • Skin lesion segmentation
  • Lung segmentation in chest X-rays

Then do this

  1. Find one survey paper on your topic
  2. Write a Boolean query with an OR group and a NOT
  3. Run it on IEEE Xplore and PubMed; compare the top 10
  4. Chain one seed paper: 2 backward, 2 forward
  5. Record every search in a log

Key takeaways

Four habits of a good literature search

1

Read a survey first, then the primary papers it points to.

2

OR joins synonyms, AND joins concepts, NOT is used sparingly.

3

For medical imaging, search one computing and one biomedical database.

4

Chain backward and forward from a strong seed paper, and log it all.
