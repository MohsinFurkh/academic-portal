Do first · 5 minutes · timed, strictly

Take one of your papers. You have five minutes. Go.

1 · 5 min — read one paper and answer the five questions below Do not read linearly. Do not read the middle. 5:00

Answer these five, in writing, in five minutes:

  1. Category. What kind of paper is it? Measurement study, new technique, analysis of an existing system?
  2. Context. Which other papers is it standing next to? Which theory or baseline does it assume?
  3. Correctness. Do the assumptions appear valid, on a first look?
  4. Contributions. What are its main contributions — in the authors' own claim?
  5. Clarity. Is it well written?

When the timer stops, answer one more question honestly: did you finish? And if you did not — what were you doing with your five minutes?

Almost everyone spends the five minutes reading the introduction slowly. That is the habit this hour exists to break.

Part A · the three-pass method

Pass 1: five minutes, and you never read a sentence in the middle

What you actually read

  1. Title, abstract, introduction
  2. Section and subsection headings only — nothing under them
  3. The conclusions
  4. Glance at the references — tick the ones you have already read

That is the whole pass. You answer the five Cs from the exercise, and then you make a decision:

Drop it. Not relevant, or not sound enough to matter. Record why — that line is your exclusion reason.

Cite it and move on. Relevant context, but you do not need its details.

Promote to pass 2. It is close enough to your gap to matter.

Of 50 papers, expect roughly 35 dropped or cited, 12 promoted to pass 2, 3 read in full. That distribution is the method working, not you cutting corners.

Why this is legitimate, not lazy

Papers are written to be read this way. The abstract, the introduction's last paragraph ("in this paper we…") and the conclusion are a deliberately redundant summary — authors know most readers will never reach section 4.

The economics. Reading 50 papers properly at 3 hours each is 150 hours. Three-pass gets the same coverage in about 20 — and spends the difference on the three papers that actually deserve it.

The failure mode it prevents. Reading papers linearly, in the order you downloaded them, until you run out of time — so you know paper 1 intimately, papers 2–7 vaguely, and papers 8–50 not at all. Your review then reflects download order, which is not a defensible basis for anything.

S. Keshav, “How to Read a Paper”, ACM SIGCOMM CCR 37(3), 2007 — two pages, and the most useful two pages you will read this semester.

Part A · when a paper earns more of your time

Pass 2: one hour · Pass 3: half a day

Pass 2 — about one hour

Goal: grasp the content well enough to summarise it to someone else with evidence. You are not verifying it.

  • Read the figures, tables and graphs carefully — this is where the paper's real claim lives. Are axes labelled? Is variance shown? Are the units sensible?
  • Read the body, but skip proofs and derivations.
  • Mark unread references for later — this is how you snowball backwards.
  • Note terms you did not understand and questions for the authors.

Outcome: you can state the claim, the evidence and the setting. If after an hour you still cannot, the paper is either badly written or outside your background — decide which, and note it.

Pass 3 — four to five hours

Goal: to re-create the work — the same assumptions, the same steps, and see whether you arrive where they did.

  • Virtually re-implement it: make the same assumptions and reconstruct the result.
  • Compare your reconstruction with what they wrote — differences reveal unstated assumptions.
  • Challenge every assumption, not just the ones they flagged.
  • Think about how you would have presented it; note ideas for future work.

Reserve this for two or three papers — the ones your own contribution stands directly on, or directly against. This is the pass that finds the gap nobody else noticed, and it is also how you end up spotting the error in a published paper.

The skill is not reading faster. It is deciding, in five minutes, how much a paper is worth — and being willing to stop.

Part B · reading as a reviewer

Appraise a paper with the machinery you already have

Nine questions, in order

  1. What is the claim? Find the sentence. If you cannot, that is the finding. (II.1)
  2. What is the quantifier? All, some, on-average-here? (II.5)
  3. What are the grounds? What did they actually measure or prove?
  4. What is the warrant, and did they defend it? (II.1)
  5. Which proof method? Demonstration, empirical, mathematical — and does it match the quantifier? (II.5)
  6. What is the baseline, and was it given a fair run? (II.4)
  7. Is variance reported? How many runs, seeds, participants? (II.4)
  8. Which validity threats apply, and did they name them? (II.1)
  9. What does this not establish? One sentence.

Two rules that keep appraisal honest

1 · Criticism must be specific and repairable. “The evaluation is weak” is not appraisal. “The baseline used default hyperparameters while the proposed method was tuned over 200 trials” is — it names the flaw, the mechanism and the fix. Anything vaguer than that tells your reader nothing and cannot go in your literature review.

2 · Read for what is usable, not only what is wrong. A paper with a weak evaluation may still contribute a good problem framing, a dataset, a metric, or a baseline you can reuse. Your job is not to award marks — it is to extract what advances your work.

The two sentences you write about every paper: “What it establishes is ___. What it does not establish is ___.”

The second sentence, gathered across 30 papers, is your research gap. You are not looking for it separately — you are accumulating it one paper at a time.

Part C · turning a pile into an argument

Three artefacts, built in this order

1 · Taxonomy

How does this field divide itself up?

Group the papers into 3–6 approaches. Name each group in the field's own vocabulary, not yours.

Test: every paper fits exactly one group, and a classmate would place them the same way. If papers straddle groups, your axis is wrong — try a different one.

Where it goes: the section structure of your literature chapter.

2 · Comparison matrix

Papers as rows. Dimensions as columns.

Columns that earn their place: approach · dataset · population/language · baseline · metric · evaluation type · scale · limitation stated.

Rule: every column must be one you could fill in for every paper. A column you can only fill for two papers is a note, not a dimension.

Where it goes: the table in your literature chapter that examiners look at first.

3 · Research-gap table

The matrix, read for what is missing.

Cross-tabulate two dimensions — say approach × population, or method × evaluation type — and count the papers in each cell.

The empty cells are your candidate contributions.

Where it goes: the paragraph that justifies your research question — and it justifies it with evidence rather than assertion.

The caution that matters. An empty cell is a candidate, not a contribution. Before claiming it, ask the Unit I question: is it empty because nobody has done it, or because it is not worth doing? Some cells are empty because the combination is meaningless, impossible, or was settled decades ago under different terminology. A gap you cannot explain the absence of is a gap you should be suspicious of.

Part C · what it looks like when it works

Approach × population, on 34 papers

Topic: automated crop-disease diagnosis from images. Cell values are paper counts.

Lab / studio images Field images, high-end camera Field images, low-end phone
Classical CV features 7 2 0
CNN, server-side 15 6 1
CNN, on-device 3 0 0 ← the gap

What the table says out loud: the field is heavily concentrated on studio-quality images with server-side inference. Fifteen papers occupy one cell. The deployment-realistic combination — on-device inference over phone-quality field images — has nobody in it.

Note that this is the population gap from Lecture I.3, and the new context novelty from I.4 — arrived at by counting rather than by hoping.

Now interrogate it before you commit. Is that cell empty because it is hard and valuable, or because it is uninteresting? Here: the constraint is real (farmers own cheap phones), the difficulty is real (domain shift plus compute limits), and the earlier cells show the accuracy claims that would be tested by filling it.

That reasoning — three sentences — is what turns “I found an empty cell” into a defensible research question.

Part C · the tooling · ten minutes now saves ten hours later

Set this up today, not in November

Zotero (recommended)

Free, open source, no storage limit locally. Browser connector grabs metadata and PDF in one click. Better BibTeX plugin gives stable citation keys.

Mendeley

Free tier with cloud sync, good PDF annotation, Elsevier-owned so it integrates with Scopus.

JabRef

Works directly on a .bib file. Ideal if you live in LaTeX and want no database in between.

The workflow, once

  1. Install Zotero and the browser connector.
  2. Create a collection for your project; sub-collections for your taxonomy groups.
  3. Save every paper at the moment you find it — not later. Later never comes.
  4. Check the metadata immediately. Auto-import routinely mangles conference names, authors and years.
  5. Tag by taxonomy group; put your two appraisal sentences in the Notes field.
  6. Export .bib, and cite with \cite{key}.

Four things that go wrong

  • Trusting auto-imported metadata. Google Scholar BibTeX in particular is often wrong about venue and year. Check every entry once; a wrong citation is a correctness error in your report, not a formatting one.
  • Citing the arXiv preprint when a peer-reviewed version exists. Always cite the published version.
  • Unstable citation keys that change on re-export and silently break your document.
  • Leaving it until the end. Reconstructing 40 references in the last week is a genuinely miserable and error-prone job — and it is where accidental plagiarism creeps in, which is exactly where III.3 begins.

IEEE, ACM and APA styles are all one dropdown in the same tool — which is why getting the data right matters far more than getting the style right.

Do · 14 minutes · your five papers · the hour's main event

Five papers in, one gap out

1 · 5 min — pass 1 on all five, two sentences each 2 · 4 min — build the comparison matrix 3 · 3 min — cross-tabulate, find an empty cell 4 · 2 min — swap and interrogate 14:00

Steps 1–3

1 · Pass 1 on each paper, one minute each. Title, abstract, headings, conclusion. Write the two sentences:

“What it establishes is ___. What it does not establish is ___.”

2 · Comparison matrix. Five rows. Columns:

paper | approach | dataset | population | baseline | metric | eval type | limitation

3 · Cross-tabulate two columns — approach × population is usually the productive pair — and count. With five papers most cells will be empty, so pick the most interesting empty cell and write one sentence on why it is empty.

Five papers is too few for a real gap table — you need 25–40. This is the mechanism at small scale so you can run it properly at home.

Step 4 · The interrogation

Swap tables with a neighbour. Their job is to ask, about your chosen empty cell, exactly three questions:

  1. “Is it empty because it is hard, because it is uninteresting, or because you searched with the wrong words?”
  2. “Who would act differently if this cell were filled?” — the so-what chain from I.4
  3. “What would you have to measure to fill it?”

Question 1 is the important one. The commonest honest answer in this room will be the third — the cell is empty because your search terms missed the work that fills it. That is a finding worth having now, because it sends you back to III.1's synonym list rather than into a project someone already did.

A gap that survives all three questions is a research question with evidence behind it.

Consolidation

Eight things that must survive this hour

  1. Three passes: five minutes · one hour · half a day — chosen deliberately, per paper.
  2. Pass 1 reads title, abstract, headings, conclusion, references. Nothing else.
  3. Of 50 papers: ~35 dropped or cited, ~12 to pass 2, ~3 read in full.
  4. Appraise with Unit II: claim · quantifier · grounds · warrant · method · baseline · variance · threats.
  1. Criticism must be specific and repairable — “the evaluation is weak” is not appraisal.
  2. Two sentences per paper: what it establishes · what it does not. The second column accumulates into your gap.
  3. Taxonomy → comparison matrix → gap table. The empty cells are candidate contributions.
  4. An empty cell you cannot explain the absence of is a cell to be suspicious of.

If you remember one sentence: write “what it does not establish” for every paper you read — thirty of those sentences, tabulated, is a literature review that argues instead of summarising.
