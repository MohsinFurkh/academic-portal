Foundational definition

A literature study is an argument, not a summary

The methodical identification, selection, evaluation and synthesis of existing scholarly works relevant to a research question — conducted to establish the state of knowledge and to justify new research.

Methodical

Without method it becomes a casual bibliography: sources accumulate, nothing is decided.

Critical

Without appraisal it only paraphrases: every source is treated as equally authoritative.

Synthetic

Without synthesis it advances nothing: five summaries are still five summaries.

Annotated bibliography — lists

  • Paper 1 says…
  • Paper 2 says…
  • Paper 3 says…

Literature study — adjudicates

  • Where the field agrees
  • Where it contradicts itself
  • What nobody has yet asked

Where the evidence lives

Six source categories — and why journals alone are not enough

Peer-reviewed journals

IEEE Transactions, ACM Computing Surveys, JMLR.

Conference proceedings

NeurIPS, ICSE, SIGCOMM, CVPR. In computing these are archival, not preliminary.

Books & dissertations

Books, monographs, and doctoral theses — the most exhaustive reviews available on a narrow topic.

Technical reports

Microsoft Research, Google DeepMind. Results of real consequence outside conventional venues.

Standards & patents

ISO, IEEE, NIST, W3C specifications — normative wording that must be quoted, not paraphrased.

Grey literature

arXiv, SSRN, white papers. Indispensable for currency; unrefereed.

Consequence: a CS literature study confined to journal articles alone is systematically incomplete.

Objectives · 1 of 3

State of the art, and the five kinds of gap

1 · Establish the state of the art

Map the theories, algorithms, architectures, datasets, benchmarks and findings that already exist. Without this map you cannot legitimately claim to address an open problem.

2 · Identify research gaps

The review is the mechanism by which gaps surface — and the articulation of a gap is itself a contribution.

Gap typeWhat is missingCS example
KnowledgeAn unanswered empirical or conceptual questionNo study measures energy cost of this layer
MethodologicalNo adequate method, tool or metricNo benchmark exists for the task
TheoreticalNo framework explains a class of observationsNo account of why scaling helps here
Population / contextAn under-studied dataset, domain or regionNever evaluated on non-English corpora
ReplicationPrior findings never independently validatedHeadline ML result never reproduced
A gap is a claim about the literature — it must be evidenced by a documented search, never asserted because you have not read enough.

Objectives · 2 of 3

Efficiency, refinement and method selection

3 · Avoid duplication

Prevents costly repetition of published work — acute in computing, where architectures and benchmarks proliferate faster than anyone can informally track.

4 · Refine problem and hypotheses

Reading reshapes the question: narrowing, broadening, re-framing, or operationalising it in measurable terms.

5 · Select methods

The literature is a repository of validated designs, metrics, statistical tests, simulators and tooling.

Objective 4 in action

Before reading

“Can AI detect disease?”

After reading

“Does a 3D U-Net trained on MIMIC-CXR improve segmentation Dice over 2D baselines?”

What Objective 5 buys you

  • Experimental design borrowed and adapted
  • The metric the field actually reports
  • The baselines reviewers will expect
  • The pitfalls predecessors documented
Diagnostic: a student whose research question is identical before and after the review has almost certainly not read critically.

Objectives · 3 of 3

Frameworks, credibility, and the ethical objective

6 · Build conceptual frameworks

Concepts, taxonomies and theoretical lenses make new observations interpretable rather than anecdotal.

7 · Establish credibility

Engaging with foundational work signals that the research was not conducted in intellectual isolation. Examiners expect this lineage.

8 · Support ethical practice

Wide reading familiarises you with attribution norms. A researcher who has read widely is far less likely to plagiarise.

Scope · 1 of 2

Declare your boundaries: sources and time horizons

Scope is a deliberately declared boundary, set to prevent both superficial coverage and unmanageable breadth. Undeclared scope is the root of most unfair criticism a review receives.

Source scope — five tiers

Primary — original research articles, full conference papers, patents

Secondary — surveys, reviews, meta-analyses, textbooks

Tertiary — ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web of Science, DBLP

Grey — arXiv, SSRN, technical reports, dissertations

Standards — ISO, IEEE, ACM, NIST, W3C

Temporal scope — three horizons

Foundational — Turing 1950, Dijkstra 1959

Contemporary — the last 3–5 years: current debates, datasets, benchmarks

Emerging — preprints signalling future direction

Working heuristic

60–70% of citations from the last five years, while deliberately retaining the classics that anchor your lineage.

Common error: relying only on secondary sources. Citing a survey's characterisation of a paper rather than the paper itself propagates any error the survey made — and examiners can detect it.

Scope · 2 of 2

Disciplinary, methodological and geographic boundaries

Disciplinary scope

A review of deep learning for medical image segmentation must traverse:

  • Core CS — ML, vision, software engineering
  • Applied mathematics — optimisation, probability, linear algebra
  • Domain — radiology, biomedical imaging, clinical workflow
  • Ethics and policy — data privacy, regulation

Because interdisciplinary literatures are unbounded, state which neighbouring fields are in scope and which are excluded, with reasons.

Methodological scope

Represent the field's methods — theoretical analysis, simulation, controlled experiment, case study, design science — so your own design is justified by contrast or extension, not by default.

Two biases to check for

Geographical bias — over-reliance on Anglo-American literature

Linguistic bias — excluding significant non-English work

These shape whose problems count as important and whose datasets count as representative.

Significance

Why it matters — at four levels

1 · Project

The foundation for every later stage. Without it the design is poorly motivated, the contribution unclear, the evaluation criteria unjustified.

2 · Researcher

Builds critical reasoning, synthesis, disciplinary vocabulary, and the capacity to position your work in a live conversation.

3 · Discipline

Aggregated across a community, reviews identify trends, controversies and frontiers, and give newcomers a defensible entry point.

4 · Society

As computing governs decisions, surveillance, healthcare and transport, the review is how researchers meet the social, legal and ethical consequences of their work.

Examinable distinction — scope is coverage: what is included. Significance is importance: why it matters. They are routinely tested against each other.

Societal level, concretely

A review of facial recognition that never engages the literature on demographic error-rate disparity has met a technical standard while failing a societal one.

Unit III thesis

The literature study is the ethical foundation of research

Ethical research practice begins long before data collection. This unit pairs literature studies with ethics because the two are causally linked — not merely adjacent.

Attribute ideas correctly and avoid plagiarism.

Recognise prior contributions; respect intellectual property.

Identify ethical controversies already debated in the literature.

Avoid the redundant — and ethically questionable — repetition of completed studies.

Mechanism, not exhortation: plagiarism is frequently a symptom of insufficient reading. A researcher who cannot restate a concept in their own words has not yet understood it.

Codified in the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018) and the IEEE Code of Ethics · developed further in Lectures 4–8 of this unit.

Practical application

Six types of review — and choosing between them

TypePurposeTypical use
NarrativeBroad overview of a topicThesis background chapter
SystematicExhaustive, protocol-driven synthesisMapping a whole evidence base
Meta-analysisStatistical synthesis of quantitative resultsPooling effect sizes
ScopingMaps breadth and nature of activityEmerging or heterogeneous fields
IntegrativeCombines empirical and theoretical workMature fields needing synthesis
UmbrellaSynthesises multiple systematic reviewsPolicy or curriculum decisions

Four selection determinants

  • The research question
  • The maturity of the field
  • The resources available
  • The depth of analysis required

Realistic expectation for you

A B.Tech or PG project usually embeds a narrative or integrative review in a background chapter — but elements of systematic method (predefined search strings, explicit inclusion criteria) are increasingly expected.

The process is iterative

Screening commonly forces you back to the search; synthesis regularly reveals the need to search again. Returning to an earlier stage is evidence of a maturing review, not of earlier failure.

One action today

Install Zotero or Mendeley this week. Retrofitting citations at submission time is where most accidental attribution failures occur.

Case study

Scope → state of the art → gap → contribution

Project proposal: “Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Healthcare Analytics”

1 · Scope defined

  • Foundational FL — McMahan et al., 2017
  • Differential privacy and secure aggregation
  • Healthcare datasets — MIMIC, UK Biobank
  • Regulation — HIPAA, GDPR
  • Benchmarks and evaluation metrics

2 · Gap identified

Recent surveys optimise accuracy under privacy constraints but neglect communication efficiency, energy consumption, and fairness across hospital sites with heterogeneous data.

3 · Contribution articulated

A communication-efficient aggregation algorithm with formal differential-privacy guarantees, evaluated across heterogeneous clinical sites — a claim now warranted by the review.

4 · Ethical dimension

The review must also surface literature on patient consent, secondary use of health data and bias in clinical algorithms — binding the project to the ethical theme of Unit III.

Your turn. For “Blockchain-based Trust Management in IoT Networks”, specify (a) three source categories, (b) two foundational works, (c) one likely research gap, (d) one ethical concern the review should surface.

Consolidation

What to retain, and what the examiner asks

Seven takeaways

  • Methodical, critical, synthetic — not casual reading, not a bibliography
  • Eight objectives — from state of the art to ethical practice
  • Scope spans sources, time, disciplines, methods, geography — and is declared
  • Significance at project, researcher, discipline and society levels
  • Types differ by purpose; the process is iterative
  • Above all, the ethical foundation of research

Exam focus

  1. Write a precise definition and distinguish it from a colloquial “literature survey”
  2. Know all eight objectives; develop any one at length with a CS example
  3. Scope vs significance — coverage against importance
  4. Primary / secondary / tertiary sources with a CS example of each
  5. The five gap types, and three review types compared with a scenario
  6. Always articulate the ethics link — the through-line of this unit
