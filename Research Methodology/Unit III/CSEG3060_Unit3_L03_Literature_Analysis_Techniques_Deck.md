## Slide 3 — The Four Operations of Literature Analysis

**Slide Content**
- **Where this lecture sits:** searching, screening and reference management are *preparatory*; analysis is the rigorous, argumentative engagement that positions your work within a wider intellectual conversation.
- **Key concepts at a glance:**

  | Concept | One-line definition |
  |---|---|
  | **Critical appraisal** | A structured evaluation of the trustworthiness and value of a research study. |
  | **Synthesis** | The integrative recombination of evidence across studies to produce new understanding. |
  | **Research gap** | An explicit, defensible absence in the body of knowledge that warrants new research. |
  | **Theoretical framework** | The conceptual scaffolding of theory that guides research questions, hypotheses and interpretation. |

- **Direction of attention:** appraisal looks **inward** at each study; synthesis looks **outward** across the corpus; gap identification looks at **what is absent**; framing supplies the **lens** through which all of it is read.
- **Not silos:** the four operations are recursive and overlapping — synthesis exposes gaps, gaps motivate the choice of theory, and theory redirects what you appraise.
- **Coverage claim:** the treatment is anchored in CS examples while drawing on social-science and engineering methodology, so both quantitative and qualitative bodies of evidence are addressed.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Use the inward/outward/absent/lens formulation as the mental index for the whole lecture, because students who hold these four directions can reconstruct the rest from first principles. Emphasise that this definitions table is examinable verbatim and that precision in the word *defensible* matters — a gap that cannot be demonstrated is an assertion, not a gap. Preview the recursion explicitly so that the linear order of the next ten slides is not mistaken for a strict procedure.

## Slide 4 — Critical Appraisal I: Purpose and the Three-Lens Model

**Slide Content**
- **Definition:** the disciplined act of asking not only *what* a paper claims but **whether those claims are warranted by the evidence presented** — the methodological conscience of the literature review.
- **Consequence of omitting it:** the review degenerates into an uncritical catalogue in which a weak preprint and a rigorously replicated study carry equal weight.
- **Discipline-specific hierarchy of evidence:** in health research the randomised controlled trial sits at the top; in computing the strongest evidence may be a **correctness proof** or a **benchmark on standardised datasets** — so appraisal frameworks must be *adapted*, not imported wholesale.
- **The three lenses:** **methodological** — were methods appropriate, rigorously executed, transparently reported? · **conceptual** — is the theoretical grounding coherent and are constructs well defined? · **contribution** — does the work advance knowledge beyond incremental description?
- **Recording practice:** for each lens record both *strengths* and *weaknesses*, then tag the paper with an appraisal summary — e.g. *"methodologically sound but conceptually narrow."*

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Insist on the distinction between reading for content and reading for warrant: students can usually restate a paper's claims but rarely ask whether the reported evidence could support them. Make the disciplinary point concrete — a formally verified protocol needs no experiment, while a deep-learning result without a held-out test set needs far more than one. Require the summary tag in their own extraction sheets, because forcing a one-line verdict is what converts passive reading into appraisal.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**

## Slide 5 — Critical Appraisal II: Structured Instruments and a CS-Adapted Checklist

**Slide Content**
- **Why standardised instruments:** none is universally mandated in computer science, but adopting one confers **transparency and defensibility** — the reviewer's judgement becomes auditable rather than personal.
- **CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme)** — ten questions, widely used for qualitative studies with variants for trials, cohort studies and systematic reviews: clear statement of aims · appropriateness of qualitative methodology · appropriateness of design · recruitment strategy · data collection · researcher–participant relationship · ethical considerations · rigour of analysis · clear statement of findings · value of the research.
- **AMSTAR-2** — *A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews, v2*: sixteen domains, including protocol registration, comprehensiveness of the search, justification for excluding studies, risk-of-bias assessment and consideration of publication bias. Use it when appraising *reviews*, not primary studies.
- **A CS-adapted checklist for computational studies:** are datasets, hyperparameters and random seeds reported? · is the setup reproducible (code, environment, container)? · are baselines appropriate **and recent**? · are statistical tests reported with **effect sizes and confidence intervals**? · is there a threats-to-validity section?
- **Examination note:** know **one** framework thoroughly — CASP is the most versatile — and be able to apply two of its questions to a sample paper.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Stress that AMSTAR-2 and CASP answer different questions, since students routinely apply a primary-study checklist to a survey paper and produce nonsense. Dwell on the "recent baselines" item, because comparing a 2025 method against a 2015 baseline is the most common way CS papers manufacture apparent improvement. Note that the reproducibility items are now enforced by artefact-evaluation tracks at ICSE, NeurIPS and similar venues, so this checklist reflects community practice rather than academic preference.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Three-column comparison: CASP (10 questions, qualitative primary studies) · AMSTAR-2 (16 domains, systematic reviews) · CS-Adapted (5 items, computational studies), with the "which instrument for which artefact" mapping shown as arrows from paper types to columns.

## Slide 6 — Critical Appraisal III: Threats to Validity and an Appraisal Verdict

**Slide Content**
- **Cook and Campbell's four validity types, operationalised for computing:**

  | Validity type | Question posed | Typical CS threat |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Internal** | Did the treatment actually cause the observed effect? | Confounds, hyperparameter leakage, data snooping |
  | **Construct** | Do the operationalisations measure the intended constructs? | Using accuracy as a proxy for "fairness" with no conceptual justification |
  | **External** | Can the findings generalise? | Single-dataset evaluation, no demographic diversity |
  | **Statistical conclusion** | Are the statistical inferences correct? | Multiple comparisons without correction, underpowered studies |

- **Worked appraisal — *"A Deep Learning Model for Phishing URL Detection Achieving 99.2% Accuracy":*** single dataset with **no temporal split** and possible leakage (methodological) · baseline is logistic regression only, no recent transformer-based detectors (methodological) · "phishing" adopted in a narrow URL-only sense that varies across the literature (conceptual) · headline metric misleading absent a sound protocol (contribution).
- **Appraisal verdict:** *promising idea, but the evidence does not yet support the claimed performance level — treat as preliminary.*
- **Transferable lesson:** a high reported number is a claim about an *evaluation protocol*, not about the world; appraisal interrogates the protocol.
- **Vocabulary discipline:** examiners reward precise use of *internal*, *construct*, *external* and *statistical conclusion* validity — never the generic word "reliable".

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Run this slide as a live appraisal: show the title, ask what students would believe, then dismantle it lens by lens and let the 99.2% collapse in front of them. Make the temporal-split point explicitly, since phishing URLs are non-stationary and random splits leak future information into training — a failure mode that generalises to fraud, intrusion and recommendation research. In the 90-minute version, hand out a one-page paper and have pairs produce a verdict sentence in six minutes.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Upper half: the four-validity table colour-coded by type. Lower half: a case box titled with the phishing paper's headline claim, structured as a four-row appraisal grid ending in a boxed **Verdict** line in contrasting colour.

## Slide 7 — Synthesis I: From a Stack of Summaries to an Argument

**Slide Content**
- **Definition:** the deliberate recombination of evidence from multiple sources into a coherent — often **new** — understanding. Without synthesis a review is a stack of summaries; with it, the review becomes an argument.
- **Five formal types:** **narrative** (textual integration by theme, method or chronology; flexible but exposed to reviewer bias) · **meta-analysis** (statistical aggregation of effect sizes; most rigorous, requires comparable metrics) · **thematic** (codes and themes developed inductively or deductively then mapped across studies; Thomas & Harden, 2008; increasingly used in software engineering) · **integrative** (combines empirical and theoretical sources; suits conceptual and design-science work) · **framework** (uses a pre-existing conceptual framework as the organising scaffold).
- **Conceptual schema:** $S = f(C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n)$, where $S$ is the synthesised claim and $C_i$ are coded observations.
- **The critical caveat:** $f$ is a **defensible inferential move** — aggregation, contrast or explanation — not a mechanical combination. Naming the move is what makes the synthesis auditable.
- **Selection logic:** comparable quantitative metrics → meta-analysis; heterogeneous qualitative or mixed evidence → thematic; an existing theory available as scaffold → framework synthesis.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Anchor the whole slide on the summary-versus-synthesis distinction, which is the most reliably examined idea in this lecture and the most commonly failed in submitted work. Explain the schema carefully: the formula is a reminder that a synthesised claim must be traceable to coded observations, and that the reviewer owes the reader an account of *which* inferential move was made. Connect back to Lecture 2 by noting that thematic synthesis is the workhorse for CS reviews precisely because commensurable effect sizes are so often unavailable.

## Slide 8 — Synthesis II: The Five-Stage Workflow, Demonstrated

**Slide Content**
- **Workflow:** **1 Tabulate** the corpus on an extraction sheet (authors, year, method, sample/dataset, findings, limitations) → **2 Code** each study (open coding for inductive, deductive coding for framework synthesis) → **3 Cluster** codes into themes → **4 Relate** clusters to the research questions, noting convergence and divergence → **5 Narrate** the relationships, citing studies as evidence *and* as counter-evidence.
- **Corpus — five studies on ML-based software vulnerability prediction:** A — Random Forest outperforms SVM on the NVD corpus · B — deep models excel only with large, balanced corpora · C — class imbalance remains the dominant performance barrier · D — static features alone are insufficient, semantic features improve recall · E — cross-project prediction is poor owing to distributional shift.
- **The summary (what students usually write):** "Study A found…, Study B found…, Study C found…" — five sentences, zero claims.
- **The thematic synthesis (what is required):** *"Across the corpus, prediction accuracy is shaped more strongly by data characteristics — imbalance, distributional shift — than by algorithmic choice. While deep models show potential in data-rich settings, classical models retain a Pareto advantage when labelled data is scarce."*
- **Why this qualifies as synthesis:** the claim appears in **none** of the five studies; it is produced by relating B, C and E (data effects) against A and D (model and feature effects) — a claim greater than the sum of its parts.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Put the summary version and the synthesis version side by side and let the class name the difference before you do — the contrast teaches faster than a definition. Trace the derivation aloud so the inferential move is visible: the data-characteristic theme emerges from three studies, the algorithmic theme from two, and the integrative claim is the *contrast* between them. In the 90-minute version, give pairs five one-line study findings and require one integrative claim in eight minutes.

## Slide 9 — Gap Identification I: A Taxonomy of Research Gaps

**Slide Content**
- **Definition:** a **defensible absence** — of evidence, theory, method, population, context or data — that justifies new research. Gap identification is the bridge between the literature review and the research question.
- **Six gap types** (adapted from Müller-Bloch & Kranz, 2015, and refined for computing): **empirical** — the phenomenon has not been examined, or not sufficiently · **theoretical** — existing theory fails to explain observed patterns, or no theory has been applied · **methodological** — prior work relies on a narrow set of methods and alternatives may yield different insight · **population/contextual** — findings are restricted to a specific demographic, geography or application context · **data** — required datasets do not exist, are inaccessible, or lack key attributes · **inconsistency** — existing studies report conflicting results that have not been reconciled.
- **Classification practice:** naming the *type* of gap is not pedantry — it determines the research design that can close it (a data gap needs a dataset contribution; an inconsistency gap needs a replication or a moderator analysis).
- **Diagnostic examples:** "no evaluation on low-resource Indic languages" → contextual · "two reviews report contradictory effect sizes" → inconsistency · "adoption theory never applied to quantum-computing-as-a-service" → theoretical.
- **The cardinal error — gap-by-omission:** claiming a gap because *you* found no paper, rather than demonstrating absence through a structured search.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Make the design implication explicit, because students treat gap classification as a labelling exercise rather than as the decision that determines what they will actually build or measure. Use the three diagnostic examples as a rapid-fire classification drill, since this exact item format appears in the short-answer section of the examination. Reinforce that gap-by-omission is simultaneously a methodological weakness and an honesty problem — the tie back to the unit's ethics theme.

**Visual / Layout Recommendation**
Six-card grid for the gap taxonomy, each card carrying a definition, a one-line CS example, and the *design implication* in a footer strip. Place a red warning chip beside the grid: "Absence must be demonstrated, not assumed."

## Slide 10 — Gap Identification II: The Canonical Grammar and Four Diagnostic Tests

**Slide Content**
- **Canonical gap-statement grammar:** *"Despite [extensive/some] research on [A] in [context C], [little/no] attention has been paid to [B], particularly with respect to [specific dimension]. This is consequential because [reason]. Therefore, this study investigates [research question] using [method] in [context]."*
- **What each clause does:** *Despite* — signals awareness of and respect for prior work · *Gap* — names the absence precisely · *Consequence* — demonstrates significance · *Therefore* — positions the new research.
- **Four diagnostic tests:** **evidence** — can the absence be demonstrated by a structured search? · **novelty** — is the gap distinct from gaps already addressed in recent literature? · **tractability** — can it be addressed with available resources, data and time? · **significance** — will closing it matter to theory, practice or both?
- **Worked example — adversarial robustness.** The literature shows: predominant use of ImageNet · evaluation almost exclusively of $L_{\infty}$-bounded perturbations · sparse study of realistic threat models (physical-world stickers, audio adversarial examples) · few evaluations across demographic subgroups.
- **Resulting statement:** *"Despite extensive research on adversarial robustness in image classifiers, limited attention has been paid to the intersection of robustness and fairness, particularly for $L_2$-bounded perturbations under realistic threat models. This intersection is consequential because deployed classifiers may fail unequally across subgroups, raising both ethical and operational concerns. Therefore, this study investigates the fairness–robustness trade-off in medical imaging classifiers using a constrained optimisation framework."*

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Tell students plainly that this grammar is the highest-yield sentence pattern in the unit and that they should be able to produce one in ninety seconds for any topic they are given. Walk the worked example clause by clause against the four observations above it, so they see that every specific phrase — $L_2$, realistic threat models, subgroups — is *earned* by a documented feature of the literature rather than invented. Note that the tractability test is the one students skip and supervisors enforce: a perfectly novel gap requiring inaccessible clinical data is not a viable project.

## Slide 11 — Theoretical Framing I: Frameworks, Distinctions and the CS Theory Repertoire

**Slide Content**
- **Definition and stance:** theoretical framing is the selection, adaptation and articulation of a theory that informs *every* subsequent methodological and interpretive choice. A theoretical framework is **not decoration — it is the organising logic of the research.**
- **Theoretical versus conceptual framework:**

  | Aspect | Theoretical framework | Conceptual framework |
  |---|---|---|
  | Source | Established, citable theory (TAM, DOI) | Researcher-built diagram linking variables |
  | Function | Supplies explanatory logic and propositions | Clarifies relationships to be tested |
  | Testability | Generates falsifiable hypotheses | Provides a roadmap for analysis |
  | Example | Technology Acceptance Model | A diagram linking code-review frequency, defect density and team size |

- **They can coexist:** a theoretical framework may *contain* a conceptual framework when the constructs are derived from theory but arranged for the specific study.
- **Recurring theories in CS research:** **TAM** (adoption via perceived usefulness and ease of use) · **UTAUT** (TAM extended with social and facilitating conditions) · **DOI** (diffusion of innovations) · **TOE** (technology–organisation–environment) · **Activity Theory** (HCI, CSCW mediated action) · **Constructivism / Situated Learning** (educational technology, CS pedagogy) · **General Deterrence Theory** (cybersecurity compliance) · **PDCA and variants** (software process improvement) · **Resource-Based View** (firm-level IT capabilities).
- **Failure mode to avoid — "theory-of-the-month":** adopting a theory because it is fashionable rather than because its constructs align with the research questions.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Confront the common student belief that theory is a humanities imposition irrelevant to systems work: without a framing lens, research questions are *empirically orphaned* and findings cannot be interpreted beyond the specific dataset. Use the table actively — ask students whether their own project has a citable theory or a self-drawn variable diagram, and note that the honest answer is often the latter, which is acceptable if labelled correctly. Point out that the repertoire listed here covers most B.Tech and PG work in adoption, HCI, security-behaviour and pedagogy sub-domains.

## Slide 12 — Theoretical Framing II: A Four-Step Construction Procedure

**Slide Content**
- **Step 1 — Identify candidate theories:** surface three to five candidates through seminal works, systematic reviews and citation tracing; competition between candidates is what makes the final choice defensible.
- **Step 2 — Map constructs to research questions:** verify that the theory's constructs align with what you are actually asking, and modify or extend the theory where they do not.
- **Step 3 — Derive propositions or hypotheses:** translate theoretical relationships into testable statements — e.g. $H_1: \beta_1 > 0$ for the effect of perceived usefulness on adoption intention.
- **Step 4 — Articulate assumptions:** state the boundary conditions under which the theory is believed to hold, so that later disconfirmation is interpretable rather than fatal.
- **Worked example — adoption of serverless computing among Indian startups.** *Theory:* **DOI combined with TOE**. *Constructs:* relative advantage, compatibility, complexity (DOI); firm size, top-management support, regulatory environment, competitive pressure (TOE). *Propositions:* $H_1$ — relative advantage positively affects adoption; $H_2$ — top-management support moderates the effect of complexity on adoption. *Assumptions:* startups operate under resource scarcity; respondents are technically literate.
- **Downstream consequence:** the framework now determines the survey instrument, the sampling strategy, the analysis (e.g. PLS-SEM) and the interpretation — which is precisely why framing precedes design.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Emphasise the examination heuristic here: when asked about theoretical frameworks, always close the loop from theory to method — "TAM implies mediation, therefore structural equation modelling is appropriate" earns marks that a description of TAM alone does not. Highlight the theory-combination move in the worked example, since single theories rarely span both individual-perception and organisational-context constructs. Note that Step 4 is what distinguishes a scholarly framework from an assertion: naming boundary conditions is how a theory remains falsifiable.

## Slide 13 — Integration & Case Study: Energy-Efficient Inference on the Edge

**Slide Content**
- **The recursive pipeline:** Critical appraisal (per study) → extraction and coding → synthesis (across studies) → gap identification (within and across themes) → theoretical framing (interpretive lens) → **research questions anchored in gap *and* theory**.
- **Step 1 — Appraisal:** 23 primary studies appraised and tagged by dataset, quantisation method, hardware target and reported metrics; studies flagged where latency, energy and accuracy are not reported *jointly*.
- **Step 2 — Synthesis:** thematic synthesis yields three convergent themes — the **quantisation-depth/accuracy trade-off** (8-bit emerges as the Pareto sweet spot) · **hardware–software co-design** outperforms algorithmic optimisation alone · **workload heterogeneity**, with findings diverging between vision and speech, implying context-dependent optimal policies.
- **Step 3 — Gap (methodological):** most studies optimise accuracy under a *fixed* energy budget; few explore **adaptive** policies responding to runtime context (battery level, network latency, user activity). *Statement:* "Despite extensive research on quantisation and pruning for energy-efficient edge inference, limited attention has been paid to adaptive policies that dynamically trade accuracy for energy based on runtime context. This is consequential because static configurations underutilise the flexibility of modern edge accelerators. Therefore, this study proposes a reinforcement-learning-based adaptive inference framework and evaluates it on a Jetson Nano platform."
- **Step 4 — Framing:** the **Markov Decision Process** formalism supplies the theoretical lens, with the policy learned by deep reinforcement learning; state, action and reward are derived from system-level measurements and propositions state expected policy behaviour under varying reward weights.
- **The two failure modes this avoids:** appraisal and synthesis performed but framing skipped, leaving research questions **empirically orphaned**; or sophisticated theory undermined by a **poorly evidenced gap**. Mastery requires all four.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Present this as proof that the four operations compose: the flagged reporting omission in Step 1 becomes the heterogeneity theme in Step 2, which exposes the adaptivity gap in Step 3, which the MDP formalism in Step 4 makes tractable. Draw particular attention to the framing step, since students in systems-oriented work assume theory does not apply to them — an MDP *is* their theory, and stating it converts an engineering tweak into a research contribution. Close by naming the two failure modes and asking which one their current draft is closer to.

## Slide 14 — Summary, Common Pitfalls & Discussion Questions

**Slide Content**
- **Four takeaways:** **critical appraisal** evaluates each study's trustworthiness and contribution using a structured checklist and the four-validity framework · **synthesis** integrates evidence into new defensible claims through narrative, thematic, meta-analytic, integrative or framework approaches · **gap identification** articulates absences precisely via the six-type taxonomy, the canonical grammar and four diagnostic tests · **theoretical framing** anchors the work in citable theory, generates testable propositions and governs every methodological decision.
- **The techniques are interdependent** — literature analysis is the foundation on which all methodological choices rest, and the review chapter is frequently the most cited section of a thesis or paper.
- **Five pitfalls and their mitigations:**

  | Pitfall | Description | Mitigation |
  |---|---|---|
  | Cataloguing | Listing studies without evaluation | Append appraisal and synthesis sentences to every cluster |
  | Gap-by-omission | Claiming a gap because no paper was found | Demonstrate absence with a structured search |
  | Theory-of-the-month | Adopting a fashionable theory | Justify selection by construct alignment |
  | Synthesis-by-citation | Dropping names without integrating findings | Write integrative claims, cite evidence *for and against* |
  | Mixing levels | Conflating empirical findings with opinion | Separate evidence from interpretation explicitly |

- **Discussion questions:** (i) Differentiate critical appraisal from synthesis with a one-sentence example of each from a paper you have read. (ii) Rewrite a summary paragraph from your own draft so that it becomes genuinely synthetic. (iii) A senior researcher claims that *"in fast-moving CS sub-fields, formal theoretical framing is impossible because theories become obsolete quickly"* — evaluate this with reference to two theories from Slide 11.
- **Higher-order prompts:** Are systematic reviews inappropriate for CS given the absence of standardised reporting conventions — defend or refute · Diagnose the claim *"Across all surveyed studies, larger models always outperform smaller ones"*: name the synthesis technique, the embedded assumptions, and the conditions under which it fails.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Consolidate by returning to the guiding claim from Slide 1 and asking the class which of the four operations converts a corpus into an argument — the answer is all four, in composition. Use the pitfalls table diagnostically: ask students to identify which pitfall their own draft review currently exhibits, since self-diagnosis is more durable than correction. Set the "larger models always outperform" prompt as the marquee discussion item, because it exposes overgeneralised synthesis, the scaling-regime assumption and the data-budget caveat in a single example.

## Slide 15 — Exam Preparation & Further Reading

**Slide Content**
- **Internalise the gap-statement grammar** — examiners routinely require students to formulate *or critique* a gap statement; practise writing five across different CS sub-fields (NLP, security, HCI, systems, SE).
- **Master one appraisal framework thoroughly** — CASP is the most versatile; know its ten questions and be able to apply two of them to a sample paper on demand.
- **Distinguish synthesis from summary, in writing** — be prepared to convert a summary paragraph into a synthetic one under examination conditions, and know the five synthesis types with one "use when" condition each.
- **Always connect theory to method** — e.g. "TAM suggests testing mediation, hence structural equation modelling is appropriate"; a framework described without its methodological consequence is an incomplete answer.
- **Use the threats-to-validity vocabulary precisely** — *internal*, *construct*, *external*, *statistical conclusion* — and be able to pair each with a CS-specific threat; also know the six gap types and be ready to classify unseen examples.
- **Follow the worked-example format** — when asked to demonstrate any technique, answer in the lecture's structure: **definition → procedure → example → pitfalls.**
- **Further reading:** Booth, Sutton & Papaioannou (2016) · Cronin, Ryan & Coughlan (2008) · Kitchenham & Charters (2007) · Müller-Bloch & Kranz (2015) · Thomas & Harden (2008) · Webster & Watson (2002) · Wohlin (2014) on snowballing.

**Professor Speaker Notes**
Set the five gap statements as compulsory written practice, since this is the one skill on which fluency cannot be faked in an examination hall. Recommend Webster & Watson (2002) as the highest-value short reading, because its argument for *concept-centric* rather than author-centric writing is the direct cure for synthesis-by-citation.
