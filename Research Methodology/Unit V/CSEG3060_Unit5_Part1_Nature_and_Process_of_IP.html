Where we are

Your project is finished. Now decide what happens to it.

The running example reaches a decision point

The segmentation pipeline works: Dice recovers 0.11 on clinical scans with a missing MRI sequence. A hospital wants to pilot it. A start-up has offered to commercialise it. Your supervisor wants a MICCAI paper by next month.

  • Can any of this be patented — it is software?
  • Who owns it: you, the university, the hospital, the funder?
  • If you publish first, what is left to protect?
  • If you patent first, what does it cost and who pays?

Why a research methodology course covers IPR

Because these decisions are made before the research ends, not after — and they are irreversible in one direction. A disclosed invention cannot be un-disclosed.

Because your obligations under a funding agreement, an institutional IP policy or a hospital data agreement are already binding on you.

And because the same literature-search skill from Unit III is, in this unit, called a prior-art search — and it decides whether a patent is worth filing at all.

Units I–IV asked is it true, and can you explain it? Units V–VI ask is it yours, and what can you do with it?

Nature of IP

Four properties that explain almost everything about IP

1 · It is intangible

The property is the creation of the mind, not the object. Selling a copy of your software transfers the copy, not the copyright in it.

2 · It is a negative right

A patent does not give you the right to practise your invention. It gives you the right to stop others — you may still be blocked by someone else's patent.

3 · It is territorial

An Indian patent stops nobody in Germany. There is no such thing as a world patent — only routes, such as the PCT, that make filing in many countries easier.

4 · It is time-limited

Twenty years for a patent, then it belongs to everyone. The system is a bargain: you disclose how it works, the public gets it eventually.

Hold on to the bargain: disclosure in exchange for a limited monopoly. Every rule that follows — novelty, publication at 18 months, working requirements, compulsory licensing — exists to keep one side of that bargain honest.

Patents

What a patent actually is

The bargain

You publish a complete, enabling description — enough for a skilled person to work the invention. In exchange the State grants you, for 20 years from the filing date, the right to stop others from making, using, selling, offering for sale or importing it in that country.

After that it is public domain. The disclosure is the price, and it is paid whether or not the patent is ever granted — applications publish at 18 months.

What a patent is not

  • Not a licence to practise. Your improvement may still infringe someone's broader patent.
  • Not automatic. It is examined, opposed, and often narrowed before grant.
  • Not worldwide. One country, one right.
  • Not free. Filing, examination, attorney drafting and renewal fees every year — abandonment for non-payment is common.
  • Not a research result. A granted patent is evidence of novelty, not of the invention working well.
The three tests an invention must pass — novelty, inventive step and industrial applicability — plus the Section 3 exclusions, are the whole of Part 2.

Designs

Designs protect how a thing looks, not how it works

What qualifies

Features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to an article, judged solely by the eye. It must be new or original and not previously published anywhere.

What does not

Anything dictated purely by function, mere mechanical devices, trademarks and artistic works as such. If the shape is the only way to make it work, it is a patent question, not a design one.

Practical points

Registered for 10 years, renewable by 5. Classified under the Locarno system. Registration is relatively fast and cheap — which makes it the most under-used right in student hardware and product projects.

For computing projects: the appearance of a device enclosure, an instrument panel or a wearable is design subject matter. Graphical user interfaces and screen displays are treated differently across jurisdictions and the practice has shifted over time — if a GUI is commercially important, check the current Indian position with a practitioner rather than assuming either answer.

Copyright

Copyright protects expression, never the idea

What you get, automatically

  • Protection on creation — no registration needed, no notice required (registration is optional and helps as evidence)
  • Computer programmes are literary works under the Copyright Act, 1957 — source and object code
  • Economic rights: reproduce, adapt, translate, distribute, communicate to the public
  • Moral rights: attribution and integrity, which stay with the author even after the economic rights are assigned
  • Term for literary works: the author's life plus 60 years

What it will not do for you

It does not protect the idea, method or algorithm. Anyone may read your paper, understand the technique and write their own implementation from scratch. That is not infringement — it is how science works.

Copying your code, your figures or substantial parts of your text is infringement — and is also the plagiarism question from Unit III, now with a legal remedy attached.

Fair dealing (§52) permits certain uses — including private study, research, criticism and review — within limits.

Open-source licences are copyright instruments. GPL, MIT and Apache work by granting permissions the copyright owner would otherwise withhold — which is why ignoring a licence file is a copyright problem, not a courtesy problem.

Trade secrets

The right that exists only while nobody knows

How it works in India

There is no dedicated trade-secrets statute. Protection comes from contract (NDAs, employment and consultancy terms), equitable duties of confidence, and practical security measures.

That makes the paperwork the protection: if no agreement says the information is confidential, and it was shared freely, there may be nothing to enforce.

Secret or patent? The trade-off

Choose a patent whenthe invention can be reverse-engineered from the product, and you need to stop competitors
Choose secrecy whenit cannot be seen from outside, and you can keep it contained — a process recipe, tuning know-how

Secrecy has no expiry — but also no protection against independent invention. Someone who invents it separately owes you nothing, and may patent it.

For a research student this is mostly a discipline question: what you put on a public repository, a preprint server, a poster or a slide deck stops being a secret permanently — and, as the next slides show, may also stop being patentable.

Ownership

Inventor, applicant, owner — three different people

Inventor

Whoever actually conceived the invention. This is a question of fact, not of seniority — and naming a supervisor who contributed nothing, or omitting a student who did, is a defect in the application.

Applicant / owner

Whoever holds the right to apply — usually the employer or institution under an employment contract or IP policy, or a funder where the grant terms say so.

Author (copyright)

For works made in the course of employment, the employer is usually the first owner, subject to contract. For a student's own thesis, the position depends on your institution's policy — read it.

Four documents that have already decided this for you

1 Your institution's IP policy · 2 your funding or fellowship agreement · 3 any industry or hospital collaboration agreement · 4 the copyright transfer form you sign with a publisher. Read all four before the first disclosure, not at the point of a dispute — and if a collaboration has no written IP clause, that is itself the problem to raise.

The disclosure trap

Publishing first can destroy the patent you were going to file

Why

Novelty is tested against everything already available to the public anywhere in the world — including your own paper, preprint, poster, demo, thesis in the library, GitHub repository or conference talk.

Your own disclosure counts against you. India has a limited grace for certain disclosures — for example, papers read before a learned society or display at a notified exhibition, within twelve months — but the conditions are narrow and many countries give no grace at all.

The practical rule

File before you disclose. A provisional specification is the standard tool: it secures a priority date quickly and cheaply, and you have twelve months to file the complete specification.

So the sequence is: draft provisional → file → then submit the paper, upload the preprint, give the talk.

Tell your IP cell early. “We are submitting to MICCAI in three weeks” is a workable timeline; “the paper came out last month” usually is not.

This is the one slide in Unit V with an irreversible consequence. Everything else can be repaired; a public disclosure cannot be taken back.

The process

Where the IP decisions sit in the research pipeline

Research

A finding

Units I–III. Keep a dated, signed lab notebook — it is evidence of conception.

→

Invention disclosure

The decision point

Tell the IP cell before publishing. Prior-art search decides whether to file.

→

Protection

File

Provisional, then complete. Publish freely once the priority date exists.

→

Development

Make it usable

Prototype, validate, pilot with the hospital. Costs money, which is what the licence pays for.

→

Transfer

Licence or spin-off

Unit VI: who may use it, on what terms, for what royalty.

The gap that kills most academic inventions is between “protection” and “development”. A patent on a method that has never run outside a lab is hard to license: nobody is buying the certificate, they are buying evidence that it works in their setting. Your pilot data — the same evidence Part 3 of Unit IV wanted for the proposal — is what makes the right worth something.

Try it · 20 minutes

Map the IP in your own project

In your team (12 min)

  1. List six outputs of your project: method, code, dataset, figures, interface, name, know-how.
  2. For each, name the instrument (patent / design / copyright / trademark / secret / none) and one sentence of why.
  3. Name the owner of each — and the document that decides it.
  4. Write your disclosure timeline: what has already been made public, when, and where.
  5. Decide: is there anything here worth filing before you publish?

Swap and challenge (8 min)

  • Any output they claimed as patentable that is really just code? (Copyright, and see Section 3(k).)
  • Any output they called IP that is actually data, governed by ethics and the DPDP Act instead?
  • Any ownership claim with no document behind it?
  • Has anything already been disclosed — a demo, a GitHub push, a poster — that would defeat novelty today?

Keep the sheet. In Part 2 you run a real prior-art search against the item you chose to protect.

Key takeaways

Five things to remember about IP

1

IP is intangible, negative, territorial and time-limited — and the bargain is disclosure for a limited monopoly.

2

Different instruments protect different things: patent = invention, design = appearance, copyright = expression, trademark = origin, secret = confidence.

3

One project carries several rights at once — and some valuable assets, such as patient data, are not IP at all.

4

Ownership is already decided by your IP policy, funding terms and the forms you sign. Read them before the first disclosure.

5

File before you publish. A public disclosure cannot be withdrawn.
