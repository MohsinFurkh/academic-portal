The starting definition

Not every good idea is an invention

An invention, in the Act's terms

A new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application.

Three conditions, all of which must hold — and then it must also survive the excluded subject matter in Section 3.

Three things that are not inventions

  • A discovery: finding a principle that was already there. Discovering that a particular MRI texture correlates with tumour grade is a discovery; a machine that uses it to do something may be an invention.
  • A mere idea with no way of working it. “A system that diagnoses any disease from any scan” is a wish, not a disclosure.
  • An obvious combination of things already known, with no unexpected result.
The examiner's habitual question is the same one from Unit I: what exactly is the technical problem, and what exactly solves it? If you cannot answer in one sentence, the claim is not ready.

Test 1 · Novelty

Novelty is absolute, worldwide, and unforgiving

What destroys novelty

Anything published or publicly used anywhere in the world before your priority date — a paper, a preprint, a patent application, a thesis on a shelf, a conference poster, a public demo, a product on sale, a public GitHub repository.

Including your own work. Self-disclosure is the most common way Indian academic inventions lose novelty.

The single-document rule

Novelty is destroyed only if one prior disclosure contains all the features of your claim. Two documents that together cover it do not defeat novelty — they defeat inventive step instead, which is the next test.

This is why a narrow claim can be novel while a broad one is not: the fewer features you claim, the easier it is for one old document to contain them all.

India recognises a limited grace for certain disclosures — for example a paper read before a learned society or display at a notified exhibition, within twelve months. The conditions are narrow, and many jurisdictions give no grace at all. Never plan around it.

Test 2 · Inventive step

Would it have been obvious to a skilled person?

The Indian formulation

An inventive step is a feature that makes the invention not obvious to a person skilled in the art, and that involves technical advance compared with existing knowledge, or economic significance, or both.

The “person skilled in the art” is a legal fiction: a competent practitioner who knows everything published in the field but has no inventive imagination.

How examiners argue it

Usually by combining documents: “D1 teaches a 3D U-Net; D2 teaches dropout of input channels; combining them is obvious.”

Your counter-arguments: the documents point away from each other; there was no reason to combine them; the combination gives an unexpected result; the problem had persisted despite both being known; or the field had a prejudice against your route.

The trap for researchers: hindsight. Once you know the answer, every step looks obvious — which is exactly the bias the examiner is applying to your work. Evidence against obviousness is the same evidence your paper needs anyway: a surprising number, a baseline that fails, a limitation other people accepted.

Test 3 · Industrial applicability

Capable of being made or used in an industry

A low bar, usually

Most engineering inventions pass without argument. The requirement excludes things that cannot work at all — a perpetual motion machine — or that have no identified use.

Where it bites

Purely theoretical results, and claims so abstract that no concrete use is stated. A model with no application named in the specification invites the objection.

What to do about it

State at least one concrete industrial use in the specification, and show the invention works — a worked example, a measurement, a result table. Your Unit IV results section is the raw material.

Sufficiency of disclosure is the related duty. The specification must enable a skilled person to work the invention without undue experimentation. Writing “a suitable neural network is trained appropriately” fails that test — and holding back the crucial detail to keep it secret is how applications get refused, or granted and then revoked.

The two clauses that decide your project

3(k) and 3(i), applied to a medical AI pipeline

3(k) — computer programme per se

A claim to “an algorithm that computes X” or “a program that, when run, segments an image” is squarely within the exclusion.

What examiners look for instead is a technical effect beyond the ordinary running of a computer: a measurable improvement in the functioning of hardware or of a technical process — faster inference within a fixed memory bound, reduced acquisition time, control of a device.

3(i) — methods of diagnosis and treatment

“A method of diagnosing glioma in a patient” is excluded subject matter.

Applicants therefore claim the apparatus or system — an imaging system configured to produce a segmentation output — and describe the technical process it performs, rather than the clinical act performed on a person.

Practical reading for this course: the outcome usually turns on how the contribution is characterised, not on whether software is involved. The Indian position is shaped by the Patent Office's guidelines on computer-related inventions and by decisions such as Ferid Allani, which directed attention to the word “per se” and to technical effect. Practice evolves — Unit VI Part 3 takes this apart, and a practitioner should confirm the current line before you file.

Before you file

A prior-art search is a literature search with different stakes

How it differs from Unit III

  • The corpus is patents plus everything else: papers, products, manuals, theses, repositories, standards.
  • You search by classification — IPC and CPC codes — as well as keywords, because patent language avoids your vocabulary on purpose.
  • You are looking for the closest single document, then for combinations.
  • A negative result is never proof of novelty; it is evidence of reasonable diligence.

A workable procedure

  1. Write your invention as one sentence with its technical features listed.
  2. Generate synonyms for each feature — patents say “image data set”, not “scan”.
  3. Find one relevant patent, read its classification codes, then search by code.
  4. Chain: read its citations and the documents citing it (Unit III Part 1, again).
  5. Record every query in a search log with database, date and counts.
  6. Fill a feature-by-document matrix: which document shows which feature?
Databases and hands-on searching — InPASS, Espacenet, PATENTSCOPE, Google Patents, USPTO — are Unit VI Part 2, which is run as a lab on your own project ideas.

If one column is all ticks

Not novel. Narrow the claim to a feature that document does not have, or stop — and publish instead.

If two columns together cover it

Novel but vulnerable on inventive step. You will need the argument from slide 5: why would nobody have combined them?

If some rows are empty everywhere

Those rows are your candidate inventive features. They belong in claim 1 — and they must be described in enough detail to be enabled.

Drafting

Provisional or complete: what each one buys

Provisional specification

Describes the invention but need not contain claims. Cheap, fast, and it fixes your priority date — which is the date novelty is measured against.

You then have twelve months to file the complete specification, or the application lapses.

Warning: only what is disclosed in the provisional gets that early date. A vague provisional buys a date for nothing.

Complete specification

The full document: title, field, background, object, summary, drawings, detailed description sufficient to enable, at least one example, and the claims — which define the legal monopoly.

It also carries the abstract and, in India, must be supported by the disclosure: you cannot add new matter later.

For a student with a conference deadline, the standard sequence is: write the provisional from your paper draft — it is often 80% the same text, plus the technical-effect framing — file it, then submit the paper. Twelve months later you will also know, from reviews and follow-up experiments, whether the complete specification is worth its cost.

Claims

The claim is the invention; everything else is explanation

Structure of a claim

Preamble (what it is) + transition (“comprising”) + elements (the features, each doing something).

1. An imaging system comprising: a memory storing a segmentation model; a processor configured to determine which of a plurality of image sequences are present; select a sub-model corresponding to the available sequences; and generate a segmentation map within a predetermined memory budget…

“Comprising” is open — extra features still infringe. “Consisting of” is closed, and much narrower.

Breadth is the whole game

Every word you add narrows the claim — making it easier to grant and easier for a competitor to design around. Every word you remove broadens it — and makes it more likely that one old document anticipates it.

So applications carry independent claims (broad) and dependent claims (narrower fallbacks) — if the broad one falls in examination or opposition, a narrower one may survive.

Method claims, system claims and computer-readable-medium claims are often filed together, because each is infringed by a different party.

Read one real claim before your next lecture. Open any patent in your field, ignore the description, and read claim 1 twice. You are reading the boundary of a property right, written as a single sentence.

Try it · 25 minutes

Test your own project for patentability

In your team (15 min)

  1. Write your invention as one sentence, then list its technical features as separate lines.
  2. Search Google Patents or Espacenet for the closest two documents. Note their classification codes.
  3. Fill the feature × document matrix from slide 10.
  4. Decide: novel? inventive? Which feature is doing the work?
  5. Check it against Section 3 — especially 3(k), 3(i) and 3(m).
  6. Draft claim 1 in the preamble–comprising–elements form.

Swap and act as examiner (10 min)

  • Can you find one document that has every feature of their claim 1? Novelty objection.
  • Can you find two that together do, with a reason to combine? Inventive-step objection.
  • Does the claim read as a computer programme or algorithm as such? Section 3(k) objection.
  • Does it claim a method of diagnosis performed on a patient? Section 3(i) objection.
  • Could a skilled person work it from what they wrote? Sufficiency objection.

Write your objections the way an examination report does: the clause, the document, and the specific feature.

Key takeaways

Five rules of the patenting process

1

Three tests — novelty, inventive step, industrial applicability — and then the Section 3 exclusions, which are where computing inventions fail.

2

Novelty is absolute and worldwide, defeated by a single document containing every feature — including your own paper.

3

A prior-art search with a feature matrix tells you whether to narrow, argue or publish instead.

4

A provisional buys a priority date for what it actually discloses; the complete must enable the invention and cannot add new matter.

5

The claims are the invention. Every word narrows or broadens a property right.
