  1. Apply the substantive criteria of patentability — novelty, inventive step and industrial application — and the Section 3 exclusions relevant to computing.
  2. Conduct a structured prior-art search using appropriate patent and non-patent databases.
  3. Assess novelty claim-by-claim and distinguish it rigorously from inventive step.
  4. Prepare a competent invention disclosure capturing the technical information a patent attorney requires.
  5. Draft the structural and linguistic elements of a complete patent specification, including independent and dependent claims.

2. Patenting Within the Research Lifecycle

For a computer science researcher, the patenting process is rarely a downstream, post-thesis formality. In contemporary practice — especially in artificial intelligence, cryptography, networking protocols and hardware–software co-design — patenting decisions are made during the research lifecycle. Researchers publish, file patent applications and protect trade secrets in a parallel and tightly coupled workflow.

The transformation chain
R  →  I  →  D  →  S  →  G(1)

where R is the research output, I the invention, D the disclosure, S the specification and G the granted patent. The transitions are conception, disclosure, drafting and examination. Each demands its own methodological discipline.

The pipeline has four operational pillars before filing: prior-art search, novelty assessment, invention disclosure, and drafting of the specification.

Where patentable inventions come from

Patentable inventions typically emerge from research at Technology Readiness Level 3–7 — proof of concept through prototype demonstration. Lower-TRL basic science and higher-TRL manufacturing scale-up yield fewer patentable inventions, though they may produce other forms of IP such as trade secrets, know-how and regulatory data.

Patentability versus freedom to operate

These are different questions and are routinely conflated. Patentability asks whether you can obtain a patent. FTO asks whether you can commercialise without infringing someone else’s. You can have one without the other.

4. Patentability Criteria

4.1 Novelty

Under Section 2(1)(j), “new” is interpreted as absolute or global novelty — the invention must not form part of the prior art anywhere in the world, in any language, in any form, before the filing or priority date.

This contrasts with the United States, which historically applied a first-to-invent rule, modified by the America Invents Act, 2011 to align with the global first-to-file standard. India provides a limited 12-month grace period for disclosures arising from exhibitions or academic publications by the inventor — narrow, and not to be relied upon.

Where novelty questions actually arise in computing
  • Open-source software releases that disclose the algorithmic innovation.
  • Conference publications at NeurIPS, SIGCOMM and similar venues that predate the filing.
  • Public demonstrations at hackathons or product launches.

Every one of these is prior art against your own application.

4.2 Inventive Step

Section 2(1)(ja) defines an inventive step as a feature involving a technical advance compared to existing knowledge, or having economic significance, or both, and that makes the invention not obvious to a person skilled in the art.

In the United States the analogous standard under 35 U.S.C. §103 is assessed against the Graham v. John Deere Co. (1966) framework, which considers: (1) the scope and content of the prior art; (2) differences between the prior art and the claims; (3) the level of ordinary skill in the pertinent art; and (4) secondary considerations — commercial success, long-felt need, failure of others.

Example

An algorithm that merely combines known sorting techniques without producing a measurable technical advance would lack an inventive step, even if the combination is novel.

4.3 Industrial Application

Section 2(1)(ac) requires that the invention be capable of being made or used in an industry, where “industry” is read broadly under Section 2(1)(m) to include any manufacturing, service or commercial activity. For software patents this criterion requires a specific, substantial and credible utility going beyond an abstract idea.

4.4 Statutory Exclusions — Section 3

ExclusionDescriptionCS relevance
Section 3(a)Frivolous inventions, or contrary to natural lawsPure mathematical methods without specific application
Section 3(k)A mathematical or business method, or a computer program per se, or algorithmsThe governing provision for every software patent question in India
Section 3(m)Mere arrangement, re-arrangement or duplication of known devicesRoutine UI changes without technical effect
Section 3(h)Methods of agriculture or horticultureComputational methods may still qualify as a technical solution
The clarification that decides most cases

The Indian Patent Office’s revised guidelines (2017 and subsequent amendments) acknowledge that while computer programs per se are excluded, inventions involving software that produce a technical effect or contribute to a technical advancement are patentable.

Likely patentable: a software-implemented encryption method that improves the security architecture.
Likely not: a business method implemented entirely in software for accounting purposes.

The drafting consequence: frame claims around a specific technical improvement, never around an abstract idea implemented on a generic computer.

5. Stage 1 — Prior-Art Search

5.1 Definition and Legal Significance

A prior-art search is the systematic retrieval and analysis of existing public disclosures relevant to a proposed invention. Its legal significance is twofold: examiners use prior art to assess novelty and inventive step, and applicants use it to evaluate the probability of grant before incurring expenditure.

5.2 Four Types of Prior Art

  • Patent literature — granted patents and published applications filed with national and regional offices.
  • Non-patent literature (NPL) — peer-reviewed journals, ACM and IEEE conference proceedings, theses, textbooks, technical reports, standards documents such as RFCs, and product manuals.
  • Public disclosures — conference talks, posters, blog posts, open-source code commits, video demonstrations and marketing material.
  • Public use or sale — in many jurisdictions, including the US under 35 U.S.C. §102(a)(1), prior public use or sale constitutes prior art regardless of whether it was documented in writing.
The modern CS sources that matter

Open-source repositories on GitHub, preprints on arXiv, and standardisation documents from the IETF, IEEE and 3GPP are now first-class sources of prior art. A commit is a publication.

5.4 Search Strategy

A defensible search combines three orthogonal strategies with iterative refinement.

  1. Keyword-based search. Boolean operators, proximity operators and truncation. For a novel caching scheme:
    ("cache replacement" OR "eviction policy")
    AND ("machine learning" OR "neural network")
    AND ("distributed" OR "edge computing")
  2. Classification-based search. Uses CPC or IPC codes rather than words, so it survives the vocabulary problem — a patent that names your idea differently still surfaces. Caching in distributed systems, for example, falls under G06F12/0866 (cache hierarchies) or H04L67/568 (caching at network nodes).
  3. Citation-based search. Forward and backward citation chasing: once a relevant patent is identified, inspect both the references it cites and the patents citing it, iteratively.
Why keyword-only searching fails

A keyword-only search is the commonest reason a student concludes an idea is novel when it is not. Search logs must also be retained: they form part of the due-diligence record required by the duty of candor before the USPTO and the disclosure obligations under Article 5 of the PCT.

5.5 Outcome — the Prior-Art Landscape Report

The search produces a report containing the closest prior art; categorisation by relevance (X, Y, A designations in EPO and Indian search opinions); identified gaps the invention fills; and identified barriers if the invention lies in a heavily patented space. This feeds directly into novelty assessment.

6. Stage 2 — Novelty Assessment

6.1 The Novelty Test

The test is claim-by-claim. For each claim the assessor asks:

Single-reference anticipation

Does a single prior-art reference disclose every element of the claim, either expressly or implicitly?

If so, anticipation is established and the claim lacks novelty — even if the document does not explicitly state that those elements function together as claimed.

6.2 Novelty versus Inventive Step

A common confusion arises between the two. The distinction is critical:

  • Novelty is binary. A claim is either anticipated or it is not.
  • Inventive step is a sliding scale, assessed against the person skilled in the art.

Formally, given prior art P and invention I:

I is novel   ⟺   ∄ p ∈ P : p ⊇ I(2)

Inventive step additionally requires that I would not be reached by combining elements of P using ordinary creativity. The two tests are complementary, and patentability requires both.

The practical difference

If one document contains everything, you have a novelty problem and the claim is dead as drafted. If you need to combine two or more documents, novelty survives and the analysis moves to inventive step, where you can argue the combination was not obvious.

This is why an examiner’s X citations (novelty-destroying alone) are far more serious than Y citations (relevant only in combination).

6.3 The Subject-Matter Trap

For software, novelty is necessary but not sufficient. In Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International, 573 U.S. 208 (2014), the US Supreme Court established a two-step test to determine whether a software claim is directed to a patent-ineligible abstract idea. Pure algorithms, mathematical formulas and abstract business methods, even if novel, are not patent-eligible subject matter under 35 U.S.C. §101.

6.4 Assessment Procedure

  1. Construct the broadest reasonable interpretation (BRI) of each claim.
  2. Identify the elements of each claim.
  3. Map each element to specific passages in the closest prior art.
  4. Document the element-by-element comparison.
  5. Flag any claim where a single reference anticipates all elements.
  6. Flag any claim where anticipation requires combining references — this moves the analysis to inventive step.
  7. Record findings in an invention review report.

If the assessment shows no anticipation and reasonable grounds for inventive step, the invention proceeds to disclosure and drafting. If anticipation is identified, the inventors revise the claims to introduce differentiating features — a process known as claim sculpting — before proceeding.

7. Stage 3 — Invention Disclosure

7.1 Definition and Purpose

An invention disclosure is a confidential written record prepared by the inventors describing the invention, its technical context, the problem solved and the contributors. It is the bridge between research output and legal filing. Its purposes are to capture inventive detail while fresh; to establish internal priority among co-inventors; to give the patent attorney sufficient technical disclosure to draft a defensible specification; to comply with institutional IP policies; and to serve as evidence in disputes over inventorship, ownership or priority.

7.2 Timing — the Thing That Actually Goes Wrong

Disclose internally before you disclose externally

In an academic setting the inventor must file the disclosure before any external public disclosure — including paper submission, conference presentation or thesis deposit — that might compromise novelty. Most universities have technology transfer offices that review disclosures confidentially and may recommend a publication embargo.

In industry the disclosure goes through an internal invention management system, often within 30 days of conception, as employer invention-assignment policies require.

7.3 Components of an Invention Disclosure Form

  1. Title — concise, descriptive, non-marketing.
  2. Inventors — full legal names, affiliations, contribution percentages. Note that inventorship is a legal determination; the IDF lists candidate inventors.
  3. Funding sources — grants, contracts, sponsors. These affect ownership and government rights.
  4. Problem solved — the technical problem addressed.
  5. Prior art known to the inventors.
  6. Detailed technical description — sufficient for a person skilled in the art to reproduce the invention: the enablement standard.
  7. Best mode — the inventors’ preferred embodiment at the time of filing.
  8. Drawings, flowcharts, block diagrams.
  9. Keywords and suggested CPC/IPC classifications, to guide prior-art searching.
  10. Commercial application — anticipated industries, products and licensees.
  11. Public disclosures to date, with dates of talks, papers, posters and code releases.
  12. Confidentiality statement, signed by all inventors.

7.4 Common Pitfalls

  • Late disclosure after public presentation, which destroys novelty in absolute-novelty jurisdictions.
  • Insufficient detail, preventing the attorney from drafting a defensible specification.
  • Improper inventorship listing — omitting a contributor who contributed to the conception of a claim element, or including a supervisor who did not.
  • Failure to identify funding sources, which may create government ownership rights.
  • Premature external disclosure during commercialisation discussions without a non-disclosure agreement.

8. Stage 4 — Drafting the Specification

8.1 Provisional versus Complete Specification

Provisional (Section 9)Complete (Section 10)
FunctionEstablishes the priority dateThe document on which examination and grant are based
ClaimsNot requiredRequired
ContentsTitle; description indicating nature, purpose and components; any drawings (Rule 15)Full description, abstract and claims (Rule 13)
TimingFiled first, when the invention is at a conceptual or experimental stageWithin 12 months of the provisional

A provisional is preferred when the invention is at a conceptual or experimental stage, further refinement is anticipated, market or publication pressure requires an early filing date, or investor discussions require an IP position to be established.

Two hard limits

The 12-month deadline is absolute. Missing it means the provisional is deemed abandoned and the priority date is lost (Section 9(1)).

Priority covers only what was disclosed. A complete specification may add matter but cannot claim priority for matter absent from the provisional. Adding such material is new matter, and it destroys priority for those claims.

The practical implication: a thin provisional filed in a hurry buys a date for very little. Disclose broadly even where you are not yet ready to claim.

8.2 Structure of a Complete Specification — Rule 13

  1. Title of the invention — concise, technically descriptive, free of trade names.
  2. Field of the invention — broad classification of the technical domain.
  3. Background and prior art — existing solutions and their limitations.
  4. Objects of the invention — explicit statements of what the invention seeks to achieve.
  5. Summary of the invention — a concise overview of the solution.
  6. Brief description of the drawings.
  7. Detailed description — full technical description including embodiments and best mode. Must satisfy the enablement requirement of Section 10(4).
  8. Claims — the legally operative section defining the scope of the monopoly.
  9. Abstract — a summary of up to 150 words used for prior-art retrieval.

Parallel provisions: 37 CFR §1.77 in the United States and Rule 42 EPC in Europe. The structure is effectively international.

8.3 Drafting the Claims

Claims are the most critical part of the specification: they define, with legal precision, the scope of the monopoly.

  • Independent claims stand alone and define the broadest inventive concept.
  • Dependent claims reference one or more preceding claims, add further limitations, and provide fallback positions if the independent claim is invalidated.

Four claim types in CS patents: method claims (a sequence of steps, e.g. a method for training a neural network); system claims (an apparatus comprising components); computer-readable medium claims (a non-transitory medium storing instructions that, when executed, perform a process); and use claims (a new use of a known device — rare in CS).

The structural template

A [method / system / computer-readable medium] for [purpose], comprising: (a) [first element]; (b) [second element]; and (c) [third element].

Two drafting principles govern claim language:

  • Definiteness (35 U.S.C. §112 / Indian Section 4(4)(iii)) — claims must particularly point out and distinctly claim the subject matter. Functional language without structural support is rejected as indefinite.
  • Support (Section 10(4)) — claims must be supported by the description. Claim features absent from the description are not allowable.

8.4 Best Practices

  • Use consistent terminology throughout the description and claims.
  • Avoid relative terms such as “large” or “fast” without quantitative anchors.
  • Define key terms explicitly in a definition section.
  • Disclose alternative embodiments even if not currently claimed — they provide fallback positions and support for dependent claims.
  • Provide working examples sufficient to satisfy enablement.
  • Coordinate claim scope with commercial value: too broad invites rejection, too narrow is easily designed around.
  • Maintain direct correspondence between claim features and description paragraphs.

8.5 Common Drafting Errors

  • Anticipation by the description — the specification itself discloses the invention in a manner that anticipates the claims.
  • New matter — introducing into the complete specification matter absent from the provisional, destroying priority.
  • Improper dependent claim formatting — a dependent claim that does not further limit its parent.
  • Lack of unity — a single application claiming disparate inventions, triggering an objection under PCT Rule 13.

9. Filing Modes and Pre-Filing Discipline

9.1 Documentation and Record-Keeping

Inventors should maintain a contemporaneous lab notebook or digital research journal recording: the date and inventor of each conception; a detailed description of how the invention works; alternative embodiments explored; and witness signatures, preferably from non-inventors. This serves both as evidence of invention date and as the substrate from which the specification is drafted.

10. Examination Procedure

10.1 Request for Examination — Section 11B

Examination in India is on request

Unlike jurisdictions where examination is automatic, India follows an examination-on-request system. The applicant, or any interested person, must file a Request for Examination (Form 18) within 48 months of the priority date or filing date, whichever is earlier.

Failure to file within this period results in the application being treated as withdrawn. Students routinely assume examination is automatic; it is not, and there is no remedy.

10.2 The First Examination Report

The application is assigned to an examiner who conducts substantive examination and issues a First Examination Report (FER) citing: prior art references, classified X, Y, A; deficiencies in form or substance; objections regarding novelty, inventive step or industrial application; and clarity, succinctness or support objections under Section 10.

The applicant must respond within six months of issuance, extendable by a further three months on a request in Form 4.

10.3 Prosecution and Hearing

  1. The applicant files a response addressing each objection with arguments and/or amendments.
  2. The examiner reviews and may issue further objections.
  3. If objections persist, a hearing may be requested under Section 14, where the applicant argues the case orally before the Controller.
  4. The Controller either allows the claims (with or without amendments) or refuses the application.

A refused application was formerly appealed to the Intellectual Property Appellate Board, succeeded by the Commercial Courts and Intellectual Property Divisions of High Courts under the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021.

11. Publication of the Application

Under Section 11A, a patent application is published in the Patent Journal 18 months after the priority or filing date. Earlier publication may be requested by filing Form 9.

11.1 Four Effects of Publication

  1. Constructive notice. The public is deemed to have notice of the application.
  2. Provisional rights. Under Section 11A(7), the applicant acquires the right to claim damages for infringement occurring after publication, provided the patent is ultimately granted, the act would have infringed the granted patent, and notice was given to the infringer before suit.
  3. Examination trigger. Publication often prompts examination requests by third parties with commercial interests.
  4. Pre-grant opposition period. Publication opens the window for third parties to file pre-grant oppositions under Section 25(1).

Prior to publication, applications are kept confidential. Inventors should be cautious about disclosing to unauthorised parties, publishing academic papers that may constitute prior art against themselves, and discussing the invention at public forums without a non-disclosure agreement.

12. Opposition Proceedings

12.1 Pre-Grant Opposition — Section 25(1)

Any person may file a pre-grant opposition at any time after publication and before grant, by notice on Form 7A supported by evidence and arguments.

12.2 Post-Grant Opposition — Section 25(2)

Following grant, any interested person may file a post-grant opposition within 12 months of the date of grant notification, or the publication of the grant in the journal, whichever is later. Grounds largely mirror pre-grant opposition and additionally include non-disclosure under Section 8, fraud on the patent office, and claiming a wrong priority date.

12.3 Strategic Implications

Opposition is used by competitors as a defensive strategy: to block or limit patent monopolies in critical technology areas; to delay or invalidate patents threatening market position; and to test the validity of weak or overly broad patents. For the applicant, the possibility of opposition is the argument for careful drafting, thorough prior-art consideration and robust responses.

13. Grant of Patent

Upon successful prosecution and resolution of all objections, the Controller grants the patent under Section 43. The grant is recorded in the Register of Patents and a patent number is issued.

The patent certificate contains: the patent number and date of grant; the priority date and filing date; the names of patentee and inventor; the full specification as granted; and the term.

13.1 Term and Renewal

Under Section 53, the term is 20 years from the date of filing — whether provisional or complete — subject to payment of renewal fees. Renewal fees under Rule 80 are payable annually from the third year onwards. Failure results in lapse, though restoration is possible within 18 months of lapse with a prescribed surcharge.

13.2 Rights Conferred — Section 48

A granted patent confers the exclusive right to prevent third parties from making, using, offering for sale, selling or importing the patented product or process; to assign or license the patent; and to seek reliefs for infringement including damages, injunctions and accounts of profits.

Three different clocks

The term runs from the filing date; renewal fees begin in the third year; and examination must be requested within 48 months of priority. Three clocks, three different start points. Students routinely merge them.

14. Commercialisation and Development

A patent is not merely a legal right; it is a strategic commercial asset, and its value is realised only when actively deployed.

14.1 Four Commercialisation Pathways

  • Direct commercialisation. The patentee manufactures, markets and sells. Most common where the patentee has delivery capability and the invention is core to the business model.
  • Licensing. The patentee retains ownership while granting defined rights. Structures include exclusive (sole licensee, excluding even the patentee), sole (one licensee, but the patentee may also practise), non-exclusive (multiple licensees) and cross-licensing (mutual, common in standards-essential patents).
  • Assignment. A complete transfer of ownership, typically for a one-time payment, structured via an Assignment Deed under Section 69. Assignments must be registered with the Controller to be effective against third parties.
  • Spin-off companies. Increasingly supported by academic institutions: preserves institutional ownership while enabling exploitation through a dedicated venture.

Licensing terms typically include: upfront fees, running royalties as a percentage of revenue, minimum royalties, milestone payments, field-of-use restrictions and geographic limitations.

14.2 Technology Transfer

Technology transfer is the broader process by which patented inventions move from research environments to commercial deployment. Mechanisms include university–industry collaborative research agreements, government-funded research translation programmes, patent pools and open innovation platforms, and standards-setting organisation patent commitments.

14.3 Valuation

  • Cost method — the sum of R&D and patent procurement costs.
  • Market method — comparable transaction analysis.
  • Income method — discounted cash flow analysis of expected future income.
  • Real options method — for patents with uncertain but potentially high-value applications.

14.4 What Is Different About Software Patents

  1. Rapid technological evolution. Software patents may become obsolete well before the term expires.
  2. Defensive aggregation. Companies build large portfolios purely to deter litigation — for example the LOT Network for open-source-friendly companies.
  3. Open-source compatibility. Many software patents are licensed on FRAND (fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory) or open-source-friendly terms to facilitate ecosystem participation.
  4. Standards integration. Patents incorporated into technical standards such as H.265 or 5G NR carry significant licensing obligations.

15. Research, Innovation and Portfolio Strategy

15.1 Patent Landscape Analysis

Before committing resources to a research direction, sophisticated R&D organisations conduct a patent landscape analysis — a structured study of existing filings in the relevant technology area. Landscape analyses reveal white spaces (underexplored areas), crowded spaces (over-patented, with high freedom-to-operate risk), key assignees, citation clusters and filing trends. This allows research to be directed toward patentable and strategically valuable problems.

15.2 Freedom to Operate

Distinct from patentability, freedom to operate is the ability to commercialise a product without infringing existing third-party patents. An FTO analysis identifies blocking patents whose claims read on the proposed product or process.

CS researchers developing algorithms, libraries or protocols must conduct FTO analyses — often more stringently than patentability searches — because jurisdictions that restrict patent-eligibility of pure algorithms still allow patents on specific applications of algorithms.

15.3 The Decision Matrix

Quality over quantity

In mature IP management frameworks — such as those aligned with ISO 56005 — patent quality is preferred to patent quantity. A small portfolio of well-drafted, well-prosecuted and well-maintained patents typically outperforms a large portfolio of marginal filings.

15.4 Research–Patent Synergy in Academia

The Bayh-Dole Act in the United States, and analogous Indian provisions including the Public Funded Research Result Related IP Protection and Management Rules, 2023, allow universities to retain title to inventions arising from publicly funded research, subject to compliance with disclosure, election of title and march-in rights. This has institutionalised the research–patent synergy and made patent awareness a core research methodology competency.

16. Case Studies

16.1 Prior-Art Search in Action — Touchscreen Patents

In the late 1990s multiple inventors independently developed capacitive touchscreen technology. The prior-art landscape at the time included FingerWorks (acquired by Apple in 2005), IBM patents on capacitive sensing, and academic publications in IEEE Transactions on Consumer Electronics. A rigorous search would have revealed the crowded landscape and shaped both claim scope and filing strategy. The subsequent litigation history — including the Apple v. Samsung disputes — illustrates the cost of inadequate landscape awareness.

16.2 Novelty and Software Patents — Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank

Alice Corporation held patents on a computerised method for mitigating settlement risk in financial transactions. The Supreme Court held that claims directed to the abstract idea of intermediated settlement, even when implemented on a generic computer, were not patent-eligible under §101.

The case reshaped CS patent strategy, requiring drafters to frame claims around specific technical improvements — for example, “a method of training a neural network wherein the gradient update is computed using a novel pruning threshold” — rather than abstract ideas implemented on a computer.

17. Summary

  • Patentability is novelty + inventive step + industrial application, cumulatively. Failing any one is fatal.
  • Section 3(k) excludes computer programs per se; the escape is a demonstrable technical effect or technical advancement.
  • A defensible prior-art search combines keyword, classification and citation strategies across patent and non-patent literature.
  • Novelty is binary and claim-by-claim. Single-reference anticipation kills a claim; combination moves the analysis to inventive step.
  • Disclose internally before you publish externally. Your own preprint is prior art against you, and there is no way to undo it.
  • Claims are the monopoly. Everything else in the specification exists to support them.
  • India examines on request within 48 months; publication occurs at 18 months and opens pre-grant opposition.
  • A patent has value only when deployed — through direct commercialisation, licensing, assignment or a spin-off.

18. Exam Preparation

Preparation strategies
  1. Create comparative tables distinguishing provisional versus complete specifications, pre-grant versus post-grant opposition, and the different licensing arrangements.
  2. Trace the procedural timeline on paper to internalise the chronological sequence of filing, publication, examination and grant.
  3. Practise application-based questions involving CS inventions to test your ability to apply patentability criteria.
  4. Refer to primary legislation — the Indian Patents Act, 1970 with 2005 amendments — for citation accuracy.
  5. Know the frequently tested topics: definitions of prior art, novelty, inventive step, specification and claims; the novelty/inventive step distinction; components of a complete specification; types of claims; the role of the PSA; patentability search versus FTO; and the Alice/Mayo framework.
  6. Stay aware of recent case law, particularly from the Delhi and Madras High Court IP Divisions.

18.4 Case-Based Practice

Scenario

Dr. R. and two PhD students, A. and B., developed a novel routing protocol for low-earth-orbit satellite constellations. Before filing a patent, A. presented preliminary results at an international conference, with the slides uploaded to the conference website. Six months later the team filed a complete specification claiming the protocol. A year later the examiner cited a 2019 paper by an unrelated group (X. et al.) describing a similar routing principle but not disclosing the specific congestion-control mechanism of Dr. R.’s invention.

(a) Identify any novelty issues arising from A.’s conference presentation. In which jurisdictions is this fatal to patentability?
(b) Can X. et al.’s 2019 paper anticipate Dr. R.’s claim? Apply the single-reference anticipation test.
(c) Recommend corrective actions the team could have taken, and assess whether retroactive correction is possible.

18.5 Application-Based Questions

  1. A group of students develops a novel machine learning algorithm that significantly reduces training time for deep neural networks on edge devices. They wish to patent in India and file internationally. Outline a step-by-step patenting strategy, including documentation requirements, specification drafting considerations and commercialisation options. 15 marks
  2. After filing for a distributed consensus protocol, a competitor files a pre-grant opposition citing prior art from an arXiv preprint by a different group. Analyse the likely grounds, the procedural implications, and the strategies available to defend the application. 10 marks
  3. A university spin-off wishes to commercialise a patented cybersecurity solution. Compare licensing, assignment and spin-off commercialisation models, recommending the most suitable path with justification. 10 marks
