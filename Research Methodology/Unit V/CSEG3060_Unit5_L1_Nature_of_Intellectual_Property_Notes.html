This lecture is the conceptual foundation of Unit V. Lecture 2 examines how a patent is actually obtained; Lecture 3 examines how that national right is projected across borders.

How to use these notes

These notes consolidate two syllabus topics — the nature and process of intellectual property and the overview of patents, designs and copyrights — because the second is simply the first made concrete for the three regimes that matter most to a computer scientist.

One sentence carries the whole lecture: an intellectual property right is a negative right. It lets you stop others; it does not, by itself, let you practise your own invention. Sections 3 and 9.4 develop the consequence.

Answer pattern for the examination: definition → statutory section → computer-science example. Sections 8, 9 and 13 are written as tables for exactly this reason, and Section 13 is the single most predictable long-answer item in the unit.

2. Defining Intellectual Property and IPR

Computer science is fundamentally a discipline of knowledge creation. From the design of novel algorithms and data structures to the engineering of compilers, operating systems, machine learning architectures and distributed protocols, every original contribution constitutes intellectual output. The legal frameworks protecting such outputs fall collectively under Intellectual Property Rights.

In contemporary research the question is no longer whether to protect an innovation, but how, where, and to what extent.

2.1 Intellectual Property

Definition — WIPO

Intellectual property is “creations of the mind: inventions, literary and artistic works, designs and symbols, names and images used in commerce.”

A more legally precise formulation appears in Article 1(2) of the TRIPS Agreement (1994), which obliges WTO members to protect the following categories:

  • Copyright and related rights
  • Trademarks
  • Geographical indications
  • Industrial designs
  • Patents
  • Layout designs of integrated circuits
  • Undisclosed information, including trade secrets
  • Plant varieties (optional)

Quote both in an examination: WIPO supplies the intuition, TRIPS supplies the enumeration.

2.2 Intellectual Property Rights

IPR refers to the legal rights that arise from the creation and use of intellectual property. These rights grant the creator an exclusive, time-limited monopoly over exploitation of the creation, subject to the public policy objectives of the granting jurisdiction.

3. Five Characteristics of an IP Right

CharacteristicDescriptionPractical consequence
Negative rightIPR confers the right to exclude others from exploiting the creationIt does not automatically confer the right to use the creation yourself
TerritorialityRights are bounded by the jurisdiction of the granting authorityA patent in India confers no rights in the United States
Temporal limitationAll IP rights are time-boundCopyright: life + 60 years in India. Patents: 20 years from filing
Assignable and licensableTransferable by assignment, licence or successionThis is what makes IP the currency of technology transfer
Statutory originIPR exists only because a statute recognises itThere is no natural-law basis for IP in modern jurisprudence
The misconception that costs marks

A patent is a sword, not a shield. It permits you to sue, not to practise. Where a complementary patent is held by another party, the patentee may be blocked and may have to seek a licence — or a compulsory licence under Section 84 of the Patents Act.

4. Scope of Intellectual Property Rights

4.1 Subject-Matter Scope

  • Technical creations — inventions, industrial processes, product designs, software-implemented innovations, biotechnological inventions.
  • Cultural and informational creations — literary works, musical compositions, films, broadcasts, computer programs, databases.
  • Commercial identifiers — trademarks, brand names, logos, domain names, trade dress.
  • Geographical and traditional markers — geographical indications, appellations of origin, traditional knowledge.
  • Confidential knowledge — trade secrets, know-how, customer lists, formulae.

4.2 Spatial (Territorial) Scope

IPR is fundamentally territorial. Each jurisdiction has its own regime, and rights must be secured in every country where protection is desired. International conventions — the Paris Convention, the Berne Convention, the Patent Cooperation Treaty — provide procedural mechanisms for streamlined multi-jurisdictional filing, but they do not create a global IP right.

4.3 Temporal Scope

Each category carries its own term, balanced to reconcile the private interest of the creator with the public interest in dissemination. Once the term expires, the creation enters the public domain and may be freely used by all.

5. Importance of Intellectual Property Rights

  1. Incentive to innovation. A time-limited monopoly allows innovators to recover R&D costs and earn a return. Without it, the free-rider problem discourages socially beneficial investment in risky research.
  2. Encouragement of disclosure. The patent system embodies a quid pro quo: the inventor discloses in sufficient detail to enable a person skilled in the art to practise the invention, in exchange for a limited monopoly. This enriches the public domain and prevents indefinite secrecy.
  3. Facilitating technology transfer. Licensing, joint ventures, university–industry collaborations and cross-border R&D partnerships all depend on a regime that defines the rights being exchanged.
  4. Attracting investment. A robust IP regime reduces investment risk; venture capital and corporate R&D funding flow preferentially to jurisdictions where innovations can be protected and infringement meaningfully remedied.
  5. Competitive and strategic value. Portfolios are used offensively to assert exclusivity and defensively to deter litigation and establish bargaining positions. The strategic use of standard-essential patents in 3G, 4G, 5G and Wi-Fi is the clearest illustration.
  6. Ethical and professional responsibility. Researchers must attribute prior work, respect others’ rights, and disclose inventions made in the course of employment. IPR literacy is part of the responsible conduct of research.

5.1 Why IP Is Acute in Computer Science

  • Software is both functional and expressive, blurring the line between patentable subject matter and copyrightable expression.
  • Open-source licensing has created an ecosystem in which rights are granted conditionally and must be carefully managed.
  • Algorithms and business methods are subject to jurisdictional divergence — patentable in the United States under certain conditions, generally not in India and Europe.
  • Data and databases raise questions at the intersection of copyright, sui generis database rights and confidentiality.
  • AI-generated works challenge the foundational requirement of human authorship.

6. Evolution of Intellectual Property Rights

6.1 Early Origins

The earliest recorded grant of a patent-like privilege appears in Venice in 1474, in a statute protecting new and useful inventions for ten years. It induced Venetian glassmakers and other artisans to disclose their techniques rather than maintain them as guild secrets — the same bargain the modern patent system makes.

In England, the Statute of Monopolies of 1624 ended the Crown’s practice of granting arbitrary monopolies but preserved an exception for inventions, laying the doctrinal foundation for the modern patent system. The Statute of Anne of 1710 is generally regarded as the foundation of modern copyright.

6.2 The Nineteenth Century — International Coordination

  • Paris Convention (1883) — established national treatment and the right of priority for patents, trademarks and industrial designs.
  • Berne Convention (1886) — established automatic copyright protection without formalities.
  • Madrid Agreement (1891) — facilitated international registration of trademarks.

6.3 The Twentieth Century — Institutionalisation

  • Patent Cooperation Treaty (1970) — administered by WIPO; a unified procedure for filing in multiple jurisdictions through a single international application.
  • Budapest Treaty (1977) — international recognition of the deposit of microorganisms.
  • TRIPS Agreement (1994) — administered under the WTO; the most comprehensive multilateral IP agreement in force, setting minimum standards across all categories and binding all WTO member states.
  • WCT and WPPT (1996) — extended copyright protection to the digital environment.

7. Evolution of IP in India

India’s IP history is marked by a tension between indigenous knowledge systems and the colonial legacy of British IP law.

Pre-independenceThe Indian Patents and Designs Act, 1911 governed the colonial period, allowing product patents in all fields including pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals

Why this matters beyond history

The 1970 restriction and the 2005 reversal are the background to Novartis v. Union of India (Section 17.2) and to every argument about TRIPS flexibilities that recurs in Lecture 3.

Trade secrets in computing

Notable examples include the Coca-Cola formula, the precise weighting factors of the Google search algorithm (never publicly disclosed), and the proprietary training data of large language models. A trade secret costs nothing and lasts indefinitely — but evaporates the moment someone independently discovers or reverse-engineers it.

Geographical indications are less directly relevant to computing, though they matter where software or data products are associated with regional craftsmanship. Layout designs are directly relevant to chip design; plant varieties increasingly touch computational biology and agricultural informatics.

9. Patents

9.1 Definition and the Three Conditions

Statutory definition

Section 2(1)(j) defines an “invention” as “a new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application.” Section 2(1)(l) defines a “patent” as the patent granted under the Act.

Patentability requires three cumulative conditions — failing any one is fatal:

  1. Novelty (new) — not anticipated by prior art anywhere in the world before the filing or priority date.
  2. Inventive step (non-obvious) — involves a feature that would not be obvious to a person skilled in the art.
  3. Industrial application (useful) — capable of being made or used in an industry.

9.2 Subject Matter and Scope

Patentable in a computer science context:

  • A novel algorithm embodied in a concrete technical application — for example an image compression method producing measurable technical effects.
  • A hardware architecture or chip design.
  • A computer-implemented invention that produces a technical effect or solves a technical problem.
  • A method of manufacturing or industrial process.

Not patentable in India (Section 3):

  • An invention that is frivolous or contrary to public order or morality.
  • Discovery of a scientific principle, theory or mathematical method in the abstract.
  • Schemes, rules or methods of performing mental acts or playing games.
  • Presentation of information.
  • Section 3(k) — a mathematical or business method, or a computer program per se, or algorithms.
  • Topographies of integrated circuits (governed separately).
  • Plants and animals in whole or part, with exceptions for microbiological processes.
The most actively litigated boundary in the field

The line between a patent-eligible computer-implemented invention and a non-patentable abstract algorithm is contested in every major jurisdiction. Section 3(k) and the technical effect test are examined in detail in Lecture 2; the comparative position across the US, EPO, Japan, China and India is in Lecture 3.

9.3 Duration

Tpatent = 20 years from the date of filing the application(1)

Under Section 53, aligned with TRIPS Article 33, which mandates a minimum of 20 years from the filing date. There is no provision for extension of the term in India. Renewal fees are payable annually from the third year; failure causes lapse, with restoration possible within 18 months on a surcharge.

9.4 Rights Conferred — Section 48

  1. Right to prevent third parties from making the patented product.
  2. Right to prevent third parties from using the patented process.
  3. Right to prevent third parties from offering for sale, selling or importing the patented product, or a product obtained directly by the patented process.
  4. Right to assign or licence the patent (voluntary licensing).
  5. Right to seek relief for infringement — injunctions, damages, accounts of profits.
The caveat

The patentee does not have a positive right to practise the invention; the right is purely negative. If a complementary patent is held by another party, the patentee may face a blocking situation and may seek a compulsory licence under Section 84.

10. Industrial Designs

Definition — Designs Act, 2000, Section 2(d)

“Design means only the features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to any article whether in two dimensional or three dimensional or in both forms, by any industrial process or means.”

An industrial design concerns how an article looks, not how it works.

10.1 Conditions and Exclusions

Three cumulative conditions govern registrability: novelty (new or original), distinctiveness (significantly distinguishable from known designs), and non-disclosure (not published or used in India or elsewhere before filing).

Not registrable (Section 4): designs that are not new or original; designs already disclosed to the public; designs contrary to public order or morality; a mere mechanical contrivance or principle; a mere workshop improvement or mode of construction; trade or property marks; and a design dictated solely by function — the “must-fit” exclusion.

10.2 Duration — Section 11

Tdesign = 10 years (initial) + 5 years (extension) = 15 years maximum(2)

The initial period runs ten years from the date of registration. A five-year extension may be sought before the expiry of the initial period.

10.3 Rights Conferred — Section 22

The registered proprietor has the exclusive right to apply the design to any article in the registered class, and may: sue for infringement under Section 22 (which incorporates the Copyright Act’s provisions on offences, penalties and remedies); assign or license the design; recover damages for unauthorised application; and seek injunctions.

10.4 Relevance to Computer Science

  • The physical form of computing devices — laptops, smartphones, wearables.
  • The overall visual design of consumer electronics.
  • Graphical user interfaces — registrable in India under the Designs Act since 2008, though with restrictions on purely functional or animated GUIs.
Design versus patent — the examinable one-liner

A design protects appearance; a patent protects function. A novel laptop hinge mechanism may be patented, while the visual appearance of the laptop’s casing is registrable as a design. Both can coexist on the same product.

11. Copyright

11.1 Definition and Subject Matter

Copyright is a bundle of legal rights arising automatically upon creation of an original work. Unlike patents and designs, copyright does not require formal registration for subsistence, although registration under Section 44 provides procedural advantages and is prima facie evidence.

Under Section 13, copyright subsists in seven classes of work:

  1. Original literary works — books, computer programs, software code, tables, compilations, databases where original.
  2. Dramatic works — scripts, choreographic works.
  3. Musical works — compositions with or without words.
  4. Artistic works — paintings, sculptures, drawings, engravings, photographs.
  5. Cinematograph films.
  6. Sound recordings.
  7. Architectural works.

11.2 Copyright in Computer Programs

Computer programs are explicitly treated as literary works under Section 2(o). Protection extends to the expression — source code, object code, and certain structural and organisational elements — but not to the ideas, algorithms, methods of operation or underlying logic.

In practice copyright subsists in: source code in any programming language; object code; documentation and user manuals; website content; software architecture diagrams as artistic works; database contents where there is originality of selection or arrangement; and multimedia content.

The idea–expression dichotomy

Copyright protects the expression of ideas, not the ideas themselves — the central tenet codified by Section 13 and reinforced by the Berne Convention.

The algorithm for sorting arrays is not copyrightable. A specific implementation of it in C++ is.

11.3 Duration — Sections 22 to 28

Class of workTerm
Literary, dramatic, musical, artistic worksLife of the author + 60 years
Cinematograph films, sound recordings, photographs, government works60 years from the year of publication
Anonymous and pseudonymous works; works of joint authorship60 years from the year of publication

The term is calculated from the calendar year following the author’s death, or from the year of first publication for corporate and statutory works.

11.4 Rights Conferred

A. Economic rights (Section 14). The exclusive right to: reproduce the work; issue copies to the public; perform it in public; communicate it to the public; make translations, adaptations or arrangements; and make cinematograph films or sound recordings of it. These are assignable and licensable — they are the commercial asset.

B. Moral rights (Section 57). Retained by the author even after assignment of the economic rights:

  1. Right of paternity — to claim authorship of the work.
  2. Right of integrity — to restrain distortion, mutilation or modification prejudicial to the author’s honour or reputation.
  3. Right of retraction — in limited circumstances, to withdraw the work from circulation, subject to compensating the assignee.
Frequently omitted in answers

Moral rights are perpetual, inalienable and non-assignable. That combination is precisely what makes them examinable, and students routinely mention only economic rights.

15. The Overall Process of IP Protection

Each category has its own procedural regime, but the protection process shares a common architecture. For patents the process is most elaborate and serves as the paradigm.

Identification → Prior art / prior right search → Determining the category → Drafting and filing → Formal and substantive examination → Grant / registration → Post-grant maintenance and renewal → Enforcement

15.1 Stage-wise Description

  1. Identification of the IP. Determine whether the subject matter qualifies as an IP asset. Mere ideas, abstract principles, natural phenomena and discoveries are not protectable.
  2. Prior art / prior right search. Prior art for patents includes any disclosure anywhere in the world before the filing date. For trademarks, prior rights include earlier identical or similar marks; for copyright, prior publication establishes an earlier work.
  3. Determining the category. A single innovation may be protected under multiple categories simultaneously — see Section 18.
  4. Filing the application. In India, with the Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (CGPDTM) under DPIIT. International filings proceed under the PCT for patents, the Madrid System for trademarks and the Hague System for designs.
  5. Examination. Formal examination checks procedural compliance; substantive examination checks novelty, inventive step and industrial applicability. In India, patent applications are examined only upon request, which must be made within 48 months of the priority date.
  6. Grant. The IP is granted or registered and the owner acquires enforceable rights.
  7. Post-grant maintenance. Most rights require periodic renewal — annually for patents, every ten years for trademarks in India. Failure to renew results in lapse.
  8. Enforcement. Civil litigation (injunction, damages, accounts of profits), criminal prosecution where applicable, and administrative remedies such as customs seizure of infringing imports.

15.2 Illustrative Case: The Patent Process in India

A researcher develops a novel method for detecting network intrusions using a machine learning architecture optimised for edge devices. The process proceeds as follows:

  1. The invention is documented in a patent specification, including claims delineating the scope of protection sought.
  2. A prior art search is conducted across patent databases (Indian Patent Office, USPTO, EPO, WIPO) and non-patent literature (IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, arXiv).
  3. An application is filed either directly in India (provisional or complete) or under the PCT designating India.
  4. The applicant requests examination within 48 months of the priority date.
  5. The Controller examines for novelty, inventive step, industrial applicability and sufficiency of disclosure, including compliance with Section 3(k).
  6. The application is published; third parties may file pre-grant oppositions under Section 25(1).
  7. If granted, the patent is published in the Patent Office Journal.
  8. The patent is renewed annually after the seventh year.
  9. In case of infringement, the patentee may sue in a District Court or the relevant High Court.

16. Case Studies

16.1 Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International, 573 U.S. 208 (2014) — United States

The U.S. Supreme Court held that abstract ideas implemented on a generic computer are not patent-eligible. The decision narrowed the scope of software patents in the United States and is constantly cited in comparative discussion. It contrasts with the Indian approach under Section 3(k), which excludes computer programs per se and algorithms but does not adopt the “abstract idea” test.

16.2 Novartis AG v. Union of India, (2013) 6 SCC 1 — India

The Indian Supreme Court denied a patent for the beta crystalline form of imatinib mesylate (Glivec). The decision turned on Section 3(d), which denies patents for new forms of known substances unless they demonstrate significantly enhanced efficacy. The case is significant for balancing innovation incentives against public health, and for demonstrating how developing countries use TRIPS flexibilities.

16.3 Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., 593 U.S. (2021) — United States

The Court held that Google’s use of Oracle’s Java API declarations in Android constituted fair use. The case illustrates the difficult boundary between copyright protection of literal code and the freedom to interoperate using functional interfaces.

16.4 India’s Open Source Software Ecosystem

India’s policy of encouraging open-source software in government and public systems reflects a strategic choice to use IP regimes flexibly — managing rights collectively through licensing frameworks (GPL, MIT, Apache) rather than exclusive proprietary rights.

16.5 The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library

The TKDL, a collaboration between CSIR, the Ministry of Science and Technology and the Ministry of AYUSH, documents traditional Indian medical knowledge digitally to prevent its wrongful patenting abroad. It is a striking example of using IP tools — defensive disclosure and prior art documentation — to protect public goods rather than to create exclusivity.

17. Worked Case: A Layered IP Strategy

Scenario

A team of B.Tech (CS) students develops a novel image compression algorithm (“Algorithm X”) and produces a full-stack mobile application implementing it. The team wishes to protect their intellectual contribution.

  1. The algorithm as a method. If it produces a technical effect — reduced bandwidth utilisation at no measurable loss in perceptual quality — it may qualify as a patentable computer-implemented invention under Sections 2(1)(j) and 3. File before any public disclosure.
  2. The source code. Automatically protected by copyright under Section 13(1)(a). Registration with the Copyright Office adds prima facie evidentiary value.
  3. The app icon and UI layout. The icon may qualify as an artistic work; the visual appearance of the application’s screens may be registrable as an industrial design under the Designs Act post-2008.
  4. The brand name. A separate application under the Trade Marks Act, 1999.
  5. Multi-country protection. A single PCT application designating the chosen jurisdictions; the 30/31-month national phase deadline must be tracked.
The ordering constraint

Patent first, publish second. A conference paper, an arXiv preprint or a public GitHub commit is prior art against your own application in an absolute-novelty jurisdiction. There is no way to undo it.

Strategic recommendation: a layered IP strategy — combining copyright, patent, design and trademark protection — is usually the most robust approach for software innovations, because each layer fails in a different way and covers a different attack.

18. Summary

  • Intellectual property refers to creations of the mind; intellectual property rights are the legal entitlements that vest in creators.
  • IPR is negative, territorial, time-limited, assignable and statutory in origin. There is no natural-law basis for IP in modern jurisprudence.
  • The evolution from the Venetian statute of 1474 to TRIPS in 1994 reflects an ongoing negotiation between private reward and public benefit.
  • The eight categories of IP are patents, copyright, trademarks, industrial designs, geographical indications, trade secrets, layout designs and plant varieties.
  • Patent protects function; design protects appearance; copyright protects expression — and all three can coexist on one product.
  • The protection process follows a common eight-stage architecture: identification, search, categorisation, filing, examination, grant, maintenance and enforcement.
  • For computer scientists, IPR literacy is essential for protecting innovations, avoiding infringement, navigating open-source ecosystems and understanding standards-essential patents.

19. Exam Preparation

Preparation points
  1. Memorise definitions with precision. Examiners frequently test the formal definition of IP, IPR and each category.
  2. Master the TRIPS framework as the international benchmark, and relate each Indian statute to the TRIPS obligation behind it.
  3. Memorise the statutory durations — patent 20 years from filing; design 10 + 5 = 15; copyright life + 60; films and sound recordings 60 from publication. These are frequently tested numerical facts.
  4. Learn the statutory sections. Short-answer questions cite them directly — Sections 2(1)(j), 3(k), 48, 53 of the Patents Act; 2(d), 4, 11, 22 of the Designs Act; 13, 14, 22, 44, 57 of the Copyright Act.
  5. Draw the comparison tables for the eight categories and for patent–design–copyright. Practise producing them from memory.
  6. Map each treaty to the corresponding Indian statute — Berne to the Copyright Act, Paris to the Patents Act, Hague to the Designs Act.
  7. Connect theory to two or three landmark cases — Novartis, Alice Corp., Google v. Oracle.
  8. Practise the flow diagram of the patent process; examiners often test procedural knowledge.
  9. Use precise terminology. “Negative right”, “national treatment”, “priority date”, “PCT national phase” and “idea–expression dichotomy” all carry specific meanings.
  10. Avoid the common errors — do not confuse copyright with patent, do not assume copyright protects ideas, and do not conflate territoriality with universality.
