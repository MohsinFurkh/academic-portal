Definitions

Creations of the mind — and the rights that vest in them

WIPO: intellectual property is “creations of the mind: inventions, literary and artistic works, designs and symbols, names and images used in commerce.”

TRIPS Article 1(2) — the operative list

WTO members must protect: copyright and related rights · trademarks · geographical indications · industrial designs · patents · layout designs of integrated circuits · undisclosed information (trade secrets) · plant varieties (optional).

WIPO gives the intuition; TRIPS gives the legally precise enumeration. Quote both.

IP versus IPR

Intellectual property is the creation itself.
Intellectual property rights are the legal entitlements that arise from creating and using it — an exclusive, time-limited monopoly over exploitation, granted subject to the public policy objectives of the jurisdiction.

The Indian statutory framework

Patents Act, 1970 (amended 1999, 2002, 2005) · Copyright Act, 1957 · Trade Marks Act, 1999 · Designs Act, 2000 · Geographical Indications Act, 1999 · Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Design Act, 2000 · Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers’ Rights Act, 2001 · Information Technology Act, 2000

The anatomy of an IP right

Five characteristics — each with a practical consequence

CharacteristicWhat it meansWhy it bites
Negative rightThe right to exclude others from exploiting the creationIt does not confer a right to use your own invention — a blocking patent can still stop you
TerritorialityBounded by the jurisdiction of the granting authorityAn Indian patent confers no rights in the United States. There is no world patent
Temporal limitationAll IP rights are time-boundPatents 20 years from filing; copyright life + 60 years in India. Then the public domain
Assignable and licensableTransferable by assignment, licence or successionThis is what makes IP the currency of technology transfer
Statutory originIPR exists only because a statute recognises itThere is no natural-law basis for IP in modern jurisprudence — it is a policy instrument
The first row is the one students misremember. A patent is a sword, not a shield: it lets you sue, not practise. If someone else holds a complementary patent, you may need a licence — or a compulsory licence under Section 84.

Three dimensions

Scope: subject matter, territory, time

Subject-matter scope

Technical — inventions, processes, product designs, software-implemented innovations
Cultural / informational — literary works, films, computer programs, databases
Commercial identifiers — trademarks, brand names, logos, domain names, trade dress
Geographical — GIs, appellations of origin, traditional knowledge
Confidential — trade secrets, know-how, formulae

Spatial (territorial) scope

IPR is fundamentally territorial. Each jurisdiction has its own regime, and rights must be secured in every country where protection is wanted.

The Paris Convention, Berne Convention and PCT provide procedural mechanisms for streamlined multi-jurisdictional filing — but they do not create a global IP right.

Temporal scope

Each category carries its own term, set to reconcile the private interest of the creator with the public interest in dissemination.

On expiry the creation enters the public domain and may be freely used by all.

Lecture 3 of this unit is devoted entirely to the consequences of the middle column — the international machinery built to work around territoriality.

Importance

Six reasons IPR exists — and five that are specific to computing

1 · Incentive to innovate

A time-limited monopoly lets innovators recover R&D costs. Without it, the free-rider problem discourages costly research.

2 · Encouraging disclosure

The patent bargain, a quid pro quo: full enabling disclosure in exchange for a limited monopoly. It prevents indefinite secrecy.

3 · Technology transfer

Licensing, joint ventures and university–industry collaboration all depend on a regime that defines what is being exchanged.

4 · Attracting investment

Venture capital and corporate R&D flow preferentially to jurisdictions where innovations are securely protected.

5 · Competitive and strategic value

Portfolios are used offensively (market exclusivity) and defensively (deterrence, cross-licensing). See standard-essential patents in 3G/4G/5G and Wi-Fi.

6 · Ethical and professional duty

Attribute prior work, respect others’ rights, and disclose inventions made in employment. IPR literacy is part of the responsible conduct of research.

Five features that make IP acute in computer science

Software is both functional and expressive, blurring patentable subject matter and copyrightable expression · open-source licensing grants rights conditionally and must be managed · algorithms and business methods diverge across jurisdictions · data and databases sit at the intersection of copyright, sui generis rights and confidentiality · AI-generated works challenge the requirement of human authorship.

History · 1 of 2

From a Venetian statute to the WTO

Early origins

Venice, 1474 — the earliest recorded patent-like statute: ten years’ exclusivity for new and useful inventions. It induced glassmakers to disclose techniques rather than keep them as guild secrets.

England, 1624 — the Statute of Monopolies ended arbitrary Crown monopolies but preserved an exception for inventions, founding the modern patent system.

1710 — the Statute of Anne, generally regarded as the foundation of modern copyright.

The 19th century — international coordination

Paris Convention, 1883 — national treatment and the right of priority, for patents, trademarks and designs.

Berne Convention, 1886 — automatic copyright protection without formalities.

Madrid Agreement, 1891 — international registration of trademarks.

The 20th century — institutionalisation and globalisation

PCT, 1970
Unified international filing procedure, administered by WIPO

Budapest Treaty, 1977
Deposit of microorganisms

TRIPS, 1994
The most comprehensive multilateral IP agreement in force; binds all WTO members

WCT & WPPT, 1996
Copyright extended to the digital environment

Read the arc as a negotiation, repeated every century, between private reward and public benefit.

History · 2 of 2

India: from colonial statute to TRIPS compliance

1911

Pre-independence — the Indian Patents and Designs Act governed the colonial period. It allowed product patents in all fields, including pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals.

1970

Post-independence — the Patents Act, 1970 restricted product patents in pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals while permitting process patents, deliberately balancing IP protection against public welfare.

2005

TRIPS compliance — following the Uruguay Round, amendments in 1999, 2002 and 2005 reintroduced product patents across all fields of technology, with effect from 1 January 2005.

2016

Contemporary — the National IPR Policy (2016) provided a roadmap; specialised IP benches in High Courts, the IPR Awareness Campaign, and the IP Facilitating Centre followed.

The through-line is a tension between indigenous knowledge systems and the colonial legacy of British IP law — and a continuing insistence on preserving public-interest flexibilities. You will see the same tension again in Novartis v. Union of India in Lecture 3.

Patents · 1 of 2

Three cumulative conditions — and Section 3(k)

Section 2(1)(j) defines an invention as “a new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application.” The three conditions are cumulative — failing any one is fatal.

Novelty — new

Not anticipated by prior art anywhere in the world before the filing or priority date.

Inventive step — non-obvious

A feature that would not be obvious to a person skilled in the art.

Industrial application — useful

Capable of being made or used in an industry.

Patentable in a CS context

  • A novel algorithm embodied in a concrete technical application — e.g. an image compression method with measurable technical effect
  • A hardware architecture or chip design
  • A computer-implemented invention producing a technical effect
  • A method of manufacturing or industrial process

Not patentable — Section 3

  • Frivolous, or contrary to public order or morality
  • A scientific principle, theory or mathematical method in the abstract
  • Schemes, rules or methods of performing mental acts or playing games
  • Presentation of information
  • Section 3(k): a mathematical or business method, or a computer program per se, or algorithms

The boundary between a patent-eligible computer-implemented invention and a non-patentable abstract algorithm is the most actively litigated question in the field. Lecture 2 treats Section 3(k) and technical effect in detail.

Patents · 2 of 2

Twenty years, five rights, and one famous caveat

Duration — Section 53

T_patent = 20 years from the date of filing

Aligned with TRIPS Article 33, which mandates a minimum of 20 years from filing. There is no provision for extension of the term in India.

Renewal fees are payable annually from the third year; failure to pay causes lapse, with restoration possible within 18 months on a surcharge.

Rights conferred — Section 48

  1. Prevent third parties from making the patented product
  2. Prevent third parties from using the patented process
  3. Prevent offering for sale, selling or importing the product, or a product obtained directly by the process
  4. Assign or licence the patent to others
  5. Seek relief for infringement — injunctions, damages, accounts of profits

The caveat that matters

The patentee has no positive right to practise the invention — only a negative right to exclude. Where a complementary patent is held by another party, the patentee may be blocked, and may seek a compulsory licence under Section 84.

Designs

Industrial designs protect how a thing looks, not how it works

Designs Act, 2000, Section 2(d): “design means only the features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to any article… by any industrial process or means.”

Three conditions

Novelty — new or original
Distinctiveness — significantly distinguishable from known designs
Not disclosed — unpublished and unused, in India or elsewhere, before filing

Duration — Section 11

10 years + 5 years = 15 years max

Ten years from registration; a five-year extension must be applied for before the initial period expires.

Not registrable — Section 4

Not new or original · already disclosed · contrary to public order or morality · a mere mechanical contrivance · a workshop improvement · trade or property marks · a design dictated solely by function — the “must-fit” exclusion

Rights conferred — Section 22

The exclusive right to apply the design to any article in the registered class: sue for infringement (Section 22 incorporates the Copyright Act’s offences and remedies) · assign or licence · recover damages · seek injunctions.

Relevance to computer science

The physical form of laptops, smartphones and wearables · the visual design of consumer electronics · graphical user interfaces, registrable in India since the 2008 amendment, though with restrictions on purely functional or animated GUIs.

Design versus patent, in one line: a novel laptop hinge mechanism is patentable; the visual appearance of the casing is registrable as a design. Both can coexist on the same product.

Copyright · 1 of 2

Copyright arises automatically — and protects expression, not ideas

Seven classes of work — Section 13

Original literary works — books, computer programs, tables, compilations, databases where original · dramatic works · musical works · artistic works · cinematograph films · sound recordings · architectural works

Unlike patents and designs, copyright requires no registration to subsist. Registration under Section 44 gives procedural advantage and prima facie evidence.

What copyright covers in software

  • Source code, in any programming language
  • Object code — the compiled form
  • Documentation and user manuals
  • Website content: text, graphics, layout
  • Architecture diagrams, as artistic works
  • Database contents, where there is originality of selection or arrangement

The idea–expression dichotomy — the central tenet

Computer programs are explicitly treated as literary works under Section 2(o). Protection extends to the expression — source code, object code, and certain structural and organisational elements — but not to the ideas, algorithms, methods of operation or underlying logic.

The algorithm for sorting an array is not copyrightable. A specific implementation of it in C++ is.

Codified by Section 13 and reinforced by the Berne Convention, which Lecture 3 examines.

Copyright · 2 of 2

Two kinds of right — and only one of them can be sold

Duration — Sections 22 to 28

Life of the author + 60 years
literary, dramatic, musical, artistic works

60 years from publication
films, sound recordings, photographs, government works

60 years from publication
anonymous and pseudonymous works, and works of joint authorship

Counted from the calendar year following the author’s death, or the year of first publication for corporate and statutory works.

Economic rights — Section 14

The exclusive right to reproduce the work · issue copies to the public · perform it in public · communicate it to the public · make translations, adaptations or arrangements · make films or sound recordings of it.

These are assignable and licensable — they are the commercial asset.

Moral rights — Section 57

Retained by the author even after assigning the economic rights:

Paternity — the right to claim authorship
Integrity — to restrain distortion or modification prejudicial to honour or reputation
Retraction — in limited circumstances, to withdraw the work from circulation

Moral rights are perpetual, inalienable and non-assignable.

The distinction that matters most

Copyright in software versus a software patent

AspectCopyright in softwareSoftware patent
ProtectsExpression — the code, its structureThe underlying functional concept
Registration requiredNo — automatic on creationYes
DurationLife + 60 years20 years from filing
Disclosure requirementNoneYes — full enabling disclosure
Cost of acquisitionMinimalSubstantial

They are not alternatives

In practice software is protected by both — copyright on the code, and where available, a patent on the underlying method. They protect different things and fail in different ways.

The trade-off, stated plainly

A patent costs money and requires you to publish the invention. A trade secret costs nothing and lasts indefinitely — but evaporates the moment someone independently discovers or reverse-engineers it. Choosing between them is a strategic decision, not a legal one.

Process

Securing protection: one architecture, eight stages

1 · Identification

Does the output qualify as an IP asset? Mere ideas, abstract principles, natural phenomena and discoveries do not.

2 · Prior art search

Any disclosure anywhere in the world before the filing date. For trademarks, earlier similar marks; for copyright, prior publication.

3 · Choosing the category

A single innovation may be protected under several categories at once — the layered strategy on the next slide.

4 · Drafting and filing

In India, with the CGPDTM under DPIIT. Internationally via the PCT (patents), Madrid (marks), Hague (designs).

5 · Examination

Formal (procedural compliance) and substantive (novelty, inventive step, industrial applicability). In India, examination is on request, within 48 months of priority.

6 · Grant

The IP is granted or registered, and the owner acquires enforceable rights.

7 · Maintenance

Periodic renewal — annually for patents, every ten years for trademarks in India. Failure to renew causes lapse.

8 · Enforcement

Civil litigation (injunction, damages, accounts of profits), criminal prosecution where applicable, and administrative remedies such as customs seizure of infringing imports.

The patent process is the most elaborate of the eight regimes and therefore serves as the paradigm. Lecture 2 takes stages 2 to 8 apart in detail.

Worked case

One product, five forms of protection

Scenario — a team of B.Tech students develops a novel image compression algorithm (“Algorithm X”) and ships a full-stack mobile application implementing it. What can they protect, and how?

The analysis

1 · The algorithm as a method — if it produces a technical effect, such as reduced bandwidth at no perceptual loss, it may be a patentable computer-implemented invention under Sections 2(1)(j) and 3. File before any public disclosure.

2 · The source code — automatically protected by copyright under Section 13(1)(a). Registration adds prima facie evidentiary value.

3 · The app icon and UI layout — the icon may be an artistic work; the screen appearance may be registrable as an industrial design post-2008.

4 · The brand name — a separate application under the Trade Marks Act, 1999.

5 · Multi-country protection — a single PCT application designating the chosen jurisdictions; track the 30/31-month national phase deadline.

The ordering constraint

Patent first, publish second. A conference paper, an arXiv preprint or a public GitHub commit is prior art against your own application in an absolute-novelty jurisdiction.

Strategic recommendation

A layered IP strategy — copyright plus patent plus design plus trademark — is usually the most robust approach for a software innovation, because each layer fails in a different way and covers a different attack.

Consolidation

What you must be able to do by Lecture 2

Six takeaways

  • IP is creations of the mind; IPR is the legal entitlement that vests in the creator
  • IPR is negative, territorial, time-limited, assignable and statutory — no natural-law basis
  • The evolution from Venice 1474 to TRIPS 1994 is a repeated negotiation between private reward and public benefit
  • Eight categories, of which patents, designs and copyright matter most in computing
  • Patent protects function, design protects appearance, copyright protects expression — and they coexist
  • Protection follows one eight-stage architecture: identify, search, categorise, file, examine, grant, maintain, enforce
