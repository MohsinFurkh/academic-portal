Why cooperation exists

Rights stop at the border; products do not

The nineteenth-century problem

Exhibitors refused to show inventions at international exhibitions because displaying them abroad destroyed novelty at home, and foreign applicants were often treated worse than locals.

Two treaties answered it: the Paris Convention (1883) for industrial property and the Berne Convention (1886) for literary and artistic works. Both are still the foundation.

What treaties can and cannot do

They can: require national treatment, create priority periods, set minimum standards, and build shared filing and search machinery.

They cannot: grant a right. Every patent, every trademark, every design is still granted and enforced country by country, under that country's law and in that country's courts.

National treatment is the oldest idea in the system: a member state must treat applicants from other member states no worse than its own nationals. Everything else is built on that.

The architecture, in order of construction

Six milestones that made the present system

1883

Paris Convention

Industrial property: national treatment and the right of priority.

1886

Berne Convention

Copyright: protection without formality, and minimum terms.

1967

WIPO established

A UN agency administering the treaties and the global filing systems.

1970

PCT concluded

One international application, one search, many countries later.

1995

TRIPS in force

IP minimum standards tied to WTO membership and dispute settlement.

Since

Indian alignment

Product patents, PCT membership, and the 2005 amendments to the Patents Act.

Read the shape of it: the nineteenth-century treaties were about fair treatment of foreigners; the twentieth-century ones added shared administrative machinery; TRIPS added enforceable minimum standards with trade consequences. Each layer reduced national discretion a little further — which is exactly what the development debate on the last slides is about.

Paris Convention

The priority right: your filing date travels with you

How it works

File first in one member country. You then have 12 months for patents and utility models — and 6 months for industrial designs and trademarks — to file in other member countries and claim the first filing's date.

Those later filings are judged for novelty as if they had been made on the first date. Anything published in between — including by someone else — does not defeat them.

Why the 12 months matter so much

  • You can publish your paper after the first filing without losing foreign rights.
  • You get a year to find out whether the invention is worth money before paying for filings in several countries.
  • You get a year to raise the funds — foreign filing is where academic IP budgets break.
  • And, combined with the PCT, that year can effectively become thirty months before most national costs fall due.

Indian residents have an extra duty: before filing abroad for an invention made in India, you generally need either to have filed in India first and waited the prescribed period, or to obtain written permission for foreign filing from the Indian Patent Office. Filing straight to a foreign office without it can have serious consequences — ask your IP cell, always.

Berne Convention

Copyright travels with no paperwork at all

No formalities

Protection arises automatically on creation in every member state. No registration, no notice, no fee. Your code and your thesis are protected in dozens of countries the moment they exist.

Minimum standards

Members must give a minimum set of rights and a minimum term — life plus 50 years for most works. Many countries give more; India gives life plus 60.

National treatment again

A work by an Indian author gets, in France, whatever French law gives French authors. So the term and the exceptions differ by country even though the right arises everywhere.

The practical contrast to take away: copyright is global and automatic but narrow — it protects your expression only. Patents are national, expensive and slow but broad — they stop independent re-invention. That asymmetry explains why most software research is protected by copyright plus publication, and only occasionally by patents.

WIPO

The agency that runs the machinery

What WIPO does

  • Administers treaties — Paris, Berne, PCT, Madrid, Hague, Budapest and others
  • Runs global filing systems: one application, many designated countries
  • Publishes and indexes: PATENTSCOPE, the global brand and design databases
  • Provides dispute services, including domain-name arbitration
  • Builds capacity in member states and maintains classifications (IPC, Nice, Locarno)

What WIPO does not do

It does not grant patents. The PCT gives you an international application, an international search and a written opinion — the decision to grant is always taken by a national or regional office.

It does not enforce anything. Infringement is a matter for national courts. WIPO administers; WTO dispute settlement, under TRIPS, is where state-to-state disputes go.

Useful to you as a researcher: PATENTSCOPE for searching published international applications, and the IPC classification you met in Part 2. Both return in the Unit VI database lab.

TRIPS · 1995

Minimum standards, with trade consequences attached

What it requires of members

  • Patents available for inventions in all fields of technology, product and process
  • A minimum term of 20 years from filing
  • Protection for copyright, trademarks, designs, geographical indications, layout designs and undisclosed information
  • Computer programmes protected as literary works under Berne
  • Effective enforcement procedures, and non-discrimination between fields

The flexibilities it preserves

  • Members define patentability criteria in their own law — which is how India's Section 3(d) survives
  • Compulsory licensing, on stated conditions
  • Exceptions for research and regulatory approval use
  • Freedom on exhaustion and parallel imports
  • The Doha declaration's confirmation that TRIPS should be read as supporting public health
TRIPS is why India moved to product patents in pharmaceuticals in 2005. The flexibilities are why the same amendment carried Section 3(d) — a deliberate national choice within an international minimum.

Putting it together

A realistic filing plan for a university invention

WhenStepWhy then
Month 0File an Indian provisional specificationFixes priority cheaply, before the conference submission
Month 0+Submit the paper, give the talk, push the codePriority already secured; publication no longer defeats you
Month 12File the Indian complete specification, and a PCT application claiming priorityThe Paris year expires here; PCT keeps every member country open
Month ~16–18Read the international search report and written opinionThe first independent assessment of novelty and inventive step — often the cheapest reality check you will ever get
Month 18International publicationThe application becomes public and citable prior art against others
Month 30–31Enter national phases in chosen countries onlyBy now you know whether anyone wants it. This is where the money goes.
The PCT's real product is time and information: thirty months of optionality, plus a search report that tells you whether to spend more. For an academic group with no licensee yet, that is usually worth more than the filings themselves.

The other side of the argument

Harmonisation is contested, and India has been at the centre of it

Access to medicines

Stronger patents raise prices; generics lower them. India's pre-2005 process-patent regime built a large generics industry, and the post-TRIPS settlement — Section 3(d), oppositions, compulsory licensing — is how the tension is managed in Indian law.

Traditional knowledge

Patents granted abroad over uses of turmeric, neem and basmati were challenged and revoked or narrowed. India's response included the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library, so examiners can find such knowledge as prior art.

Software and standards

Jurisdictions differ sharply on software patentability, and standard-essential patents raise licensing disputes that cross every border at once. Both return in Unit VI.

The methodological point for this course: IP rules are policy choices, not natural laws. Each one trades incentive to create against access to use. When you read that a country's rule is “weak” or “strong”, ask the researcher's question — weak or strong for whom, measured how?

Key takeaways

Five things about the international system

1

Rights are territorial. Treaties coordinate; only national offices grant.

2

Paris gives national treatment and the priority year — 12 months for patents, 6 for designs and marks.

3

Berne makes copyright automatic and borderless; WIPO runs the machinery; TRIPS sets enforceable minimum standards and preserves flexibilities.

4

PCT for patents, Madrid for marks, Hague for designs — each buys one filing and deferred cost, not a global right.

5

Indian residents must respect the foreign-filing permission requirement for inventions made in India.
