Where we are

You have results. Nobody has read them yet.

What Units I–III gave you

  • Unit I — a bounded problem, objectives, a hypothesis that can lose
  • Unit II — a proof method, baselines, metrics, an evaluation you can defend
  • Unit III — a gap statement, ethics approval, honest citation practice

All of it now has to survive the journey into someone else's head — a reviewer's, an examiner's, a manager's — through nothing but text on a page.

The uncomfortable arithmetic

A reviewer gives your paper 20–40 minutes. An examiner reads your chapter in one sitting, late. A panel member skims your proposal on the morning of the meeting.

They are not hostile. They are time-bound. Any sentence that has to be read twice spends a currency you cannot refill.

So the bar is not “understandable if you try hard”. It is understandable at reading speed, once.

Writing is not decoration applied after the research. It is the last stage of the method — and it can invalidate the rest.

Two cases

A missing comma. A missing unit. Both were writing failures.

O'Connor v. Oakhurst Dairy · 1st Cir., 2017

Maine's overtime law exempted “the canning, processing, preserving … packing for shipment or distribution of” food.

One activity, or two? Drivers said one and claimed overtime; the dairy said two. The court found the clause ambiguous, ruled for the drivers, and the parties settled for $5 million.

A serial comma would have settled it either way.

Mars Climate Orbiter · NASA, 1999

One team's software produced thrust in pound-force seconds; the other consumed it as newton-seconds. The interface spec named the unit; the delivered file did not, and nobody checked.

The spacecraft entered the atmosphere ~170 km too low and was destroyed. The board's root cause: a failure of communication between teams.

An unlabelled number is not data — it is a rumour.

Your intention is not evidence. Only the sentence is evidence, and it will be read by someone who cannot ask you what you meant.

The map · keep this slide

Five characteristics — and the question each one answers

Clarity

Reader asks: what is this sentence about?

One idea per sentence. A real subject doing a real verb. No pronoun that is a guess.

Conciseness

Reader asks: why am I still reading this?

Every word earns its place. Brevity by deletion, not by abbreviation.

Coherence

Reader asks: why is this sentence here?

Each sentence connects to the last. One paragraph, one point, announced first.

Accuracy

Reader asks: is this exactly true?

Numbers with units and n. Methods with versions. Claims with conditions.

Objectivity

Reader asks: are you selling me something?

The evidence argues; you do not. No emotive adjectives, no hidden negatives.

Ordered by who is harmed when they fail: the first two cost the reader time, coherence costs you your argument, the last two cost you your credibility.

A · Clarity

Unclear sentences fail in only three ways

1

Buried actor

✗ “An evaluation of the model was performed.”

✓ “We evaluated the model on 1,200 scans.”

Repair: ask who did what? and put it at the front.

2

Nominalisation

✗ “performs the segmentation of”, “gives an improvement in”

✓ “segments”, “improves”

Repair: hunt -tion, -ment, -ance, -ity; turn each back into its verb.

3

Floating reference

✗ “Dice rose but inference time rose. This is a problem.”

✓ “This latency increase is a problem.”

Repair: never let “this” stand alone — add the noun.

The 30-second test: read a sentence and answer out loud — who does what to whom? If you cannot, it has one of these three defects.

B · Conciseness

Brevity comes from deletion, not from abbreviation

The four deletions, in this order

  1. Throat-clearing openers — “It is important to note that…”, “In this section we will discuss…”
  2. Empty intensifiers — very, quite, really, extremely, highly
  3. Redundant pairs — each and every, basic fundamentals, final outcome, past history
  4. Long connectives — see the table

Not conciseness: dropping units, abbreviating technical terms, or removing the conditions on a claim. That is inaccuracy wearing a smaller coat.

DeleteWrite
due to the fact thatbecause
in order toto
a large number ofmany (or the number)
has the ability tocan
at this point in timenow
make a comparison ofcompare
the results that were obtainedthe results
Target: cut 25% of the words with no loss of content. It is almost always there. When you can no longer find it, your prose has reached professional density.

C · Coherence

Start each sentence with something the reader already knows

✗ Broken chain

Our model reached 0.91 Dice. Data augmentation was applied to the training set. Field scans are often missing a sequence. We used a 3D U-Net backbone.

Four topics in four sentences. Every sentence is true; the paragraph says nothing.

✓ Chained

Our model reached 0.91 Dice on BraTS. That score fell to 0.64 on hospital scans, which often lack one MRI sequence. To recover part of this loss, we dropped sequences at random during training. The augmented model recovered 0.11 of the 0.27 lost.

One paragraph, one point

Announced in sentence one. A reader who reads only your first sentences should get the argument.

Signpost honestly

“However” must mark a real contrast; “therefore” a real consequence. Decorative connectives are worse than none.

Parallel form

Three objectives start with the same verb form. Tables and discussion follow the same order. Coherence is keeping promises about order.

D · Accuracy

The verb must match the evidence you actually collected

Evidence you haveYou may writeYou may not write
One run, one dataset, no baseline“On BraTS, the model reached 0.88 Dice in a single run.”outperforms · is effective · demonstrates
5 seeds, 3 datasets, published baseline“Across three datasets and five seeds, M improves Dice by 4.1 ± 0.6 points over B.”any claim about why it works
The above + an ablation“The gain is attributable to the missing-sequence augmentation.”“caused by”, unless everything else was controlled
A 12-person user study, no control“All 12 participants completed the task faster with interface B (median 41 s vs 58 s).”users prefer B · B is more usable
A correlation in logged data“Sites with caching enabled showed 38% lower median latency.”caching reduced latency
Hedging is accuracy, not weakness. “Suggests”, “is consistent with”, “under these conditions” are precision instruments — and a reviewer is checking whether you know your own limits.

D · Accuracy

Five checks to run over every results section

Units

Every number carries its unit — and a precision you can justify. “0.9142” from 40 test cases claims a resolution your sample cannot support.

n

On how many cases, how many runs, with what spread? “Dice improved by 3 points” without n is not checkable.

Versions

Library, model, dataset release, hardware, and the date of access for anything online.

Conditions

“X beats Y” is incomplete. “X beats Y on BraTS 2021 under a 4 GB memory budget” is a claim.

Citations

Every reference says what you say it says. Cite-and-run is found instantly by a reviewer who knows the paper.

Accuracy is also what Unit III called integrity — the same habit, now visible as a number on a page.

E · Objectivity

Four ways objectivity fails — three of them without any lie

1 · Emotive vocabulary

“Our novel and powerful framework achieves outstanding performance.”

Adjectives are your opinion. Numbers are evidence. Let the reader supply the adjective.

2 · The convenient baseline

Comparing against U-Net 2015 when nnU-Net exists.

No sentence is false; the selection is the deception. A reviewer notices the missing baseline first.

3 · The vanished negative

The two datasets where the method lost are not mentioned.

A method with a stated failure mode is usable; one that never fails anywhere is not believed.

4 · Causal language, correlational data

“Augmentation caused the gain” — with no ablation.

The most common integrity error in student reports. Write “coincided with” or “is associated with”.

Objectivity is not the absence of a position. You may argue your method is better — with evidence a hostile reader could check, and by showing where it is weakest.

Writing for different audiences

The audience decides the structure, not just the tone

Academic reader (reviewer, examiner)Industry reader (manager, client)
Their question“Is this true, and is it new?”“What should we do on Monday, and what will it cost?”
OrderContext → gap → method → results → conclusionRecommendation first, then evidence, then detail in an annexe
Method detailFull: reproducible by a strangerEnough to trust the number, not to rebuild it
Counts as strongControls, baselines, statistics, limitationsCost, risk, timeline, effect on users and existing systems
UncertaintyStated precisely; hedging is a virtueStated as a decision risk: “if this fails, we lose X”
Length8–12 pages, or a chapterOne page, or five slides
Failure modeDesk rejection for an unsupported claimSilence: skimmed, filed, never acted on
The placement mistake: sending a manager the academic version, with the finding in the last paragraph. Invert the pyramid.

Same finding · three readers

Missing-sequence augmentation recovers 0.11 Dice, but training costs 2× and inference needs 4 GB

Academic · results section

“Randomly dropping sequences during training recovered 0.11 ± 0.02 macro-Dice on scans with one missing sequence (n = 3 sites, 5 seeds), at 2.1× training cost. The residual 0.16 gap tracks scanner variation, which the augmentation does not simulate; this is stated as a limitation in §6.”

Checked: n, spread, cost, failure regime, declared limit.

Industry · one-pager

Recommendation: deploy for the two hospitals with incomplete protocols; not for the paediatric unit. Accuracy on incomplete scans improves by roughly 40% of the lost quality. Cost: training doubles (one extra GPU-week) and inference needs a 4 GB card. Untested on paediatric cases.

Checked: what to do, cost, what breaks, what is out of scope.

Executive · one line

“The tool now works on scans where part of the study is missing, which is most scans outside a teaching hospital — with the caveat that we have not yet validated it for children.”

Notice: the caveat survived all three versions. Simplifying the language is legitimate; dropping the risk is not.

Conventions

Tense, voice and person — the defaults that sound professional

Tense by section

Established knowledgePresent
Prior work as an actPast
Your method and experimentsPast
What a figure showsPresent
ConclusionsPresent
Future workModal

Voice

Active by default — it names the actor and is shorter.

Passive deliberately, when the actor is irrelevant: “Scans were de-identified before analysis.”

The error is passive voice used to hide who made a choice: “the threshold was set to 0.7” invites the question you least want.

Person

“We” is standard in computing, even for a single author in many venues — check the venue.

“I” is normal in a thesis, where a named person defends choices.

Avoid “the author” and “one may observe that”. They cost words and buy nothing: objectivity comes from evidence, not from the pronoun.

Try it · 20 minutes

Sentence surgery on your own paragraph

Alone (10 min) — five passes, in this order

  1. Actor: for each sentence write who does what to whom; rewrite any you cannot answer
  2. Nominalisation: circle every -tion / -ment / -ance / -ity; convert three back to verbs
  3. Reference: circle every “this / it / these”; add the noun
  4. Deletion: openers, intensifiers, pairs, long connectives — count words before and after
  5. Chain: does each sentence open with something from the previous one?

Swap with your neighbour (10 min)

Read only the edited version. Answer in writing — answers, not encouragement.

  1. What is the one point of this paragraph? If you cannot say, name the sentence where you got lost.
  2. Which sentence did you read twice? Name its defect: actor, nominalisation or reference.
  3. Is any claim stronger than its evidence? Quote the verb.
  4. Which three words would you still cut?

Keep both versions. Part 2 places this paragraph inside a document structure.

Key takeaways

Five habits of readable technical prose

1

Unclear sentences fail in three ways: buried actor, nominalisation, floating reference.

2

Conciseness is deletion. Aim at 25% with nothing lost.

3

Coherence is the known–new chain, one point per paragraph, announced first.

4

Accuracy is units, n, versions, conditions and honest citations — and a verb matched to the evidence.

5

The audience sets the order: academic builds to the point, industry starts with it.
