The situation

The panel is deciding one thing: can this work be trusted?

What the panel already has

Your document — which one member may have read closely, two may have skimmed, and one received this morning.

So your talk is not a summary of the document. It is the argument, delivered in a form that survives partial reading: what you claim, what evidence supports it, and where it is weak.

Most of the marks in a review are decided in the Q&A, not the talk. The talk's job is to make the panel ask the questions you can answer well.

Three things the panel is testing

  1. Do you understand your own work? Anyone can read slides aloud. Only the author can answer “why this baseline?”
  2. Do you know its limits? A candidate who names their own weakness before the panel does is believed on everything else.
  3. Can you be corrected? A defensive answer to a fair criticism costs more marks than the criticism itself.
You are not defending a verdict of “flawless”. You are defending a claim, its evidence and its stated boundary — which is exactly what Units I to IV have been building.

Structure

A 10-minute review presentation, minute by minute

TimeSlideWhat it must do
0:00–0:30TitleName, title, supervisor, one sentence on what the work is about
0:30–2:00Problem & gap (1–2)The problem in the world, then the one sentence of what is missing. End with your research question on screen.
2:00–3:00Objectives (1)Three to four measurable objectives. The panel will use this slide as the agenda for the rest.
3:00–5:00Method (2)One pipeline figure, plus data, baselines and metrics. Enough to judge, not to reproduce.
5:00–7:30Results (2–3)One figure per objective, each with the takeaway stated aloud. Include the negative result.
7:30–8:30Limitations & next steps (1)What the evidence does not cover, and what you would do next. This slide buys you credibility.
8:30–9:00Conclusion (1)The claim, its number, its condition. Then stop talking.
9:00–10:00Backup slidesNever shown unless asked: extra tables, hyperparameters, failure cases, the ablation you left out.
Roughly one slide per minute, and build the backup deck deliberately — a backup slide produced instantly in answer to a question is the single most convincing moment available to you.

Slide design

The 7×7 rule — and what it is really telling you

The rule

At most 7 lines per slide, at most 7 words per line. Variants exist — 6×6, or the 1-6-6 rule (one idea, six bullets, six words) — and the number matters less than what it enforces.

What it actually enforces: that the audience can read the slide in the few seconds before they must listen to you again. Text beyond that is not read — it is competing with you.

Where it stops being useful

  • A figure, table or equation is not seven bullet points. Do not chop a results table to satisfy an arithmetic rule.
  • A slide with one sentence is better than one with seven fragments, when the sentence is the claim.
  • Backup slides may be dense — they exist to answer one question, on screen for thirty seconds, with you narrating.

Treat 7×7 as a smoke alarm, not a law: if you are breaking it, check whether you are reading your script off the wall.

The deeper rule behind it: the slide is the evidence, you are the argument. If the slide can be read aloud instead of you, one of you is unnecessary.

Slide design

The same content, twice

✗ The slide as a script

Results and Discussion of the Proposed Methodology

• In this work, we have proposed a novel deep learning based approach for the segmentation of brain tumours from magnetic resonance imaging data which has been collected from two different hospitals.

• The proposed methodology has been implemented using PyTorch 2.1 and trained on an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU with 16 GB memory for 300 epochs using the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of 1e-4.

• From the results obtained it can be clearly observed that the proposed method gives better performance as compared to the existing state-of-the-art methods in terms of the Dice similarity coefficient.

• The results are also compared with various other existing methods and the comparison is shown in the table given below for reference.

The audience reads it, finishes before you, stops listening — and the one number that matters never appears.

✓ The slide as evidence

Dropout training recovers 0.11 of 0.27 lost Dice
0.87 → 0.60 → 0.71

BraTS → clinical → clinical + sequence dropout

180 studies · 2 sites · 5 seeds · ± 0.03

Residual 0.16 gap: scanner variation, not the missing sequence

Title states the finding. Three numbers, their conditions, and the honest remainder. You supply the sentences.

Slide design

Eight rules that survive every template

1 · Title states the finding

“Dice falls 0.27 on clinical scans”, not “Results”. The title is the sentence a distracted panel member reads.

2 · One message per slide

If you need “and also”, make another slide. Slides are free; attention is not.

3 · Minimum 24 pt

Anything smaller will not be read from the back row — including axis labels, which are usually the worst offenders.

4 · High contrast, colour-safe

Assume a washed-out projector and at least one colour-blind viewer. Never encode meaning in colour alone.

5 · Number every slide

So the panel can say “go back to slide 7” instead of “that one with the table”.

6 · Figures over tables

A table on a slide should have at most four rows and four columns; anything larger belongs in backup.

7 · No unexplained acronyms

The panel includes non-specialists. Every acronym costs one member a sentence of attention.

8 · No animation that delays reading

Build a figure in steps if it explains a process; never make people wait for a word to fly in.

Slide design

A results figure on a slide is not the figure from your paper

Strip it down

Remove gridlines, extra series, minor ticks and the legend if two labels on the plot would do. A paper figure is studied; a slide figure is glanced at.

Point at the claim

Highlight the one bar, point or cell the sentence is about, and grey the rest. If you must say “look at the third column”, the slide has already failed.

Keep the honesty

Axis starts at zero unless you say otherwise, error bars stay, and n stays on the slide. Simplifying the design is legitimate; simplifying the uncertainty is not.

Say the takeaway before you describe the axes: “this figure shows the gap is scanner-driven — accuracy on the x-axis, site on the y-axis.” A panel that knows what to look for finds it in two seconds.

Delivery

What actually makes a talk land

Rehearsal is the whole technique

  • Aloud, standing, timed — at least three full runs. Silent reading takes 30% less time than speaking, which is how talks overrun.
  • Once to a non-specialist. If your roommate cannot state your claim afterwards, the panel's non-specialist will not either.
  • Rehearse the first and last 30 seconds until they are automatic. Those are where nerves do the most damage.
  • Rehearse the answers, not just the talk — see the next slide.

In the room

  • Cut to fit, in advance. Running over is read as poor planning — the same judgement your Gantt chart was being scored on.
  • Look at the panel, not the screen. Glance at your laptop, speak to faces.
  • Pause instead of filling. A silence is invisible to the audience; “um, so, basically” is not.
  • Never apologise for your slides, your results or your English. It invites a criticism nobody had made.
  • Check the projector and the file format early — and carry a PDF.
Nervousness is not a defect and the panel does not mark it. Preparation is what they mark, and it is visible in the first thirty seconds.

Question time

Four kinds of question, and what each one really wants

Clarification

“What exactly is in the test set?”

They are trying to follow. Answer briefly and precisely; do not treat it as an attack.

Probing

“Why did you choose Dice and not Hausdorff?”

They are testing whether the choice was made or inherited. Give the reason and its trade-off.

Challenging

“Your sample is too small to claim that.”

Often correct. Concede the scope, defend the narrower claim you can actually support.

Hostile or off-topic

“Why didn't you use [the member's own method]?”

Stay courteous and factual. The rest of the panel is watching how you respond, not who is right.

Before the review, write down the six questions you least want to be asked, and prepare an answer and a backup slide for each. In practice, four of the six get asked.

Question time

Four steps, in this order, for every answer

1

Listen, then pause

Let them finish. Two seconds of silence buys a better answer than any opening filler.

2

Restate in one line

“So the question is whether the gain survives on the second site.” It confirms you understood, and buys thinking time.

3

Answer with evidence

Lead with the answer, then the evidence — a number, a figure, a backup slide.

4

Bound it

Say where your evidence stops. “Beyond two sites I have no data, and that is in my future work.”

Worked answer

Q: “Twelve of your 180 studies are paediatric. Can you really claim anything about children?”

A: “The question is whether the paediatric subgroup supports a claim. It does not — twelve studies is far too few, and I have not made a claim for that group. On slide 14 the paediatric cases are reported separately precisely because their Dice is 0.09 lower, and I treat that as a signal rather than a result. Establishing it properly would need roughly eighty cases, which is the first item in my future work.”

Conceded, evidenced, bounded, and turned into a plan — in four sentences, without defending something the data cannot support.

Question time

The five questions that catch students out

The questionWhat it is really askingHow to handle it
“What is novel here?”Can you state your contribution in one sentence?Have it memorised: the gap, the thing you built or measured, the number.
“Why didn't you compare with [method X]?”Do you know the field, and was the omission a choice?If you know it: the reason (no code, different task, unavailable data). If you do not: “I don't know that work — I will look at it” and write it down.
“Your dataset is too small.”Do you know what your evidence supports?Agree, then narrow the claim to what n supports, and give the number you would need.
“Isn't this just applying an existing method?”Is the contribution the method, or the finding?Name which one it is. A careful evaluation in a new setting is a legitimate contribution — say so, and say what it revealed.
“How would this work in practice?”Have you thought past the benchmark?Give the deployment constraint you measured — hardware, latency, the missing-data case — and the one you did not.
“I don't know” is a complete answer when followed by one more sentence: what you would do to find out. Inventing an answer is the only response that cannot be recovered from.

Question time

Six sentences to delete from your vocabulary

“That's in my report.”

Answer the question. The panel is asking whether you know it, not whether the page exists.

“Everyone does it this way.”

Convention is not a justification. Give the reason the convention exists, or concede the point.

“I didn't have time.”

Say what you chose to prioritise and why. Scope is a decision; time is an excuse.

“It's obvious that…”

It is not obvious to the person asking, and the phrase makes an ally into an opponent.

“My results prove…”

Almost nothing in empirical computing is proved. “Show”, “indicate”, “are consistent with” — the calibration from Part 1.

“Sorry, this slide is a bit messy.”

You have just told a panel that had not noticed. If it is messy, fix it before; if it is not, say nothing.

Try it · mock review panel

Three minutes each, then two minutes of questions

Presenting team (3 min)

Four slides only, from your Part 3 proposal sheet.

  1. Problem and gap — one sentence each
  2. Objectives — three, measurable
  3. Method and plan — one figure plus your Gantt chart
  4. Expected outcome and its biggest risk

Prepare two backup slides for the questions you least want.

Panel (2 min) — every member asks one question

Use one from each kind, and score the answers.

  • Clarification: “What exactly is your test data?”
  • Probing: “Why that baseline and not a stronger one?”
  • Challenging: “Your timeline has no buffer — what slips first?”
  • Practical: “What happens if ethics approval takes four months?”

Score each answer 0–2 on: direct, evidenced, bounded. Give the presenting team one sentence of written feedback.

Key takeaways

Four habits of a defence that holds

1

Structure the talk on a time map, roughly one slide per minute, with a deliberate backup deck.

2

7×7 is a smoke alarm, not a law: the slide is the evidence, you are the argument.

3

Name your own limitation before the panel does — it buys belief in everything else.

4

Answer in four steps — pause, restate, evidence, bound — and “I don't know, here is how I would find out” is a complete answer.
