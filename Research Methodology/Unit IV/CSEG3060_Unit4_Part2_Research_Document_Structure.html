The reading path

Nobody reads your paper from the first word

3 sec

Title

Is this my area? Do I take the review?

60 sec

Abstract

What is claimed, on what evidence?

3 min

Figures & captions

Your results are read before your prose.

1 min

Conclusion

Does the claim match the figures just seen?

8 min

Methods

Hunting for the flaw that explains the numbers.

last

Introduction

Mostly to check you know the field.

Your figures are load-bearing

They are read before your explanation, so every caption must stand alone — what is plotted, on what data, with what n.

The abstract is not an introduction

It is the whole paper in 200 words, including the number. “Results are discussed” wastes your only guaranteed minute.

Polish where it is read

Title, abstract, captions, and the first and last paragraph of each section: ~15% of the words, ~80% of the attention.

Title

A title is a search query that someone else will type

What a working title contains

  • The object — what system, data or phenomenon
  • The action or finding — what you did or learned
  • The condition — where the claim holds
  • Searchable keywords a stranger would type

Length 10–14 words. Acronyms only ones the whole field knows — not your own system's name, unless you also say what it does.

TitleVerdict
A Study of Machine Learning TechniquesFails
An Efficient and Novel Approach for Medical Image Processing Using AIFails
SegNet-X: A Framework for Smart HealthcareFails
Brain Tumour Segmentation with Missing MRI Sequences on Clinical ScansWorks
Sequence-Dropout Training Recovers 40% of Dice Lost to Incomplete MRI StudiesWorks
Does Augmentation Close the Benchmark-to-Clinic Gap in Tumour Segmentation?Works
Failing titles describe the activity (“a study of…”). Working titles name the object, the finding and the condition.

Abstract

Five moves, in this order, one to two sentences each

1

Context

One sentence a non-specialist can follow. What area, why it matters.

2

Gap

Usually starts “However,…”. This sentence justifies the paper's existence.

3

What you did

Enough to judge, not to reproduce. Name the data and the evaluation.

4

Result

With a number. This is the sentence readers quote.

5

Implication

What changes — for practice, or for the next study.

Worked example · 112 words, all five moves

Automated brain tumour segmentation could extend expert review to hospitals without a neuroradiologist. However, published models assume all four MRI sequences are present, and the accuracy cost of a missing sequence has not been quantified on routine Indian clinical scans. We evaluated three published models on 180 clinical studies from two hospitals and measured the effect of dropping sequences at random during training. Mean Dice fell from 0.87 on BraTS to 0.60 on clinical scans missing one sequence; sequence-dropout training recovered 0.11 of the 0.27 lost. Augmentation is therefore a partial remedy, and the residual gap tracks scanner variation rather than the missing sequence itself.

Abstract

The abstract I receive most often

✗ As submitted

“Brain tumour detection is a very important problem in medical imaging. Many researchers have proposed various methods using deep learning. In this paper, a novel deep learning based approach is proposed for tumour segmentation. The proposed methodology is implemented and various experiments are performed. The results show that the proposed method gives better performance compared to existing methods. The paper is organised as follows.”

What is missing, move by move

  • Gap: “many researchers have proposed” is not a gap — nothing is said to be missing
  • Method: no data, no setting, no evaluation named
  • Result: no number at all — the one sentence a reader would quote
  • Implication: replaced by a table of contents

Every sentence would survive unchanged in a paper about a completely different topic. That is the definition of a sentence carrying no information.

Write the abstract last, from the finished sections — it is a miniature of a document that already exists.

Introduction

Four moves, wide to narrow, ending in a numbered list

1 · Territory — the problem in the world; why anyone outside your lab cares
▼
2 · What is known — the state of the art, grouped by approach, not listed by author
▼
3 · The gap — one explicit sentence
▼
4 · This paper — question, approach, contribution list

The classic Create A Research Space move structure (Swales, 1990): establish a territory, establish a niche, occupy the niche.

The last paragraph does the heavy lifting

This paper makes three contributions:

(1) a clinical dataset of 180 labelled studies from two hospitals, with realistic missing sequences;

(2) the first quantification of the benchmark-to-clinic Dice gap for these conditions (0.27);

(3) an ablation showing sequence-dropout training recovers 0.11 of that gap, and evidence that the rest is scanner variation.

Three failure modes: a contribution that is an activity (“we studied X”); the obvious application of an existing method with nothing new measured; and six contributions, which signals that none is substantial.

Methodology

The test: could a competent stranger repeat this and get your numbers?

What must be stated

  • Data: source, size, period, labelling, agreement, and the exact split
  • Preprocessing: every step, in order, with parameters
  • Method: what you changed, made explicit
  • Hyperparameters: values and how they were chosen
  • Baselines: which version, retrained or as published
  • Metrics: defined, with the averaging stated
  • Environment: versions, hardware, seeds, run count

How to organise it

In execution order: data → preprocessing → method → training → evaluation protocol, with those subsection names.

A pipeline figure replaces two paragraphs — but every box must be named in the text.

Past tense, and honest about choices: “we set…” rather than “it was set…”, which invites the question was it tuned on the test set?

The four omissions that sink student methods

  1. The split is never described — so leakage cannot be ruled out, and will be assumed
  2. Hyperparameter selection unstated — if chosen on test, every number is optimistic
  3. Number of runs missing — one run is an anecdote
  4. Baseline configuration missing — a baseline trained for 10 epochs against your 100 is not a comparison

Self-test: give it to a classmate outside your group and ask what they would still have to guess.

Results & Discussion

Results say what happened. Discussion says what it means.

Results — observations only

  • Present findings in the order of your objectives
  • Every result gets a figure or table; the text says the one thing to notice
  • Negative and unexpected results go here too, in the same neutral voice
  • Numbers carry units, n and spread

“Mean Dice was 0.87 (± 0.01) on BraTS and 0.60 (± 0.03) on clinical scans across five seeds (Table 2). The largest drop was for the enhancing rim, 0.81 → 0.44.”

Banned here: because · this shows that · surprisingly · unfortunately · any comparison to another paper.

Discussion — five obligations

  1. Interpret the main result against the question you asked
  2. Compare with prior work, agreeing and disagreeing, with a reason
  3. Explain the surprises, including the negatives
  4. State limitations and what each costs the claim
  5. Bound the generalisation: where would you expect it to fail?

“Our 0.27 drop exceeds the 0.12 reported by [14], whose clinical set came from one scanner. This suggests the gap is driven less by the missing sequence than by acquisition variation — consistent with the enhancing rim, the most contrast-dependent region, falling furthest.”

Banned here: a number that has not already appeared in Results.

Figures & tables

If the caption does not stand alone, the figure does not work

Figure or table?

Figure when the message is a shape: a trend, a distribution, a comparison across conditions, a pipeline.

Table when the reader needs exact values or many dimensions at once.

Never both for the same data.

Caption rules

  • Figure captions below, table captions above (IEEE and ACM)
  • State what is plotted, on what data, with what n and what error bars mean
  • A caption may state the takeaway
  • Every figure referenced by number in the text
  • Axes labelled with quantity and unit; legible in print and in greyscale

Honest plotting — an ethics point, not an aesthetic one

  • Truncated y-axes turn one point into a cliff — say so in the caption
  • Bars without error bars imply precision you do not have
  • Different scales across panels being compared
  • Cherry-picked qualitative examples with no statement of how they were chosen

Unit III called this integrity. It arrives in your document as a choice of axis limits.

Conclusion & future work

A conclusion is not a summary. It states what is now known.

Conclusion · four sentences is often enough

  1. What we now know that we did not — the claim, with its central number
  2. Under what conditions it holds
  3. What changes as a result
  4. The honest boundary — what this does not settle

THE VERSION TO AVOID

“In this paper we proposed a novel method. Experiments were performed and the results show better performance than existing methods. In future we will extend this work.”

The abstract, reworded, with the numbers removed.

Future work · a plan, not a wish list

Weak future work lists topics: “we will try transformers, explore other datasets and deploy on mobile.” Anyone could have written that without doing your study.

Strong future work comes out of your own limitations: what gap it closes, why your evidence points there, and roughly what it would take.

“Because the residual gap tracks scanner variation rather than the missing sequence, the next study should test intensity harmonisation before segmentation, evaluated on the same 180 studies so the two are directly comparable. This needs a phantom scan per site — about two weeks of access.”

A proposal is, structurally, somebody's future-work section turned into a plan with a budget. That is Part 3.

Writing order

Printed order is not writing order

Write the parts constrained by facts first

  1. Figures and tables — before any prose. If you cannot draw the figure, you do not yet have the result.
  2. Methodology — you did it; it also reveals gaps while you can still fix them
  3. Results — narrate the figures in objective order
  4. Discussion — now that the findings are fixed
  5. Introduction — only now do you know what you are introducing
  6. Conclusion and future work
  7. Abstract — a miniature of a finished document
  8. Title — the finding you actually got

Why writing the introduction first fails

You introduce the paper you intended to write. The results come out differently — they always do — and the introduction now promises what the paper does not deliver. The reviewer's most damaging comment is then available for free: “the contributions claimed in §1 are not supported by §5.”

Word budget for a 10-page paper

Abstract 200 · Introduction 900 · Related work 700 · Methodology 1,500 · Results 1,200 · Discussion 1,200 · Conclusion 400.

Set it before you write and treat it as a constraint. Sections that overrun are usually Methodology containing results, or Results containing discussion.

Try it · 20 minutes

Build the skeleton of your group-project report

In your team (12 min) — one sheet, seven rows

TitleYour best 12 words: object, finding, condition
AbstractFive moves, one clause each — the result clause must contain a number
IntroductionThe gap sentence and the numbered contribution list
MethodologySubsection names, in execution order
ResultsThe figures and tables you will produce, one per objective
DiscussionOne sentence per obligation
Conclusion“We now know … under … which means …”

Swap with another team (8 min)

  1. Does the title give the object and the finding, or only the activity?
  2. Does the abstract contain a number? If not, which move is missing?
  3. Is the contribution list outcomes, or activities (“we implemented…”)?
  4. Does every objective have a figure or table assigned? Any that does not is an experiment nobody has planned to run.
  5. Is any sentence in Results actually Discussion? Quote it.

Keep the sheet: the same seven rows reappear inside the proposal you draft in Part 3.

Key takeaways

Five rules for a document that survives a reviewer

1

Each section answers one reader question; misplaced content is a structural defect.

2

Your document is read title → abstract → figures → conclusion → methods. Polish in that order.

3

The abstract has five moves and a number; the introduction ends in a numbered contribution list.

4

Results state observations; Discussion interprets, compares, explains surprises, limits and bounds.

5

Write figures first and the title last; future work is a plan with a cost.
