Orientation

The defence, in four pillars

A committee’s mandate is not merely to accept or reject. It is to interrogate the proposal’s assumptions, methodology, novelty, feasibility and impact. Treat every question as scrutiny of the work, not of you.

A · Architecture

Structural design of the presentation — slide sequence, time allocation, cognitive load.

B · Audio-visual aids

Multimodal communication: slides, demonstrations, figures — and their design rules.

C · Communication

Verbal, vocal and non-verbal delivery; the opening, the closing, and stage fright.

D · Alignment

Strategic alignment with the evaluation criteria that the committee actually scores.

Three terms you will be examined on

Rubric — a scoring framework of explicit criteria, performance descriptors and weightage.
Elevator pitch — a 60–90 second distillation of problem, motivation and expected contribution, used to open.
Back-pocket slide — a reserve slide answering an anticipated detailed query without disrupting the flow.

Anchored in practice

The guidance here follows standard practice in the IEEE, ACM and AAAI research communities, and peer-reviewed communication research — Mayer on cognitive load, Tufte on graphical integrity.

Pillar A · 1 of 3

The 10/20/30 rule, and why time is itself a criterion

10 slides

Maximum, for a 20-minute talk. For a typical 15-minute B.Tech project review, 8–10 slides is optimal.

20 minutes

Maximum speaking time. The constraint is the audience’s attention, not your material.

30-point font

Minimum size — legible from the back row of any standard lecture hall.

Single idea per slide

Each slide communicates one dominant cognitive unit. Mayer’s cognitive load theory of multimedia learning (2009) holds that working memory processes roughly 4±1 chunks simultaneously; overloading a slide measurably reduces comprehension.

Time discipline is scored

Committees read an overrun as lack of rehearsal, poor understanding of your own work, or disrespect for their schedule — usually all three.

The safe strategy: rehearse to 80 percent of the allotted time, leaving 20 percent of buffer for the natural expansion that happens under questioning.

Applied: a 15-minute slot means you rehearse to 12 minutes. If your rehearsal runs to 15, you will finish at 19 on the day — and be stopped at 15.

Pillar B · 1 of 4

The full range of audio-visual aids

Static visual

Slides (PowerPoint, Beamer, Google Slides) · posters · printed handouts

Dynamic visual

Animations and transitions · live system demonstration · video clips · code walk-throughs in an IDE

Auditory

Spoken narration · audio clips · music (rare, context-specific)

Hybrid

Interactive simulations · live polling (Mentimeter, Slido) · AR/VR demonstrations

Live demonstrations — high reward, real risk

  • Pre-record the demo as a video in case the live version fails
  • Use a sandbox environment with pre-loaded data
  • Keep it under 90 seconds in the main talk; longer demos move to the appendix and play only on request
  • Have a fallback slide summarising the demo’s outcome

Tools in academic CS presentations

Beamer (LaTeX) — publication-quality, ideal for theory-heavy talks · PowerPoint / Keynote — versatile, design-rich · Google Slides — collaborative · Overleaf + Beamer — manuscript and slides together · Marp, RevealJS — Markdown-based · OBS Studio, Loom — pre-recording · draw.io, Lucidchart — architecture diagrams · Matplotlib, Seaborn, Plotly — figures consistent with the proposal document

Pillar B · 2 of 4

CRAP, typography and the 60-30-10 rule

C · Contrast

High contrast between text and background — dark on light, or light on dark. Nothing in between.

R · Repetition

Consistent fonts, colours and layouts across every slide.

A · Alignment

Every element visually connected to at least one other element.

P · Proximity

Group related items spatially — closeness is the claim of relationship.

Typography

Title 28–32 pt, bold · body 22–24 pt regular · captions and footnotes not below 18 pt.

Prefer sans-serif families — Calibri, Arial, Helvetica, Lato. They render better than serifs at projection distance.

Colour — the 60-30-10 scheme

60% dominant — usually a white background or the institutional colour
30% secondary — headings and emphasis
10% accent — reserved for novel contributions and key results

Use colour-blind-friendly palettes — viridis, ColorBrewer — wherever graphs and charts appear.

The accent colour is a budget, not a decoration. If everything is highlighted, the committee cannot see what you think your contribution is.

Pillar C · 1 of 3

Pace, pausing and the voice

Pace and pausing

Optimal rate 130–150 words per minute — deliberately slower than conversation.

Strategic pauses of 1.5–2 seconds after key claims let the committee absorb and reflect.

Minimise fillers — um, uh, like, you know. A silent pause is always preferable to a filled one.

Articulation

CS terms are routinely mispronounced. Settle yours in rehearsal:

LaTeX (LAH-tek or LAY-tek) · GIF (soft G, contested) · Ubuntu (oo-BOON-too) · et al. · ibid. (IB-id) · viz. (namely, videlicet)

Vocal variation

Monotone delivery measurably reduces retention. Use pitch to signal meaning:

Rising — curiosity, open questions
Falling — definitive claims, conclusions
Lowered — emphasis on a critical claim

Non-verbal channels

Eye contact
Distribute gaze across all members; never fixate on one person or the screen

Posture
Upright, open stance; no crossed arms, no leaning on the podium

Gestures
Indicate transitions, enumerate points, point to slide regions

Movement
Confined but purposeful — do not pace

Expression
Confidence and enthusiasm; smile at the opening and in acknowledgements

Mehrabian’s model is frequently over-applied, but its useful core holds: a large share of meaning in face-to-face communication travels non-verbally.

Pillar C · 2 of 3

The first sixty seconds, and the last four sentences

Four openings that work — the primacy effect is durable

A provocative question
“What if a neural network could explain its own decisions to a radiologist in real time?”

A striking statistic
“Globally, 2.5 quintillion bytes of data are generated daily, yet 80% remains unstructured and unanalysed.”

A compelling narrative
A brief anecdote of a real-world failure that motivates the research.

A clear thesis
“This work proposes a federated learning framework that reduces communication overhead by 40% while preserving differential privacy guarantees.”

The closing — four sentences, in this order

  1. Summary of contribution
  2. Restatement of impact
  3. Forward-looking statement
  4. Invitation for questions

A weak closing undermines the strongest presentation. The closing must echo the opening.

The pattern, executed

“In summary, we have presented a lightweight intrusion detection system for IoT networks that achieves 96.4% accuracy with 60% lower computational cost than the state of the art. This has direct implications for smart-city deployments. We will extend this to edge-fog architectures in the next phase. We welcome your questions and feedback.”

Pillar C · 3 of 3

Managing stage fright — six evidence-based techniques

Stage fright is a documented physiological response, mediated by the amygdala’s release of adrenaline. It is not a character flaw and it is not evidence that you are unprepared — it is managed, not eliminated.

Cognitive reappraisal

Reframe the defence as a scholarly conversation, not a performance. You are the person in the room who has read the most about this problem.

Systematic desensitisation

Rehearse repeatedly in increasingly realistic conditions — alone, then to a peer, then in the actual room with the actual projector.

Physiological regulation

Box breathing (4-4-4-4) and progressive muscle relaxation, used in the ten minutes before you are called.

Preparation ritual

Arrive early, test the equipment, walk the room. Most acute pre-talk anxiety is displaced logistical anxiety.

Power posing

Research support is contested — but the psychological priming effect is real enough to be worth two minutes.

Hydration and rest

Avoid stimulants before the talk. Caffeine converts nervousness into a visible tremor and an accelerated speaking rate.

Pillar D · 1 of 3

Five kinds of question — classify before you answer

TypeIntentExampleStrategy
ClarificationConfirm understanding“Could you clarify what you mean by ‘embedding space’?”Paraphrase the question, then give a concise definition
ProbingTest depth of understanding“Why did you choose Adam over SGD for this model?”Explain the trade-offs; admit limitations where they exist
ChallengingTest defensibility of claims“Your baseline comparison is weak; have you considered X?”Acknowledge validity; defend or concede gracefully
SpeculativeExplore extensions“Could this method generalise to time-series data?”Distinguish present scope from future work; commit if plausible
ProceduralClarify logistics“What is your data acquisition timeline?”Answer directly; refer to the work plan slide
Classifying the question is the first step, and it takes under a second. Answering a challenging question as though it were a clarification — or vice versa — is how otherwise well-prepared candidates lose control of a defence.

Pillar D · 2 of 3

IDEAL — a structure for every answer

I

Interpret — repeat or paraphrase to confirm understanding. “If I understand correctly, you are asking whether our approach handles concept drift…”

D

Declare — state your position clearly. “Yes, we have addressed this through…” or “That is a limitation we acknowledge…”

E

Evidence — support with data, citations or logical reasoning

A

Assess — evaluate the implications. “This means our model can…”

L

Link back — connect the answer to the broader proposal narrative

The back-pocket slide strategy

Anticipate five to seven likely technical questions and prepare supplementary slides with the relevant data, equations or diagrams. Keep them after the visible deck and bring them up only when needed. This demonstrates preparedness without disrupting the narrative flow — and a presenter who can produce a convergence proof sketch on request has effectively ended that line of questioning.

Pillar D · 3 of 3

When you do not know, and when the room turns hostile

When you do not know the answer

  1. Acknowledge clearly — “That is an excellent point, and I do not have a definitive answer at this moment.”
  2. Demonstrate awareness — “However, I am aware of related work by [Author] that suggests…”
  3. Commit to follow-up — “I will investigate this and revert with a written response within the week.”

The committee is testing integrity as much as knowledge.

Hostile or aggressive questions

  • Stay calm — no raised voice, no visible irritation
  • Validate the question — “I appreciate the rigour of your scrutiny.”
  • Re-route to substance — engage the technical merit, not the interpersonal dynamic
  • Appeal to data — “The empirical evidence suggests…”
  • Defer to your guide where the question is genuinely outside your scope — “That is a strategic decision my supervisor and I will discuss; my immediate understanding is…”

Fabricating an answer is the single most damaging error a presenter can make. An honest “I do not know, and here is how I will find out” costs a fraction of what an invented one costs when the committee follows it up.

Strategic alignment

Different panels, different weights — and what the comments mean

Academic project review

Emphasis on methodology, learning outcomes and demonstration.

Thesis proposal defence

Emphasis on literature review, contribution and methodological soundness.

Funding agency (DST, SERB, industry)

Emphasis on broader impact, budget justification, deliverables and societal benefit.

Conference presentation

Emphasis on novelty, technical depth and engagement.

The weighted opener

Before the talk, identify the two or three criteria that carry the most weight in this specific context, and design the opening to maximise them.

For a DST-SERB proposal, lead with societal impact and deliverables. For a thesis defence, lead with methodological rigour and literature positioning. For an industry-sponsored project, lead with commercial viability and scalability.

Decoding what reviewers write

Mandatory corrections — must be addressed before approval (a methodological flaw, missing ethical clearance). These determine acceptance.

Suggested improvements — strengthen the proposal if addressed (an additional baseline comparison). These determine quality.

Appreciative remarks — validate the approach and signal confidence. These are strategic guidance for publication.

Practical example

A 15-minute B.Tech review, slide by slide

“Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning for Healthcare IoT” — team of four, assessed against the rubric on slide 15.

1 Title — project, team, supervisor, department, date. Clean institutional template.

2 Problem — “Hospitals cannot share patient data under HIPAA/GDPR, yet AI models require large datasets. How can we train a robust diagnostic model without centralising data?” Visual: split screen of two hospitals with a no-data-sharing lock.

3 Background — healthcare data breaches 2018–2024 (graph); growth of FL publications (graph). One bullet: “FL eliminates central aggregation but introduces privacy, communication and heterogeneity challenges.”

4 Gap — FedAvg, FedProx, SCAFFOLD in three columns with their limitations. Venn diagram of the challenges none fully address.

5 Objectives — (1) a DP-aware FL framework; (2) reduce communication rounds by 40%; (3) validate on three healthcare benchmarks.

6 Methodology — architecture diagram (clients → secure aggregation → global server with DP noise). Equation θ_{t+1} = θ_t − η∇L_DP(θ_t; ξ_t). Five-step workflow.

7 Preliminary results — table of accuracy, communication rounds and privacy budget ε against baselines; the proposed method in a coloured cell.

8 Work plan — Gantt for the remaining four months: experimentation (Jan–Feb), deployment (Mar), thesis writing (Apr).

9 References — McMahan et al. 2017, Abadi et al. 2016, Kairouz et al. 2021.

10 Closing — “We present a privacy-aware FL framework achieving 93.2% accuracy with 42% fewer communication rounds at ε = 1.0. We thank Dr. X and the IoT Lab, and welcome your questions.”

Back-pocket slides prepared for this defence

BPS-1 detailed convergence proof sketch · BPS-2 privacy budget analysis under composition theorems · BPS-3 comparison with FedBN for non-IID data · BPS-4 hyperparameter sensitivity plots

Before you present

Ten errors to avoid — in the order they occur

Before and during

  1. No rehearsal — the most common cause of a poor review
  2. Apologising at the start — “Sorry, I’m nervous” undermines you before you begin
  3. Reading slides — suggests lack of depth
  4. Overuse of jargon — define technical terms on first use
  5. No clear takeaway — every slide must advance the central thesis

Content and conduct

  1. Hiding limitations — committees will find them; pre-empt them
  2. Plagiarism in slides — reuse figures only with attribution; a serious ethical violation
  3. Ignoring the guide’s role — acknowledge supervisor contributions explicitly
  4. Disrespecting time — significant overrun forfeits goodwill
  5. Adversarial tone in Q&A — every question is an opportunity, not an attack
Errors 6 and 7 are not presentation faults — they are research-integrity faults that happen to surface in a presentation, and they connect directly to the Unit III material on plagiarism and attribution.

Consolidation · end of Unit IV

What you must be able to do — and the mock panel

Six takeaways

  • Architecture mirrors the proposal, compressed for oral delivery; rehearse to 80% of the slot
  • AV aids are cognitive scaffolding, not scripts — CRAP, 30-point minimum, 60-30-10
  • Communication spans verbal, vocal and non-verbal; opening and closing carry disproportionate weight
  • Queries are classified, then answered with IDEAL; back-pocket slides pre-empt the predictable ones
  • Never fabricate an answer — integrity is being tested alongside knowledge
  • Methodology and novelty are up to 45% of the rubric; presentation quality is at most 10
