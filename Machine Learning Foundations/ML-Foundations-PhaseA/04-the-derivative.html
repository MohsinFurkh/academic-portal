The Derivative from First Principles

A derivative is a limit of slopes. That single sentence, unpacked carefully, gives you tangent lines, local linear approximation, and the entire mechanism by which a neural network learns.

By the end you can

  • Derive \(f'(x)\) from the difference-quotient definition, for polynomials and \(1/x\)
  • Explain what the derivative means geometrically, physically, and as a linear approximation
  • Prove that differentiability implies continuity, and give a counterexample to the converse
  • Read gradient descent as 'walk downhill using the derivative'
  • Verify any derivative you compute against a numerical estimate

Assumed

  • Lecture 3: limits, indeterminate forms, continuity
  • Expanding binomials such as \((x+h)^3\)

1The question

Here is the problem gradient descent has to solve. You are standing at a point \(w\) on a loss curve \(L(w)\). You want to move downhill. Which way, and how far?

"Which way" is a question about slope. And slope is easy for a straight line — rise over run — but a loss curve is not straight. Its steepness changes from point to point. So we need a notion of slope at a single point, and that phrase is initially meaningless: a slope needs two points.

Intuition

Zoom in far enough on a smooth curve and it looks straight. Photograph a one-metre stretch of the Earth's surface and you cannot see the curvature. The derivative is the slope of that straight-looking thing — the slope you would measure if you could zoom in infinitely. Limits are the tool that lets us say "infinitely" without hand-waving.

2Secants converging to a tangent

Pick a point \(x\) on the curve \(y=f(x)\), and a second point a distance \(h\) away. Two points determine a line — a secant. Its slope is the ordinary rise-over-run:

\[m_{\text{sec}}(h) = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{(x+h)-x} = \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.\]

This expression is called the difference quotient, and it is the single most important formula in this half of the course. Now shrink \(h\). The second point slides towards the first, and the secant pivots towards the tangent. At \(h=0\) the formula reads \(\tfrac00\) and dies — which is exactly the situation limits were built for.

3The definition

Definition — the derivative

The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) is

\[ f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}, \]

provided this limit exists. When it does, \(f\) is said to be differentiable at \(x\).

Equivalent notations, all meaning the same thing: \(\;f'(x)\), \(\;\dfrac{df}{dx}\), \(\;\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), \(\;Df(x)\), \(\;\dot f\) (physics, for time derivatives).

Three readings of the same object, and you should be able to move between them freely:

  • Geometric. The slope of the tangent line to the graph at \(x\).
  • Physical. The instantaneous rate of change of \(f\) with respect to \(x\). If \(f\) is position and \(x\) is time, \(f'\) is velocity.
  • Approximation-theoretic. The number \(m\) that makes \(f(x+h)\approx f(x)+mh\) the best possible straight-line approximation near \(x\). This is the reading that generalises to many variables, and the one that matters most for us.
Computing f′(x) for f(x) = x², from the definition0 / 4
\[f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}\]
Write down the definition. Substitute \(f(t)=t^2\) at \(t=x+h\) and \(t=x\). Resist any urge to use a rule — we are deriving the rule.
\[(x+h)^2 - x^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2\]
Expand and cancel. The \(x^2\) terms cancel — they must, because the numerator has to vanish as \(h\to0\) for the quotient to have any chance of converging. Every surviving term contains at least one factor of \(h\).
\[\frac{2xh+h^2}{h} = \frac{h(2x+h)}{h} = 2x + h \qquad (h \neq 0)\]
Factor out h and cancel it. This is legal because the limit never evaluates at \(h=0\) — the \(0<|h|\) clause from Lecture 3. This cancellation is the whole trick: it converts an indeterminate \(\tfrac00\) into something we can substitute into.
\[f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}(2x+h) = 2x\]
Take the limit. \(2x+h\) is continuous in \(h\), so the limit is its value at \(h=0\), namely \(2x\). Sanity check: at \(x=1\), \(f'(1)=2\) — and the interactive above, with \(x=1\), converges to 2. At \(x=0\) the slope is 0, matching the flat bottom of the parabola. At \(x=-1\) it is \(-2\), negative because the curve is falling. All three agree with the picture.

Notice the last line of step 3: the secant slope is exactly \(2x+h\). So the error between the secant slope and the true derivative is exactly \(h\) — which is what the readout in the figure reports, and which is the \(O(h)\) truncation error we predicted numerically in Lecture 3. The theory and the arithmetic agree, which is the kind of cross-check you should demand of yourself constantly.

Do this one yourself

Compute \(f'(x)\) from the definition for \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\), with \(x\neq0\).

Hint: the numerator \(\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac1x\) needs a common denominator before anything cancels.

Start with the definition:

\[ f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1}{h}\left(\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac{1}{x}\right). \]

Common denominator \(x(x+h)\):

\[ \frac{1}{x+h}-\frac{1}{x} = \frac{x - (x+h)}{x(x+h)} = \frac{-h}{x(x+h)}. \]

Divide by \(h\) — i.e. multiply by \(1/h\), and cancel the \(h\) (legal, \(h\neq0\)):

\[ \frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{-h}{x(x+h)} = \frac{-1}{x(x+h)}. \]

Take the limit. The expression is continuous in \(h\) near 0 provided \(x\neq0\):

\[ f'(x) = \frac{-1}{x\cdot x} = -\frac{1}{x^2}. \]

Sanity checks. The sign is always negative, matching the fact that \(1/x\) is decreasing on both branches. At \(x=1\), \(f'(1)=-1\); at \(x=2\), \(f'(2)=-1/4\) — the curve flattens as \(x\) grows, which is what the graph shows. And a numerical check at \(x=2\) with \(h=10^{-6}\): \(\frac{1/2.000001 - 1/2}{10^{-6}} \approx -0.2499998\) ✓.

4The derivative is itself a function

We wrote \(f'(x)=2x\), not "the derivative is 2". The derivative assigns a slope to every point in the domain — it is a new function, built from the old one. This is why you can differentiate again, getting \(f''\), and again.

5Differentiability is stronger than continuity

Theorem — differentiable ⟹ continuous

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(c\).

Proof. We must show \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)\), equivalently \(\lim_{x\to c}\big[f(x)-f(c)\big]=0\). For \(x\neq c\) write the identity

\[ f(x)-f(c) = \frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\cdot (x-c), \]

which is legitimate because we multiplied and divided by the nonzero quantity \(x-c\). Now take the limit as \(x\to c\). The first factor tends to \(f'(c)\) — that is precisely the hypothesis, in its \(\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\) form. The second factor tends to \(0\). By the product law for limits, which applies because both limits exist,

\[ \lim_{x\to c}\big[f(x)-f(c)\big] = f'(c)\cdot 0 = 0. \qquad\blacksquare\]

The converse is false, and the counterexample is one you will meet every day.

Both secants are drawn at distance h from the origin. Shrink h as far as you like: the right secant sits at slope +1 forever and the left at −1 forever. They never approach a common value, so the limit defining f′(0) does not exist. The function is perfectly continuous — you can draw it without lifting the pen — but it has a corner, and a corner has no single tangent.
Where this shows up in ML

\(|x|\) is the shape of ReLU's kink, and this figure is the reason ReLU needs a subgradient convention. At \(x=0\) the set of valid slopes is the whole interval \([-1,1]\) for \(|x|\), or \([0,1]\) for \(\max(0,x)\); frameworks simply pick one (PyTorch picks 0) and carry on. Three consequences worth carrying with you:

  • The choice is arbitrary but harmless, because a randomly-initialised network hits exactly \(x=0\) with probability zero.
  • Convergence theorems for gradient descent that assume differentiability do not literally apply to ReLU networks. The theory that does apply — subgradient methods — gives weaker guarantees. This is a real gap between practice and theory, not a technicality that has been resolved.
  • L1 regularisation \(\lambda\|\vw\|_1\) has the same kink at zero, and that kink is exactly why L1 produces sparse solutions while L2 does not. We prove that in Phase B.

6Linear approximation: the reading that generalises

Rearrange the definition. For small \(h\),

\[\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \approx f'(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h\,}\]

This is the first-order Taylor approximation, and it is the single most useful consequence of the derivative. It says: to predict how \(f\) responds to a small nudge, multiply the nudge by the derivative.

Now read gradient descent off it directly. We want to decrease \(f\). Choose the step \(h=-\eta f'(x)\) for some small \(\eta>0\). Then

\[f\big(x - \eta f'(x)\big) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\cdot\big(-\eta f'(x)\big) = f(x) - \eta\,\big(f'(x)\big)^2.\]

Since \(\eta>0\) and \((f'(x))^2 \ge 0\), the predicted new value is never larger than the old one, and is strictly smaller whenever \(f'(x)\neq0\). That is the entire justification for the update rule

\[x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\, f'(x_t).\]

Common trap

The word "approximately" is load-bearing. The guarantee holds for the linear approximation, which is only accurate for small \(h\). If \(\eta\) is too large the step leaves the region where the approximation is valid and the loss can increase — which is exactly what a diverging training run looks like. Lecture 6 quantifies the error term, and Lecture 7 turns that into an actual bound on \(\eta\).

8Exercises

★

Compute from the definition (no rules) and simplify fully:

(a) \(f(x)=3x+7\)    (b) \(f(x)=x^3\)    (c) \(f(x)=\sqrt{x}\), for \(x>0\)

(a) \(\frac{3(x+h)+7-(3x+7)}{h}=\frac{3h}{h}=3\), for every \(h\neq0\). So \(f'(x)=3\). The slope of a line is constant and equals its coefficient — reassuring, and a good check that the definition is behaving.

(b) \((x+h)^3 = x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3\), so the numerator is \(3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3\). Factor \(h\): \(h(3x^2+3xh+h^2)\). Divide:

\[\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=3x^2+3xh+h^2 \xrightarrow{\;h\to0\;} 3x^2.\]

So \(f'(x)=3x^2\).

(c) \(\tfrac00\) with a root — use the conjugate, as in Lecture 3:

\[\frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt x}{h}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x} =\frac{(x+h)-x}{h(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x)}=\frac{h}{h(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}.\]

Let \(h\to0\): \(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\).

Note this blows up as \(x\to0^+\): \(\sqrt x\) has a vertical tangent at the origin, so it is continuous there but not differentiable — a second way the converse of the theorem fails, different from the corner in \(|x|\).

★★

Let \(f(x)=x^2\). Using only the definition, prove the following exact statement:

\[\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} - f'(x) = h \quad \text{for every } h \neq 0.\]

Then explain what this says about the truncation error of the forward difference, and reconcile it with the \(O(h)\) claim from Lecture 3.

From the derivation in the lecture, the difference quotient is exactly \(2x+h\) for every \(h\neq0\), and \(f'(x)=2x\). Subtracting: \((2x+h)-2x=h\). ∎ (No limit was needed — the identity is exact.)

Interpretation. The forward-difference estimate of \(f'(x)\) overshoots by exactly \(h\). So the truncation error is not merely \(O(h)\), it is precisely \(h\) — the constant is 1.

Reconciling with Lecture 3. The general claim was truncation error \(\approx \frac{f''(x)}{2}h\), which comes from the Taylor expansion \(f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)h+\frac{f''(x)}{2}h^2+\cdots\). Here \(f''(x)=2\), so \(\frac{f''}{2}h = h\) ✓, and there are no higher terms because \(x^2\) is exactly quadratic. The general formula reproduces the exact answer, which is a good sign that Lecture 6's machinery is trustworthy.

★★

Define \[ f(x)=\begin{cases} x^2, & x \le 1\\ ax+b, & x>1.\end{cases}\]

Find \(a\) and \(b\) so that \(f\) is differentiable at \(x=1\). Explain why continuity alone is not enough, and sketch what goes wrong if you only impose continuity.

Continuity at 1 requires the two pieces to meet: \(1^2 = a(1)+b\), i.e. \(a+b=1\). That is one equation and two unknowns — a whole family of continuous choices.

Differentiability requires the two one-sided derivatives to agree, exactly as in the \(|x|\) figure. From the left, \(\frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2x\), so the left slope at 1 is \(2\). From the right, \(\frac{d}{dx}(ax+b)=a\). Agreement forces \(a=2\), and then \(b=1-a=-1\).

So \(a=2,\ b=-1\), giving \(f(x)=2x-1\) for \(x>1\) — which is precisely the tangent line to \(x^2\) at \(x=1\). That is the geometric content of the answer: the only line that can be glued on smoothly is the tangent.

What continuity alone gives you. Any \((a,1-a)\) joins the pieces without a gap, but for \(a\neq2\) the graph has a corner at \(x=1\), just like \(|x|\) at 0. It is continuous, so you could draw it without lifting the pen, but there is no single tangent and \(f'(1)\) does not exist. Differentiability is "continuous and smooth"; continuity is only the first half.

★★★

Coding + analysis. Implement gradient descent for \(f(w)=w^4-3w^2+w+4\), whose derivative is \(f'(w)=4w^3-6w+1\).

  1. Run from \(w_0=1.8\) and from \(w_0=-1.8\) with \(\eta=0.05\), 200 steps. Report the two limits and the two final loss values. What does the difference tell you?
  2. Sweep \(\eta \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.13, 0.20\}\) from \(w_0=1.8\). Classify each as converging, oscillating, or diverging.
  3. The critical learning rate for a quadratic \(f(w)=\tfrac{a}{2}w^2\) is \(\eta<2/a\). Estimate \(f''\) at the minimum you found and predict the threshold. Does it match your sweep?
import numpy as np
f  = lambda w: w**4 - 3*w**2 + w + 4
fp = lambda w: 4*w**3 - 6*w + 1

def gd(w0, eta, n=200):
    w = w0; hist=[w]
    for _ in range(n):
        w = w - eta*fp(w)
        if not np.isfinite(w) or abs(w) > 1e6: return np.nan, hist
        hist.append(w)
    return w, hist

for w0 in (1.8, -1.8):
    w, _ = gd(w0, 0.05); print(f"w0={w0:+.1f} -> w={w:.6f}, f={f(w):.6f}")
for eta in (0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.13, 0.20):
    w, h = gd(1.8, eta); print(f"eta={eta:.2f} -> {w}")

1. The critical points solve \(4w^3-6w+1=0\), whose roots are \(w\approx-1.30084,\ 0.16994,\ 1.13090\). From \(w_0=1.8\) descent lands at \(w\approx1.13090\) with \(f\approx2.9298\). From \(w_0=-1.8\) it lands at \(w\approx-1.30084\) with \(f\approx0.4861\). (The middle root has \(f''<0\) — it is the local maximum between the two wells, and descent never lands there.)

Same algorithm, same learning rate, different answers — and the left basin is the better one. This is non-convexity: gradient descent finds a local minimum, determined entirely by where you start. Every claim about deep learning optimisation lives with this fact. It is also why initialisation is a research topic and why people run multiple seeds.

2. \(\eta=0.01\) and \(0.05\): smooth convergence. \(\eta=0.10\): converges but with visible oscillation, overshooting the minimum and coming back. \(\eta=0.13\): oscillates without settling, or settles very slowly. \(\eta=0.20\): diverges to overflow within a few dozen steps.

3. \(f''(w)=12w^2-6\). At \(w^\star\approx1.13090\), \(f''\approx 12(1.2789)-6 \approx 9.347\). The quadratic-model threshold is \(\eta_{\text{crit}} = 2/f''(w^\star) \approx 0.214\).

Our sweep started diverging somewhere between \(0.13\) and \(0.20\), a bit earlier than \(0.213\) predicts. Both facts are informative: the order of magnitude is right, confirming the theory is the correct one, and the discrepancy is real because \(f\) is only approximately quadratic near the minimum — far from it, the quartic term dominates and \(f''\) is larger, so the effective threshold along the trajectory is tighter than the one computed at the minimum alone.

The general principle, which you will meet again as "the edge of stability": stable step sizes are governed by the largest curvature encountered, not the curvature at the destination. In many dimensions \(f''\) becomes the Hessian and the threshold becomes \(\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}\) — Lecture 11 and Lecture 12.
