Phase A · The lectures

I — The Language of Functions
01
What a Function Really Is
Every model you will ever train is a function. Before we can talk about learning one, we have to be ruthless about what the word means — and the part almost everyone skips is that a function is not just a formula.
  • State the formal definition of a function and say why \(\text{domain}\) is part of the object, not an afterthought
  • Compute the domain of an expression and justify each exclusion
  • Decide whether a function is injective, surjective, bijective — and therefore invertible
✓
02
Exponentials and Logarithms
Machine learning is addicted to logarithms. Log-likelihood, cross-entropy, logits, log-sum-exp, perplexity, KL divergence. This lecture builds the logarithm from its definition so that every one of those is transparent — and so that you never again wonder what log(−2) is.
  • Prove that \(a^x > 0\) for every real \(x\), and derive the domain of \(\log_b\) from it
  • Derive all four logarithm laws from the exponent laws rather than memorising them
  • Explain why \(e\) is the natural base, in terms of the derivative
✓
03
Limits and Continuity
A limit answers a question the function itself refuses to: what value is this heading towards at a point where it may not even be defined? Every derivative, every integral, and every convergence guarantee in optimisation is built on this one idea.
  • State what \(\lim_{x\to c} f(x) = L\) means, both informally and in \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) form
  • Compute limits by factoring, and explain why \(\tfrac00\) is indeterminate rather than undefined
  • Distinguish 'the limit exists' from 'the function is defined' from 'the function is continuous'
✓
II — Calculus of One Variable
04
The Derivative from First Principles
A derivative is a limit of slopes. That single sentence, unpacked carefully, gives you tangent lines, local linear approximation, and the entire mechanism by which a neural network learns.
  • Derive \(f'(x)\) from the difference-quotient definition, for polynomials and \(1/x\)
  • Explain what the derivative means geometrically, physically, and as a linear approximation
  • Prove that differentiability implies continuity, and give a counterexample to the converse
✓
05
Differentiation Rules and the Chain Rule
Four rules replace every difference quotient you would otherwise have to compute. The last of them — the chain rule — is not merely convenient: it is the entire mathematical content of backpropagation, and therefore of how every neural network ever trained was trained.
  • Derive the sum, product, quotient and chain rules from the definition
  • Prove \(\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac1x\), closing the debt from Lecture 2
  • Differentiate a long composition by peeling one layer at a time
✓
06
Taylor Series and Local Approximation
The tangent line is the best straight-line approximation to a curve. Taylor's theorem says what the best parabola is, the best cubic, and exactly how wrong each of them is — which is where second-order optimisers, learning-rate bounds, and half of numerical analysis come from.
  • Construct the degree-\(n\) Taylor polynomial by matching derivatives at a point
  • State Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder and use it to bound an approximation error
  • Derive the series for \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), \(\ln(1+x)\), \(\tfrac1{1-x}\)
✓
07
Optimisation in One Dimension
Training a model is minimising a function. This lecture assembles the complete one-dimensional theory — critical points, the second-derivative test, and convexity — so that when we go to a million dimensions in Lecture 12, only the bookkeeping changes.
  • Find and classify critical points using the first and second derivative tests
  • State and use the definition of a convex function, and prove \(f''\ge0 \Rightarrow\) convex
  • Explain precisely what convexity buys you: every local minimum is global
✓
III — Linear Algebra
08
Vectors, Span, and Geometry
A data point is a vector. A model's weights are a vector. A gradient is a vector. This lecture builds the object and its geometry — length, angle, projection — because every later result in the course is a geometric statement wearing algebraic clothes.
  • State the vector space axioms and check them for \(\RR^d\)
  • Compute a span and decide whether a set of vectors is linearly independent
  • Derive \(\vu\cdot\vv = \|\vu\|\|\vv\|\cos\theta\) from the law of cosines
✓
09
Matrices as Linear Maps
A matrix is not a grid of numbers that you multiply by a strange rule. It is a linear function, written down. Once you see that, matrix multiplication stops being arbitrary — it is composition, and the shape rules are just Lecture 1's requirement that the codomain of one map match the domain of the next.
  • Define a linear map and prove every such map \(\RR^n\to\RR^m\) is a matrix
  • Read \(\mA\vx\) both as a stack of dot products and as a combination of columns
  • Derive the matrix multiplication formula from composition
✓
10
Least Squares and the Normal Equations
Real data gives you more equations than unknowns, and no exact solution. Least squares is the principled answer: project the target onto what your model can actually reach. This is linear regression, derived twice — geometrically and by calculus — and shown to be the same theorem.
  • Explain why \(\mX\vw=\vy\) is usually unsolvable and what we should ask instead
  • Derive the normal equations \(\mX^\top\mX\vw = \mX^\top\vy\) from orthogonality
  • Derive the same equations by minimising \(\|\mX\vw-\vy\|^2\)
✓
11
Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and Quadratic Forms
Some directions survive a linear map unchanged except for scaling. Finding them turns a matrix into a list of numbers, turns curvature into geometry, and explains — precisely — why gradient descent zig-zags, why learning rates have a ceiling, and what PCA is doing.
  • Define eigenvalues and eigenvectors and compute them for \(2\times2\) matrices by hand
  • State the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices and use the diagonalisation \(\mA=\mQ\Lambda\mQ^\top\)
  • Classify a quadratic form as positive definite, semi-definite or indefinite
✓
IV — Multivariable Calculus
12
Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians
Calculus in one variable, plus linear algebra, equals the calculus that machine learning actually runs on. The gradient points uphill, the Jacobian is a derivative that is a matrix, the Hessian is curvature — and the multivariable chain rule is backpropagation.
  • Compute partial derivatives and assemble a gradient, with correct shapes
  • Prove that \(\grad f\) is the direction of steepest ascent and is perpendicular to level sets
  • Use the multivariable chain rule, and see it as the backpropagation recursion
✓
V — Probability and Statistics
13
Random Variables, Expectation, and Variance
Every loss you minimise is an expectation you cannot compute, estimated from a sample. Understanding what that sentence means — and how wrong the estimate can be — is the difference between running experiments and understanding them.
  • State the probability axioms and define a random variable properly
  • Prove linearity of expectation, and use it without assuming independence
  • Compute variance and covariance, and distinguish independent from uncorrelated
✓
14
Bayes, Distributions, and Maximum Likelihood
The last lecture of Phase A, and the one that ties it together. Maximum likelihood is a single principle from which squared error, cross-entropy, ridge and LASSO all fall out — and Bayes' theorem is the reason a 99%-accurate test can be wrong most of the time.
  • Derive Bayes' theorem and use it to resolve the base-rate fallacy quantitatively
  • Write down a likelihood, take its log, and maximise it
  • Derive the MLE for Bernoulli, Gaussian, linear regression and logistic regression
✓

What you should NOT study yet

Being specific about this saves more time than any study tip. Until Phase A is finished, avoid:

  • Transformers, LLMs, and attention. They are compositions of linear maps and softmaxes differentiated by the chain rule. Every one of those words is a Phase A topic. Reading about them now buys vocabulary, not understanding.
  • PyTorch and TensorFlow tutorials. loss.backward() will teach you nothing until you have written backpropagation by hand. Phase D, not before.
  • Kaggle competitions. They reward pipeline-assembly and hyperparameter luck. That is a real skill, but it is not the one you said you wanted.
  • Measure-theoretic probability. Genuinely necessary for research-level theory, genuinely useless before you are fluent with ordinary random variables. It belongs after Phase C.
  • Proof-heavy real analysis. You need the ideas of limits and continuity, which Lecture 3 gives you. You do not yet need epsilon-delta proofs of the Heine–Borel theorem.
