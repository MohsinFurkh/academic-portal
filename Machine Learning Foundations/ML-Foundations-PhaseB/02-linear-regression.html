Linear Regression, Properly

Phase A gave you the normal equations. This lecture turns them into a model you can actually use — basis functions, feature design, coefficient interpretation, residual diagnostics — and is honest about the four ways a linear fit lies to you.

By the end you can

  • Explain why 'linear' means linear in the parameters, not in the inputs
  • Build design matrices with polynomial, RBF and categorical features
  • Choose between the closed form and an iterative solver from \(n\) and \(d\)
  • Interpret coefficients correctly — and know when interpretation is meaningless
  • Read a residual plot and diagnose the assumption it violates

Assumed

  • Phase A, Lecture 10: normal equations, projection, the pseudoinverse
  • Phase A, Lecture 11: conditioning, why lstsq beats inv
  • Phase B, Lecture 1: train/test discipline

1What we already have

Phase A Lecture 10 derived, twice, the solution to

\[\hat{\vw} = \argmin_{\vw}\ \|\mX\vw-\vy\|^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mX^\top\mX\,\hat{\vw} = \mX^\top\vy,\]

geometrically as the projection of \(\vy\) onto the column space of \(\mX\), and by calculus as the unique minimiser of a strictly convex quadratic. Nothing about that derivation is repeated here. What we do now is make it a model: decide what goes into \(\mX\), what the numbers coming out mean, and how to tell when the whole thing is inappropriate.

2Linear in the parameters, not in the inputs

The single most common misconception about linear regression is that it can only fit straight lines. It cannot fit anything but a straight line in \(\vw\) — and that is a completely different restriction.

Definition — basis functions

Choose functions \(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_m:\mathcal{X}\to\RR\) and model

\[ f(\vx) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\,\phi_j(\vx) = \vw^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\vx). \]

The \(\phi_j\) are basis functions or features. They may be arbitrarily nonlinear in \(\vx\). Build the design matrix \(\mX_{ij}=\phi_j(\vx_i)\) and everything from Phase A Lecture 10 applies unchanged, because the objective is still quadratic in \(\vw\).

So a "linear" model can fit a sine wave, a step function, or a 12th-degree polynomial. What it cannot do is learn the basis — you choose \(\boldsymbol\phi\) up front, and that choice is the inductive bias. (Learning the basis is precisely what a neural network does, and it is the one structural difference between a linear model and a deep one.)

3Designing the features

Four constructions cover most of what you will need.

  • Polynomials. \(1, x, x^2, \dots\). Cheap and familiar, but the columns become desperately collinear (\(x^7\) and \(x^9\) are nearly proportional over a bounded interval), so the design matrix is ill-conditioned — Phase A Lecture 10's \(2.8\times10^6\) condition number. They also extrapolate disastrously. Use orthogonal polynomials (Legendre, Chebyshev) if you must go past degree 4.
  • Gaussian radial basis functions. \(\phi_j(x)=\exp(-\|x-c_j\|^2/2s^2)\), centred on a grid or on data points. Local: each coefficient affects one region, so a bad fit in one place cannot corrupt another. Two hyperparameters — the number of centres and the width \(s\) — and the width matters more than the count.
  • Categorical features. A category with \(K\) levels becomes \(K-1\) indicator columns, not \(K\). Including all \(K\) plus an intercept makes the columns sum to the intercept column, giving exact collinearity and a singular \(\mX^\top\mX\) — the dummy variable trap. The omitted level becomes the baseline that all other coefficients are measured against.
  • Interactions. Adding \(x_1x_2\) lets the effect of one feature depend on another. Note that this is the only way a linear model can express "it depends" — without an explicit interaction term, the model is additive by construction, and no amount of data will teach it otherwise.
Check yourself
You one-hot encode a colour feature with levels {red, green, blue} into three columns and also keep an intercept. What happens?
Correct. Correct. Every row has exactly one colour, so \(\text{red}+\text{green}+\text{blue}=1=\) intercept for every row. That is an exact linear dependence among the columns, so \(\rank(\mX)<d\) and \(\mX^\top\mX\) is singular (Phase A Lecture 9).

Consequences, all the same fact: inv fails or returns garbage; the coefficients are not identifiable (you can add \(c\) to the intercept and subtract \(c\) from all three colour coefficients with no change to any prediction); and interpreting any individual coefficient is meaningless.

Predictions are still fine — the projection onto the column space is unique regardless. And note that ridge fixes this silently, which is why practitioners who always regularise rarely meet the trap. That is worth knowing, because it also means ridge is quietly making an arbitrary choice among the equivalent parameterisations for you: it picks the minimum-norm one.
Not quite. One-hot encoding is standard, but with \(K-1\) columns when an intercept is present (drop_first=True in pandas, drop='first' in scikit-learn's OneHotEncoder). With all \(K\) and an intercept, the design is rank-deficient. Try it: build the matrix and print np.linalg.matrix_rank.
Not quite. Encoding a feature does not change what the model predicts — \(y\) is still the target. The issue is purely a rank deficiency in the design matrix, which affects the uniqueness of the coefficients, not what is being predicted.

4Closed form or iterate?

Both solve the same problem. The choice is about cost and conditioning.

\[\begin{array}{lll} \textbf{method} & \textbf{cost} & \textbf{use when} \\[3pt] \text{normal equations, } (\mX^\top\mX)^{-1}\mX^\top\vy & O(nd^2 + d^3) & \text{never — see below} \\[2pt] \texttt{np.linalg.solve} \text{ on } \mX^\top\mX & O(nd^2 + d^3) & d \text{ small, } \mX \text{ well conditioned} \\[2pt] \text{QR or SVD (} \texttt{lstsq} \text{)} & O(nd^2) & \textbf{the default} \\[2pt] \text{gradient descent} & O(nd) \text{ per step} & d \text{ large, or } n \text{ too large for memory} \\[2pt] \text{SGD} & O(Bd) \text{ per step} & n \text{ enormous, or streaming} \end{array}\]

Practical engineering note

The rule from Phase A Lecture 10 stands: never form \((\mX^\top\mX)^{-1}\) explicitly. Forming \(\mX^\top\mX\) squares the condition number, so a design matrix with \(\kappa=10^{8}\) — very ordinary for polynomial features — produces a Gram matrix with \(\kappa=10^{16}\), which is all of float64's precision. lstsq works on \(\mX\) directly via the SVD and never builds that product. The extra cost is a small constant factor; the accuracy difference can be total.

The crossover to iterative methods is roughly at \(d\sim10^4\): above that, \(O(d^3)\) becomes unaffordable and \(O(nd)\) per gradient step wins. It also wins whenever \(\mX\) does not fit in memory, which is the usual situation at scale. Lecture 03 is about doing that well.

5Reading the coefficients

A fitted \(\hat w_j\) says: holding every other feature fixed, a one-unit increase in feature \(j\) is associated with a \(\hat w_j\) change in the prediction. Every clause of that sentence is load-bearing.

  • "One-unit" — so coefficients are in units of \(y\) per unit of \(x_j\), and comparing \(\hat w_1=0.3\) against \(\hat w_2=45\) is meaningless unless the features share a scale. Standardise (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation, fit on the training split only) and the coefficients become comparable: change per standard deviation.
  • "Holding every other feature fixed" — which may be physically impossible. If your features are age and years_of_experience, no intervention increases one while holding the other fixed, and the coefficient describes a comparison that never occurs.
  • "Associated with" — not "causes". Regression coefficients are conditional associations in the observed data. Adding or removing a feature can flip the sign of another's coefficient (Simpson's paradox), and no amount of fit quality converts association into causation.
  • Collinearity destroys interpretation entirely. When features are near-dependent the coefficients have enormous variance and can be wildly wrong individually while predicting perfectly in combination — you saw exactly this in Phase A Lecture 10's ridge figure.

6Residual diagnostics: letting the model tell you what is wrong

Least squares is the MLE under four assumptions (Phase A Lecture 14, and Lecture 04 here): the mean is linear in the features, the noise has constant variance, the noise is independent across observations, and — for inference, though not for the point estimate — the noise is Gaussian. Each assumption leaves a distinct fingerprint in the residuals \(\vr = \vy - \mX\hat{\vw}\) when it fails.

Common trap

Note the last line of the "curvature" case. \(R^2\) stays respectable while the model is systematically wrong. Three things worth knowing about \(R^2 = 1 - \frac{\|\vr\|^2}{\|\vy-\bar y\|^2}\):

  • It never decreases when you add a feature, even a column of pure noise, because the column space grew and the projection can only get closer. Adjusted \(R^2\) penalises this; cross-validated \(R^2\) is better still.
  • It is not comparable across datasets. \(R^2=0.3\) may be outstanding for individual human behaviour and appalling for a physical measurement, because the denominator is the variance of that \(y\).
  • It says nothing about whether the functional form is right, as the figure shows. Look at the residuals.

7Exercises

★

You fit \(\text{price} = w_0 + w_1\cdot\text{area}_{m^2} + w_2\cdot\text{bedrooms}\) and obtain \(\hat w_1 = 1800\), \(\hat w_2 = 12000\), with price in pounds.

  1. State in words what each coefficient means, with units.
  2. Is bedrooms "more important" than area? Justify your answer.
  3. You refit with area in square feet instead. What happens to \(\hat w_1\)? To \(R^2\)? To predictions?

1. \(\hat w_1 = 1800\) £/m²: holding bedroom count fixed, one additional square metre is associated with £1800 more. \(\hat w_2 = 12000\) £/bedroom: holding area fixed, one additional bedroom is associated with £12 000 more. Note that second one is already suspicious — adding a bedroom while holding total area constant means subdividing the space, which is a different intervention from what most people picture.

2. You cannot tell from these numbers. They are in different units, so their magnitudes are not comparable — 12000 > 1800 says nothing. The comparable quantity is the change per standard deviation: \(\hat w_j \times \text{sd}(x_j)\). If area has sd 50 m² and bedrooms has sd 1.1, then area contributes \(1800\times50 = £90{,}000\) per sd and bedrooms \(12000\times1.1 = £13{,}200\) per sd — area matters roughly seven times more. Standardising the features before fitting produces these numbers directly.

3. One square foot is \(0.0929\) m², so \(\hat w_1^{\text{new}} = 1800\times0.0929 = 167.2\) £/ft². \(R^2\), residuals, fitted values and predictions are all completely unchanged, because rescaling a column is an invertible linear change of basis that leaves the column space — and therefore the projection — identical (Phase A Lecture 9).

This is worth internalising: OLS is equivariant to rescaling features. Ridge and LASSO are not, because the penalty \(\|\vw\|^2\) depends on the units. That is precisely why standardisation is mandatory before regularising and merely convenient before plain OLS.

★★

Show that if \(\mX\) contains a column of ones (an intercept), then the least-squares residuals satisfy \(\sum_i r_i = 0\), and hence the fitted line passes through \((\bar{\vx}, \bar y)\).

Then show \(R^2 = \operatorname{corr}(\vy,\hat{\vy})^2\) for a model with an intercept, and explain why that identity makes \(R^2\) blind to a systematic bias in the predictions.

Residuals sum to zero. The normal equations are \(\mX^\top\vr = \vzero\), i.e. the residual is orthogonal to every column. Applying this to the column of ones \(\mathbf{1}\):

\[\mathbf{1}^\top\vr = \sum_i r_i = 0. \qquad\blacksquare\]

Dividing by \(n\): \(\bar y - \overline{\hat y} = 0\), so the mean fitted value equals the mean observed value, and by linearity \(\hat y(\bar{\vx}) = \bar{\vx}^\top\hat{\vw} = \bar y\). ∎

Note this holds only because of the intercept column. Drop it and residuals need not sum to zero — and \(R^2\) can then go negative.

\(R^2 = \operatorname{corr}(\vy,\hat{\vy})^2\). Write \(\tilde{\vy}=\vy-\bar y\mathbf{1}\) and \(\tilde{\hat{\vy}}=\hat{\vy}-\bar y\mathbf{1}\) (legitimate since the means agree). Since \(\vr\perp\mathcal{C}(\mX)\) and \(\hat{\vy}\in\mathcal{C}(\mX)\), Pythagoras gives \(\|\tilde{\vy}\|^2 = \|\tilde{\hat{\vy}}\|^2 + \|\vr\|^2\). Also \(\tilde{\vy}^\top\tilde{\hat{\vy}} = (\tilde{\hat{\vy}}+\vr)^\top\tilde{\hat{\vy}} = \|\tilde{\hat{\vy}}\|^2\), using \(\vr^\top\hat{\vy}=0\). Therefore

\[\operatorname{corr}(\vy,\hat{\vy})^2 = \frac{(\tilde{\vy}^\top\tilde{\hat{\vy}})^2} {\|\tilde{\vy}\|^2\|\tilde{\hat{\vy}}\|^2} = \frac{\|\tilde{\hat{\vy}}\|^4}{\|\tilde{\vy}\|^2\|\tilde{\hat{\vy}}\|^2} = \frac{\|\tilde{\hat{\vy}}\|^2}{\|\tilde{\vy}\|^2} = 1-\frac{\|\vr\|^2}{\|\tilde{\vy}\|^2} = R^2. \qquad\blacksquare\]

Why this makes \(R^2\) blind to bias. Correlation is invariant to affine transformation of either argument: \(\operatorname{corr}(\vy, a\hat{\vy}+b) = \operatorname{corr}(\vy,\hat{\vy})\) for any \(a>0\). So a model that predicts half the true value plus a constant offset has the same correlation — and, on a fresh dataset where the intercept identity no longer holds, can report a high correlation-squared while every prediction is badly miscalibrated.

The practical warning: on training data with an intercept the two definitions of \(R^2\) coincide, so nothing goes wrong. On test data they diverge, and reporting \(\operatorname{corr}(y_{\text{test}},\hat y_{\text{test}})^2\) — which many people do, sometimes without realising — hides systematic over- or under-prediction. Report test MSE or the proper \(1-\text{SS}_{\text{res}}/\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}\) computed with the training mean, and plot predicted against actual with a \(y=x\) line.

★★

Fit a Gaussian RBF model \(f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_j\exp(-(x-c_j)^2/2s^2)\).

  1. Which matters more for the fit, \(m\) or \(s\)? Design an experiment to find out.
  2. What happens when \(s\) is much smaller than the spacing between centres? Much larger?
  3. Why does this basis behave better than polynomials outside the range of the data?

1. Grid over \(m\in\{5,10,20,40\}\) and \(s\in\{0.05,0.2,0.5,2\}\) with centres on an even grid, evaluating held-out MSE for each cell. You will find test error varies by orders of magnitude across \(s\) and comparatively little across \(m\) once \(m\) is large enough to tile the range. The width dominates.

The reason is that \(s\) controls the effective capacity — how smooth the achievable functions are — while \(m\) mostly controls resolution. Adding more narrow bumps than the data can support just produces near-duplicate columns, which regularisation handles. Adding a wrong width cannot be fixed by anything downstream.

2. \(s\) much smaller than the spacing: the bumps do not overlap, so between centres every basis function is essentially zero and the fit collapses to zero there — a row of spikes on a flat floor. The columns of \(\mX\) become nearly orthogonal and the model interpolates each centre independently, i.e. it memorises. \(s\) much larger than the range: every bump is nearly constant across the data, so all columns are nearly identical, \(\mX^\top\mX\) is horribly ill-conditioned, and the model can only express something close to a constant. Underfitting, plus numerical trouble.

A reasonable default is \(s\) equal to one to two times the centre spacing, then tuned by cross-validation.

3. Because the basis is local and bounded. Outside the range of the centres, every \(\phi_j(x)\to0\), so \(f(x)\to0\) — the model returns to its baseline and says, in effect, "no information here". A polynomial basis is global and unbounded: \(x^{12}\) grows without limit, so the coefficients fitted to interior data produce enormous extrapolated values, as you saw in Phase A Lecture 14's capstone (a fit reaching \(9\times10^4\) on data of magnitude 2).

The general principle: a model extrapolates according to the shape of its basis, not according to the data. Reverting to zero is at least a predictable failure. This is also why Gaussian-process regression — the Bayesian relative of RBF models — reverts to the prior mean with growing uncertainty away from the data, which is exactly the honest behaviour you want.

★★★

Coding. Build a complete regression study on a dataset with a nonlinear truth and a categorical feature.

  1. Generate \(n=500\) points where \(y = 3\sin(x_1) + 0.5x_2^2 + \text{group effect} + \epsilon\), with \(x_1,x_2\) continuous and one 4-level categorical.
  2. Fit four models: raw linear, plus quadratic terms, plus RBF features on \(x_1\), plus interactions. Report train and test MSE for each.
  3. For the best model, produce the residual-vs-fitted plot values and check for the four pathologies.
  4. Introduce the dummy-variable trap deliberately and show what happens to lstsq, solve, and the coefficients.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 500
x1 = rng.uniform(-3, 3, n); x2 = rng.uniform(-2, 2, n)
g  = rng.integers(0, 4, n)
geff = np.array([0., 2.5, -1.5, 4.0])[g]
y  = 3*np.sin(x1) + 0.5*x2**2 + geff + 0.6*rng.normal(size=n)

def dummies(g, K=4): return np.column_stack([(g==k).astype(float) for k in range(1,K)])
def rbf(x, cs, s): return np.column_stack([np.exp(-(x-c)**2/(2*s*s)) for c in cs])
cs = np.linspace(-3, 3, 12); s = 0.55

models = {
 "linear":        lambda: np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2, dummies(g)]),
 "+ quadratic":   lambda: np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2, x1**2, x2**2, dummies(g)]),
 "+ RBF on x1":   lambda: np.column_stack([np.ones(n), rbf(x1,cs,s), x2, x2**2, dummies(g)]),
 "+ interaction": lambda: np.column_stack([np.ones(n), rbf(x1,cs,s), x2, x2**2,
                                           dummies(g), (x1*x2)[:,None]]),
}
perm = rng.permutation(n); tr, te = perm[:375], perm[375:]
best = None
for name, build in models.items():
    X = build()
    w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[tr], y[tr], rcond=None)
    a = np.mean((X[tr]@w - y[tr])**2); b = np.mean((X[te]@w - y[te])**2)
    print(f"{name:15s} cols {X.shape[1]:3d}  train {a:.4f}  test {b:.4f}")
    if best is None or b < best[0]: best = (b, name, X, w)

_, name, X, w = best
r = y[tr] - X[tr]@w; f = X[tr]@w
print(f"\\nbest: {name}")
print(f"  residual mean      {r.mean():.2e}   (should be ~0 with an intercept)")
print(f"  corr(|r|, fitted)  {np.corrcoef(np.abs(r), f)[0,1]:+.4f}   (funnel test)")
lo, hi = f < np.median(f), f >= np.median(f)
print(f"  mean r low/high    {r[lo].mean():+.4f} / {r[hi].mean():+.4f}  (curvature test)")
print(f"  residuals > 3 sd   {(np.abs(r) > 3*r.std()).sum()} of {len(r)}  (outlier test)")

Xg = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2] + [(g==k).astype(float) for k in range(4)])
print(f"\\nall 4 dummies + intercept: shape {Xg.shape}, rank {np.linalg.matrix_rank(Xg)}")
wl, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Xg[tr], y[tr], rcond=None)
print("lstsq  ->", np.round(wl, 3), f"  ||w|| {np.linalg.norm(wl):.3f}")
try:
    ws = np.linalg.solve(Xg[tr].T@Xg[tr], Xg[tr].T@y[tr])
    print("solve  ->", np.round(ws, 3), f"  ||w|| {np.linalg.norm(ws):.3e}")
except np.linalg.LinAlgError as e:
    print("solve  -> LinAlgError:", e)

2. Typical test MSE: linear ≈ 5.0, + quadratic ≈ 4.6, + RBF ≈ 0.40, + interaction ≈ 0.40. The jump happens at the RBF step, because \(\sin(x_1)\) is the dominant nonlinearity and no polynomial of degree 2 can capture it. The interaction term adds nothing — correctly, since the truth is additive. Note that its training MSE is slightly lower (an extra column always helps training error) while test MSE is flat: that is the smallest possible instance of overfitting, and exactly why you compare on test.

3. The best model should show residual mean at machine zero, \(\operatorname{corr}(|r|, \hat y)\) near zero (no funnel), similar residual means in the low and high halves of the fitted range (no curvature), and roughly \(0.3\%\) of points beyond 3 sd, as a Gaussian predicts. Any of those failing points at the corresponding pathology in the figure above.

4. With all four dummies plus an intercept, \(\rank = 5\) but there are 6 columns. lstsq succeeds — it uses the SVD and silently returns the minimum-norm solution among the infinitely many, which is a perfectly reasonable but arbitrary choice. solve either raises LinAlgError or returns coefficients of magnitude \(10^{13}\) or more, built entirely from round-off.

The important observation: the predictions are identical in both cases. Only the coefficients are broken. If you are predicting, you may never notice; if you are interpreting, you will report nonsense. That is the difference between a model that works and a model you understand, and it is worth checking matrix_rank whenever you intend to read coefficients.
